# Emotion

## Feeling and interpretation

A reported feeling supplies evidence of that report. It does not establish every causal or factual interpretation attached to the feeling.

1. Preserve the person's actual report without substituting an inferred emotion.
2. Separate the event described, the meaning assigned to it, the reported feeling, and the contemplated response.
3. Examine the factual claim whose truth would change the response, retaining the feeling report while that claim remains unresolved.
4. Observe any later appraisal, feeling, or action separately rather than calling the revised explanation emotional regulation.

'I'm worried rollback will erase reviewed changes' reports worry and a loss concern. Whether rollback erases those changes requires evidence about rollback. A response can address that concern without asserting that worry has fallen.

The source contains a constructed later report and a revised response. It records no observed emotional improvement.

## Adverse conditions

An unwanted experience can disclose a condition that should change. Reinterpreting the experience cannot be assumed to resolve the condition.

1. Identify the reported loss, threat, demand, or unmet requirement without assuming its factual explanation is complete.
2. Separate changes to the external condition from changes to its interpretation and from support that makes it more bearable.
3. Determine which proposed operation can affect the condition actually at issue.
4. Preserve the person's choice and the observed result; a useful explanation may leave the material problem unresolved.

If the stated concern is losing reviewed changes, preserving those changes and persuading the contributor to feel differently are different targets. The first requires a suitable repair or rollback procedure. The second cannot substitute for missing preservation.

This is a design distinction derived from the supplied loss-concern case and catalog perspectives, not evidence that a particular intervention treats distress.
