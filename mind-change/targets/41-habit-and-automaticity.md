# Habit and automaticity

Candidate contributors describe possible functional relationships. Each source marker supports only its attached statement at the [specified scope](../evidence.md). [Construction](../contribution-construction.md) supplies operations for a particular use.

<a id="target-387"></a>
## 387. Habit acquisition

a response becoming more reliably triggered by a recurring condition.

**Candidate contributors:** Repeated performance in a recurring context; a stable recognizable cue; low initiation friction; repeated consequences that maintain the response.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Daily-life automaticity varied substantially between people and behaviors; repetition is not a fixed-duration guarantee [S16](../evidence.md#s16).

<a id="target-388"></a>
## 388. Habit weakening

a previously recurrent response becoming less automatic.

**Candidate contributors:** Reduced contact with the old cue; interruption of its usual response; altered context; an alternative response becoming available.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Temporary nonperformance need not erase the cue-response tendency; it can return in a familiar context.

<a id="target-389"></a>
## 389. Cue reassignment

changing which condition activates a response.

**Candidate contributors:** Repeated pairing of the intended response with a new cue; distinct separation from the old cue; a cue available at the right time.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Similar cues may continue to trigger the old response unless their relevant difference is learned.

<a id="target-390"></a>
## 390. Routine replacement

one familiar response giving way to another.

**Candidate contributors:** A replacement that serves the old routine's function; an accessible response at the same trigger; altered reward or friction; repeated enactment.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Removing the old routine without supplying its useful function can leave a recurring unmet need.

<a id="target-391"></a>
## 391. Automatic initiation

beginning without a fresh explicit decision.

**Candidate contributors:** A well-learned start cue; preparation of the first action; stable access to materials; minimal need for a fresh choice.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Automatic starting can occur after the original goal has ceased to apply.

<a id="target-392"></a>
## 392. Automatic sequencing

a learned sequence proceeding with less stepwise direction.

**Candidate contributors:** Repeated ordered transitions; reliable feedback between steps; grouping components into a practiced unit.

**Conditions and distinctions:** A fluent sequence can preserve an obsolete or incorrect step.

<a id="target-393"></a>
## 393. De-automation

bringing a familiar routine back under deliberate examination.

**Candidate contributors:** A pause at the relevant step; explicit inspection of the routine's conditions; a novel case that breaks habitual execution.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Awareness of a routine does not immediately provide an alternative method.

<a id="target-394"></a>
## 394. Context discrimination

a routine becoming restricted to appropriate conditions.

**Candidate contributors:** Contrasting contexts requiring and forbidding the routine; distinctive relevance cues; exceptions encountered during use.

**Conditions and distinctions:** A routine learned in one setting may transfer too broadly or fail to activate elsewhere.

<a id="target-395"></a>
## 395. Automatic interruption

a learned stopping condition interrupting a routine.

**Candidate contributors:** A salient stop condition; repeated cue-stop pairing; an available interrupt action; preserved detection during the routine.

**Conditions and distinctions:** The stop signal must remain accessible while execution consumes attention.
