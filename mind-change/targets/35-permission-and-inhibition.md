# Permission and inhibition

Candidate contributors describe possible functional relationships. Each source marker supports only its attached statement at the [specified scope](../evidence.md). [Construction](../contribution-construction.md) supplies operations for a particular use.

<a id="target-329"></a>
## 329. Permission to consider

allowing an idea to enter examination.

**Candidate contributors:** Separating examination from endorsement; a provisional frame; permission to preserve current commitments during comparison; reduced penalty for entertaining an idea.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Considering a possibility does not require accepting its truth or desirability.

<a id="target-330"></a>
## 330. Permission to disagree

becoming able to oppose a claim or authority.

**Candidate contributors:** Recognizing a defensible conflicting judgment; an available expression of it; explicit room for dissent; reduced threat of retaliation.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Verbal permission alone may be ineffective where actual power consequences remain.

<a id="target-331"></a>
## 331. Permission to attempt

becoming willing to try without assured success.

**Candidate contributors:** A bounded attempt; a recoverable failure; an attainable first action; separation of trying from promising success.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Permission cannot supply missing resources or erase genuine consequences.

<a id="target-332"></a>
## 332. Permission to stop

becoming able to end an activity or commitment.

**Candidate contributors:** A legitimate ending criterion; recognition of sunk costs; release from an unnecessary obligation; a preserved route for later return.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Stopping one method need not abandon the concern it was intended to serve.

<a id="target-333"></a>
## 333. Permission to revise

allowing an earlier position to be replaced.

**Candidate contributors:** A specific reason for revision; separation of accuracy from personal worth; preserving valid prior work; social acceptance of warranted correction.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Allowing revision is distinct from establishing which replacement is better.

<a id="target-334"></a>
## 334. Expression inhibition

becoming more or less able to speak or show a response.

**Candidate contributors:** An available phrase or expressive movement; changes in anticipated evaluation; a chosen display rule; a private or supportive setting.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Silence may reflect deliberate judgment rather than inability or fear.

<a id="target-335"></a>
## 335. Impulse inhibition

changing the ability to withhold an immediately available action.

**Candidate contributors:** An early stop cue; time before execution; removal of a triggering opportunity; an alternative response linked to the same cue.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Withholding one action is easier to specify than suppressing every associated thought or feeling.

<a id="target-336"></a>
## 336. Internal prohibition

acquiring, relaxing, or rejecting a self-imposed restriction.

**Candidate contributors:** A rule's stated basis; an exception revealing overbreadth; changes in values or circumstances; explicit permission conditioned on consequences.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Rejecting a restriction without identifying its original function can remove needed protection or coordination.

<a id="target-337"></a>
## 337. Social inhibition

changing restraint associated with anticipated evaluation.

**Candidate contributors:** Actual audience response; anonymity or privacy; familiarity with the setting; clear participation norms; reduced evaluative ambiguity.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Anticipated judgment may be inaccurate, but real social risk cannot be wished away.

<a id="target-338"></a>
## 338. Deliberate restraint

choosing to withhold an action while preserving the ability to perform it.

**Candidate contributors:** A reason to defer; a selected later condition for action; control over timing; an alternative serving the same concern.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Restraint preserves the available action; inability to act is a different state.
