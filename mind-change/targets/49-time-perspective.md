# Time perspective

Candidate contributors describe possible functional relationships. Each source marker supports only its attached statement at the [specified scope](../evidence.md). [Construction](../contribution-construction.md) supplies operations for a particular use.

<a id="target-460"></a>
## 460. Present orientation

changing how much current experience dominates.

**Candidate contributors:** Attention to current sensation and action; immediate demands or rewards; reduced future cues; deliberate selection of a present activity.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Present attention can support contact with experience or conceal a consequential future dependency.

<a id="target-461"></a>
## 461. Future horizon

considering a longer or shorter interval ahead.

**Candidate contributors:** Representing delayed consequences; a longer planning interval; credible information about future conditions; including later beneficiaries.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Longer horizons can add uncertainty and speculation; include distinctions that change present choices.

<a id="target-462"></a>
## 462. Past orientation

changing how strongly earlier events organize present attention.

**Candidate contributors:** Recurring reminders; autobiographical interpretation; unresolved past concerns; deliberate retrieval or backgrounding of earlier events.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Past attention can supply needed learning or displace current information; relevance decides the difference.

<a id="target-463"></a>
## 463. Future-self connection

changing the felt or understood relevance of later consequences to oneself.

**Candidate contributors:** Concrete representation of one's later situation; recognition of continuing interests; contact with foreseeable consequences; future-oriented commitments.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Age-progressed self-images affected allocation choices in experiments; enduring behavior change is a broader claim [S23](../evidence.md#s23).

<a id="target-464"></a>
## 464. Subjective duration

time seeming to pass more quickly or slowly.

**Candidate contributors:** Attention to elapsed time; density of noticed events; arousal; boredom or absorption; how an interval is remembered.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Duration judged during an interval can differ from duration reconstructed afterward.

<a id="target-465"></a>
## 465. Patience

changing willingness to wait.

**Candidate contributors:** Credible expected benefit of waiting; tolerable present conditions; an activity during the delay; trust in eventual delivery.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Waiting longer is not beneficial when delay destroys value or the promised result is unreliable.

<a id="target-466"></a>
## 466. Urgency calibration

changing when haste is experienced as warranted.

**Candidate contributors:** Actual deadline and delay consequences; expiring opportunities; comparison with recoverable delays; removal of artificial pressure.

**Conditions and distinctions:** A strong feeling of haste does not establish a high cost of waiting.

<a id="target-467"></a>
## 467. Temporal possibility

seeing that a different sequence or timing changes what can happen.

**Candidate contributors:** Reordering prerequisites; staging resources earlier; exploiting a time window; separating simultaneous demands; deferring a reversible commitment.

**Conditions and distinctions:** A new sequence changes possibility only through real timing dependencies.
