# Wakefulness and awareness

Candidate contributors describe possible functional relationships. Each source marker supports only its attached statement at the [specified scope](../evidence.md). [Construction](../contribution-construction.md) supplies operations for a particular use.

<a id="target-1"></a>
## 1. Wakefulness

waking, becoming drowsy, or falling asleep.

**Candidate contributors:** Accumulating or dissipating sleep pressure; circadian wake signals; activating sensory input; changes in brain function or substance exposure.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Sleep restriction can impair performance even when reported sleepiness changes little further; felt alertness and measured vigilance can diverge [S01](../evidence.md#s01).

<a id="target-2"></a>
## 2. Conscious access

an otherwise unreported content entering awareness.

**Candidate contributors:** Making content stronger or longer lasting; removing masking or competing content; directing attention to the relevant feature; connecting input to an available interpretation.

**Conditions and distinctions:** An accurate response without a report of awareness does not settle conscious access; reporting ability is a separate dependency.

<a id="target-3"></a>
## 3. Awareness breadth

more or less of an experience being noticed.

**Candidate contributors:** Scanning additional sensory or internal channels; loosening a narrow task focus; reducing a dominant competing demand; supplying prompts for previously omitted aspects.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Noticing more aspects can reduce detail per aspect. Attention breadth and experienced awareness are related but distinct targets.

<a id="target-4"></a>
## 4. Clarity

experience becoming clearer, hazier, or more confused.

**Candidate contributors:** Resolving conflicting interpretations; restoring missing context; improving signal quality; reducing simultaneous demands; changes in physiological alertness.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Identify whether the haze concerns sensory input, meaning, memory, or wakefulness; the same intervention need not affect each.

<a id="target-5"></a>
## 5. Absorption

becoming immersed in, or disengaging from, an activity.

**Candidate contributors:** A continuously available task; successive challenges within current capability; compelling sensory or narrative continuity; reduced interruptions; stronger competing events that break immersion.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Immersion can support an activity or conceal relevant surroundings. Its value depends on what requires access.

<a id="target-6"></a>
## 6. Dream awareness

recognizing a dream while dreaming.

**Candidate contributors:** A dream containing a noticed inconsistency; recall of an intention to recognize dreaming; emergence of reflective awareness during sleep.

**Conditions and distinctions:** These are candidate relationships, not a reliable induction method; remembering a dream later does not establish awareness during it.

<a id="target-7"></a>
## 7. Self-awareness

noticing oneself as participating in an experience.

**Candidate contributors:** A prompt to notice one's current action; reflection or self-reference; attention to one's bodily participation; feedback showing one's contribution to an event.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Self-monitoring may become distraction or evaluative self-consciousness; noticing oneself need not improve performance.

<a id="target-8"></a>
## 8. Environmental awareness

becoming more or less aware of surroundings.

**Candidate contributors:** Unobstructed sensory access; scanning the surroundings; cues identifying consequential changes; releasing attention from an absorbing internal or external task.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Information must actually reach the person; a warning cannot reveal an inaccessible environment by itself.
