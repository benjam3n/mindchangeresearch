# Bodily experience

Candidate contributors describe possible functional relationships. Each source marker supports only its attached statement at the [specified scope](../evidence.md). [Construction](../contribution-construction.md) supplies operations for a particular use.

<a id="target-9"></a>
## 9. Fatigue

feeling more or less mentally or physically tired.

**Candidate contributors:** Accumulated exertion and sleep loss; relief from sustained demands; illness-related bodily signals; changes in perceived remaining effort and payoff.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Felt fatigue, physical exhaustion, and declining accuracy require separate interpretation; their trajectories may differ [S01](../evidence.md#s01).

<a id="target-10"></a>
## 10. Vitality

feeling more or less energetic or vigorous.

**Candidate contributors:** Recovery from depletion; activating movement or sensory experience; anticipated achievable reward; relief from sustained stress or obstructed effort.

**Conditions and distinctions:** An energizing interpretation cannot be assumed to correct a physiological limitation; vitality is a felt outcome.

<a id="target-11"></a>
## 11. Comfort

changes in felt ease, discomfort, or restlessness.

**Candidate contributors:** Changing pressure, posture, temperature, noise, or bodily irritation; meeting an unmet bodily need; increasing predictability and control over the setting.

**Conditions and distinctions:** The contributor depends on the source of discomfort; social reassurance cannot remove an uncomfortable physical stimulus.

<a id="target-12"></a>
## 12. Pain

changes in experienced intensity, location, unpleasantness, or meaning.

**Candidate contributors:** Changes in nociceptive input or central processing; attention to pain; expected relief or threat; the meaning assigned to the sensation.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Intensity, unpleasantness, and interpretation can change separately. Expectation effects in experimental pain do not establish removal of injury [S02](../evidence.md#s02).

<a id="target-13"></a>
## 13. Tension

changes in felt tightness, relaxation, or readiness.

**Candidate contributors:** Changes in muscle contraction and posture; preparing for or ending an anticipated action; perceived threat; deliberate release of a held contraction.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Felt tightness may persist without voluntary contraction; a bodily description alone does not identify its cause.

<a id="target-14"></a>
## 14. Hunger and satiety

changes in felt need for or satisfaction from food.

**Candidate contributors:** Signals of energy need; oral and gastrointestinal input from eating; learned meal cues; anticipated food; sensory-specific loss of appeal during consumption.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Wanting a particular food and general hunger can diverge; the relevant contributor depends on which is changing.

<a id="target-15"></a>
## 15. Thirst

changes in felt need for fluid.

**Candidate contributors:** Body-fluid regulation; dry-mouth sensations; oral signals from drinking; heat and fluid loss; learned access cues.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Dry mouth and physiological fluid need are not interchangeable; this is a contributor map, not intake guidance.

<a id="target-16"></a>
## 16. Sexual arousal

changes in felt sexual responsiveness or interest.

**Candidate contributors:** Personally meaningful erotic cues or imagination; wanted touch and interaction; bodily and hormonal state; relationship context; stress or distraction.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Subjective desire, physiological response, willingness, and consent are separate; none can be inferred solely from another.

<a id="target-17"></a>
## 17. Bodily orientation

changes in felt balance, movement, position, or ownership.

**Candidate contributors:** Vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive input; actual movement; agreement or conflict between seen and felt position; expected sensory consequences of action.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Balance, location, movement, and ownership can dissociate; specify the component before selecting a contributor.

<a id="target-18"></a>
## 18. Interoceptive awareness

changes in noticing and distinguishing internal bodily signals.

**Candidate contributors:** Directing attention to internal signals; distinguishing similar sensations; labeling location and quality; comparing sensations with independently available physiological information.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Greater confidence in bodily interpretation need not mean greater accuracy; indiscriminate monitoring may amplify ambiguity.
