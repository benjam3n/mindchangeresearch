# Attention

Candidate contributors describe possible functional relationships. Each source marker supports only its attached statement at the [specified scope](../evidence.md). [Construction](../contribution-construction.md) supplies operations for a particular use.

<a id="target-51"></a>
## 51. Orientation

directing attention toward something previously unattended.

**Candidate contributors:** A spatial or verbal cue; sudden change; a question naming an overlooked feature; an object connected to the active goal.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Orienting toward something consumes attention elsewhere; the selected direction must serve the current activity.

<a id="target-52"></a>
## 52. Selection

changing which competing content receives attention.

**Candidate contributors:** A current selection rule; relative salience; reward history; removing distractors; placing needed content at the decision point.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Selecting more relevant content can improve use of limited memory; selection efficiency is not identical to memory capacity [S05](../evidence.md#s05).

<a id="target-53"></a>
## 53. Maintenance

sustaining attention for a longer or shorter interval.

**Candidate contributors:** A continuously meaningful task signal; manageable substeps; reminders of the active object; reduced interruptions; planned points for reorientation.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Persistence without sensitivity to changed conditions can become fixation.

<a id="target-54"></a>
## 54. Disengagement

releasing attention from a currently dominant object.

**Candidate contributors:** A clear completion or suspension condition; recording the unfinished matter; a stronger relevant cue; reducing repeated re-exposure.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Disengagement can leave unresolved work unavailable later unless a return condition is preserved.

<a id="target-55"></a>
## 55. Switching

moving attention between objects or activities.

**Candidate contributors:** An explicit switch cue; visible state of the incoming task; preservation of the outgoing task's state; removal of obsolete instructions.

**Conditions and distinctions:** A fast switch can still carry the previous rule into the new task.

<a id="target-56"></a>
## 56. Division

distributing attention across multiple demands.

**Candidate contributors:** Separating demands by timing or modality; automating a practiced component; external monitoring of one stream; explicit priority when demands conflict.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Several demanding operations may still compete for the same capacity; division is not unlimited parallel processing.

<a id="target-57"></a>
## 57. Breadth

narrowing focus or taking in a wider field.

**Candidate contributors:** A prompt to inspect context or detail; changing display scale; reducing a dominant threat cue; making peripheral information relevant.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Widening attention can sacrifice precision; narrowing can hide a consequential dependency.

<a id="target-58"></a>
## 58. Interruption resistance

becoming more or less distractible.

**Candidate contributors:** Removing avoidable alerts; distinguishing interruption criteria; making the active goal continuously available; arranging a protected work interval.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Necessary warnings still need an access route; complete isolation can obstruct required information.

<a id="target-59"></a>
## 59. Timing

attending at the moment a relevant difference occurs.

**Candidate contributors:** A cue synchronized with the event; predictable intervals; a reminder at the point of use; maintaining the relevant detection rule.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Information delivered too early may be forgotten and too late may be unusable.

<a id="target-60"></a>
## 60. Control

changing the relation between chosen and involuntary attention.

**Candidate contributors:** A deliberately chosen focus; recognition of capture; an available return action; environmental control over recurring distractors.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Voluntary instructions compete with learned and stimulus-driven capture; wanting control does not establish it.
