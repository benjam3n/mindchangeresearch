# Sensory discrimination

Candidate contributors describe possible functional relationships. Each source marker supports only its attached statement at the [specified scope](../evidence.md). [Construction](../contribution-construction.md) supplies operations for a particular use.

<a id="target-27"></a>
## 27. Detection

becoming able or unable to notice a stimulus.

**Candidate contributors:** Greater signal intensity or duration; reduced masking; informative timing or location cues; a response criterion more willing to report a signal.

**Conditions and distinctions:** More detections may come from more false alarms rather than improved sensitivity.

<a id="target-28"></a>
## 28. Sensitivity

weaker or stronger stimulation becoming noticeable.

**Candidate contributors:** Changes in sensory adaptation; lower background noise; attention to the relevant channel; physiological sensory function.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Distinguish a changed threshold from changed willingness to report and from an altered physical stimulus.

<a id="target-29"></a>
## 29. Resolution

distinguishing finer or coarser sensory differences.

**Candidate contributors:** Access to fine features; side-by-side contrasts; repeated discrimination of near neighbors; informative correction about the confusable feature.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Improvement may remain specific to the practiced stimulus, position, or task [S03](../evidence.md#s03).

<a id="target-30"></a>
## 30. Auditory discrimination

distinguishing sounds, voices, tones, or rhythms.

**Candidate contributors:** Contrasting confusable sounds; isolating pitch, timing, or timbre differences; reduced acoustic masking; pairing a sound with its correct source or label.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Volume alone cannot teach a phonetic distinction; auditory access and learned discrimination impose different limits.

<a id="target-31"></a>
## 31. Visual discrimination

distinguishing shapes, colors, movements, or configurations.

**Candidate contributors:** Comparing confusable shapes or configurations; highlighting diagnostic visual features; repeated discrimination; improved contrast or visibility.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Texture-learning results support local learning, not a general improvement in all vision [S03](../evidence.md#s03).

<a id="target-32"></a>
## 32. Tactile discrimination

distinguishing textures, pressures, or locations.

**Candidate contributors:** Controlled differences in texture or pressure; exploring an object through movement; contrast between nearby locations; feedback identifying the relevant sensation.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Skin location, stimulus force, and sensory function constrain which distinctions become available.

<a id="target-33"></a>
## 33. Smell and taste discrimination

distinguishing previously conflated sensations.

**Candidate contributors:** Comparing samples while tracking a specific sensory dimension; identifying component qualities; reduced competing odors or tastes; changes in adaptation.

**Conditions and distinctions:** A richer label vocabulary can change reports without establishing finer sensory sensitivity.

<a id="target-34"></a>
## 34. Signal discrimination

separating a relevant signal from background variation.

**Candidate contributors:** Reducing irrelevant variation; revealing a diagnostic feature; comparing signal-present and signal-absent cases; calibrating the reporting criterion.

**Conditions and distinctions:** Signal quality and decision bias need separate checks; added salience can amplify noise as well as signal.
