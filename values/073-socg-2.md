# A well-meant coalition can change who may disagree

Application: 073-socg-2. Actor: this assistant's current working model and generated artifacts unless a constructed case is explicitly identified. Human outcomes are not observed.

Starting working judgment: Privately aligning supportive participants seems a practical way to make a meeting productive, but it changes the information and power conditions for people outside those conversations.

Source: ../sources/values-socg.md. Requirements receipt: ../sources/values-socg.receipt.txt. Original loaded through read_original.py; byte checks are recorded separately in source-integrity.json.

Depth: The original specifies no numerical 8x scaling. All presently executable stages are addressed; unavailable live stages are recorded separately. Expanded scope is stated rather than inventing an 8x certification. Four perspectives, eight leverage points, a second-order reaction chain, two alternative moves, and a later invitation.

Constructed community case: A proposes a reading group; B has already privately agreed; C facilitates; D is newly invited and does not know about the prior agreement. A wants lively sessions, B wants a predictable schedule, C wants a workable group, D wants freedom to explore without mandatory preparation. The room is online and text-only; tone, face, and bodily signals are unavailable.

Stated purpose: “choose a format together.” Unstated candidates: A views the meeting as confirmation; B views it as scheduling; C views it as open selection; D views it as a genuine invitation to shape the activity. Only the private A/B agreement is a stipulated fact; other interpretations remain case hypotheses.

Four perspective models: A values momentum and fears endless discussion; B values reliability and fears schedule churn; C values participation and fears unclear decisions; D values inquiry and fears inherited obligations. A can propose, C can structure the meeting, everyone can decline membership. D’s lower history/access gives a different starting point without implying lower competence or less interest.

Action prediction: if A begins “we have agreed on weekly chapters,” B reinforces it, C treats format as settled, and D’s objection becomes a request to undo a group agreement D never joined. Second-order effect: D’s dissent can then be interpreted as lack of commitment rather than an input to the promised format choice. Alternative action: disclose “A and B favor weekly chapters; the group format is still open,” preserving the actual agreement’s scope.

Eight leverage points: disclose prior agreement; distinguish existing members from invitees; separate schedule from content; make preparation optional if genuinely optional; let D describe a desired session; state what cannot change; record open choices before voting; permit joining later. Highest-impact lever: label which decisions are already committed and which are actually open.

Generated move: “Two of us prefer weekly chapters. We have not decided for the group. Let’s compare a chapter meeting, a question-led meeting, and alternating sessions; joining is optional.” Risk: falsely reopening an externally fixed schedule; mitigation: name that constraint separately. Fallback: offer parallel or alternating formats without claiming full consensus.

Later application: a workshop invitation now distinguishes “date fixed” from “agenda open.” This is an executed wording change. It reuses the participation/scope distinction kept in socg-1, so it earns no new KEEP. Real participation and trust effects remain unknown.

Actual mind change: The social model now includes how a preparatory coalition changes the meaning of later dissent; a narrower invitation is generated.

Benefit: The invitation’s open choices are explicit; no observed human cooperation improvement is established.

Verdict: UNRESOLVED

Organization: Content separates useful preparation from undisclosed precommitment. Organization records decision status before predicting social reaction.
