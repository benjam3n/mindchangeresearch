The present account must be able to lose its authority without every true proposition in it first becoming false. That distinction is the starting claim investigated here, and it remains among the claims that can lose authority.

These 25 inquiries expose different places where such authority operates: what becomes noticeable; what counts as one thing; which questions can occur; what a person wants; how an alternative becomes intelligible; what counts as a reason; which changes are available; and what would justify concluding that the inquiry is finished. A change at one place does not establish a change at all the others.

The numbered accounts below are substantive alternatives developed for each inquiry. They are not an exhaustive enumeration of all possible accounts. Some are compatible mechanisms, some are rival explanations, some are possible meanings of the stipulated proposition, and some challenge the form of the inquiry itself. Combining them all would therefore be a logical error. Each inquiry distinguishes their roles and gives a disposition with the scope of its justification.

“Let us say” is treated as an assumption with consequences, not as evidence that its proposition is true. In particular, the possibilities of perfected perspective and already-sufficient model capacity are developed without quietly replacing them with perpetual openness or present benchmark performance. Absolute perfection, the ability to reach it, and the ability to certify that it has been reached are distinct claims.

There are two separate completeness questions. Has this document followed every one of the 25 inquiries and assessed its listed options? Has it established that no unlisted alternative, new distinction, changed language, different embodiment, or newly available mode of understanding matters? The first can be inspected in the document. The second requires an argument this investigation does not possess. No numerical option count settles it.

The concluding adjudication carries out combinations and eliminations warranted by the arguments, works through concrete changes to the judge, and identifies the exact unmet obligations for declaring perfection. Failing to establish those obligations is an admission of failure to establish perfected understanding here, not a proof that perfected understanding is impossible.
