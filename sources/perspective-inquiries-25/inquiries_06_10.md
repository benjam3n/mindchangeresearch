6. Begin from “everything is resource allocation,” then let perspective transform that starting point.

The identity claim is distinct from saying that everything consumes something or that everything deliberately maximizes a quantity. No implication ordering among these claims has been established, and neither is a licensed substitute for the identity. Taken literally, the identity requires an account of allocation broad enough to include how resources, recipients, uses, and alternatives become distinguishable at all. Otherwise it already exempts the activity that supplies its own terms.

Fourteen materially different possibilities follow. They are rival proposals about what the identity would mean, not an exhaustive division of reality.

1. **Distribution among established uses.** Resources and possible uses already exist; perspective determines which distribution appears appropriate. This preserves ordinary allocation, but cannot yet explain the creation of its categories.
2. **Production of capacities.** Allocation changes what can later be allocated and done. Learning a language changes the available activities, not just the quantities devoted to existing activities. Perspective becomes part of the production of future powers.
3. **Constitution through use.** Something becomes a resource because a relation makes it usable. A locked room, a key, and knowledge of the lock are not independent additions to a common stock; their conjunction changes what each can do.
4. **Differentiation before selection.** What appears to be allocation begins when differences become actionable. Two sounds receive different treatment only after something distinguishes them. Perspective partly establishes the recipients between which allocation can occur.
5. **Arrangement without transfer.** Identical components and quantities can support different activities when their relations change. Allocation must therefore include placement, coupling, and sequence, rather than only amounts assigned.
6. **Exclusion through commitment.** An allocation is characterized by what it makes unavailable elsewhere. This requires specifying which counterfactual alternatives genuinely coexist; imagined alternatives cannot automatically establish an opportunity cost.
7. **Formation of the feasible.** Some allocations create new alternatives or destroy the conditions for previous ones. The set of possible uses is an effect within the account, not a fixed menu outside it.
8. **Reproduction through time.** What exists now depends on earlier allocations, while present activity preserves or changes those dependencies. A perspective is partly an inherited pattern of what continues to receive support without renewed choice.
9. **Maintenance of an organized living activity.** Distinctions matter through their consequences for continued life and action. Perspective includes bodily readiness and environmental access. This explains a class of allocations; it does not by itself establish the universal identity.
10. **Jurisdiction over use.** Ownership, permission, and obligation determine what counts as available to whom. A room can be physically accessible and unavailable under an accepted commitment. Perspective therefore includes practical authority, not just representation.
11. **Formation of participants.** People and groups are not always fixed recipients. An allocation can create an institution, dependency, or collective agent. The identities of allocator and recipient can be consequences of the relations being investigated.
12. **Assignment of significance.** What receives urgency, credibility, or sacrifice changes subsequent activity. “Resource” must then distinguish physical quantities from practical standing; calling both allocations does not make them interchangeable.
13. **Unchosen differentiation.** If everything is allocation, unintended events must qualify too. Allocation cannot intrinsically require a decision maker. An account must explain what distinguishes allocation from mere occurrence without borrowing intention when convenient.
14. **A discipline of description.** “Everything is resource allocation” may require every account to expose dependencies, exclusions, and enabling conditions. That can be useful without proving that allocation is the fundamental substance or structure of everything.

The first possibility gives a familiar relationship: there is an independently specified world of resources and feasible uses; a perspective represents it; changing perspective helps select a better distribution. Its strongest attraction is that error has an external object. Miscounting the available hours remains an error even if a different description feels liberating. Its limitation appears when the question concerns the formation of “an hour available for work.” An uninterrupted hour and sixty scattered minutes can have equal duration without supporting the same activity. Once this matters, allocation over a quantity of time is underdescribed. The inquiry must include interruption, dependency, and readiness in what it takes itself to be allocating.

That implication does not establish that the world is created by a description. The stronger alternative, combining possibilities three through seven, is that description, arrangement, and action can change the practical identities of what exists. The same room becomes shelter, a workplace, a meeting place, or unusable space through different relations. Some transitions require construction; others require recognition or permission. A representational account of perspective could explain how acquiring a distinction causally completes a usable relation. If it claims to explain these transformations, however, it owes that account rather than assuming that representing an already divided world explains how usable relations form. Likewise, an allocation account that treats resources as independently given atoms owes an explanation of how usability comes into existence if it claims to include such formation. Neither account is refuted merely by pointing to a changed relation.

The strongest incompatible rival says that every such change is simply a movement within one enormous, permanently fixed space of physical possibilities. On that account, new possibilities are merely possibilities that were previously unknown. This could be true at one level and insufficient at another. A complete inventory of physically possible events would not automatically identify which events constitute a promise, a usable method, or a resource for a particular undertaking. The reduction requires a derivation of those relations, not the assertion that physics must contain them. Conversely, their practical emergence does not prove that physical possibilities literally expanded. These claims must remain separate.

Possibilities ten through twelve introduce another transformation. If allocation determines who has standing to decide, the procedure evaluating an allocation is partly one of its consequences. Suppose a group allocates every speaking opportunity to members already regarded as competent. Their later apparent competence can reflect the opportunities that the rule distributed. A perspective formed within that arrangement can mistake an effect of allocation for independent justification of it. Resource allocation must then include the production of its own evidence and judges. Perspective must include the social conditions under which an account can become credible or even audible.

This does not justify erasing differences in competence. It defeats the inference from “this arrangement produced the currently recognized competence” to “the arrangement independently deserves its authority.” Establishing the latter requires additional grounds.

There are precise combinations available. Distribution among fixed uses is retained as a special case when identities and feasibility can reasonably be held constant. Capacity production and relational constitution combine when a changed relation creates a later ability. Jurisdiction and physical feasibility combine only if they remain distinguishable: a prohibition and a material impossibility can constrain the same act without being the same constraint. Calling all three “allocation” fails to explain their differences if it erases the distinctions needed for that explanation. This establishes a limit on explanatory adequacy; the truth of the universal identity remains unsettled.

What is established conditionally is that taking the identity seriously removes the exemption of resource formation, alternative formation, participant formation, and evaluative authority from allocation. The unresolved obstacle is an account precise enough to include these formations while excluding something a rival account would mistakenly include. If “allocation” imposes no discriminating commitments, the identity explains nothing; if it begins with fixed resources and uses, it omits the formation it was supposed to explain. A perfected account has not yet been obtained between those two failures.

Disposition: 1 retained for fixed conditions; its unrestricted adequacy eliminated. 2–5 and 7–8 are compatible contributions. 6 requires genuinely feasible alternatives. 9 remains restricted to living activity. 10–12 are compatible if their differences survive. 13 is required by literal universality but remains underdefined. 14 is a live rival to the metaphysical reading. Exhaustion is unproved.

7. Reconsider the matter without the five governing nouns.

I will start with events and distinctions, rather than replace the forbidden terms with five new labels. Consider a meeting in which everyone receives five minutes. Someone concludes that everyone had an equal chance to contribute. Another participant cannot understand the technical language; another cannot speak without risking retaliation; another has evidence that becomes intelligible only after the meeting ends. Equal duration is true. The conclusion about equal participation does not follow. What must be examined is how duration acquired the authority to stand for participation.

Fourteen different accounts can begin here.

1. **Something happened, but nobody registered it.** A sound occurred; no change in subsequent conduct distinguished it from silence. The relevant difference is between occurrence and registration.
2. **Someone registered it without being able to identify it.** A listener noticed an unfamiliar expression but could not distinguish a warning from a joke. Registration does not supply intelligibility.
3. **Someone identified it without recognizing its bearing.** A participant understood every sentence but missed that a proposed rule would exclude them. Comprehension of sentences does not settle what follows for a life.
4. **Someone recognized its bearing without feeling its urgency.** A distant consequence can be understood and remain practically inert. Being able to state its importance and being moved by it are different achievements.
5. **Someone felt its urgency without being able to act.** Fear, exhaustion, lack of permission, or material dependence can prevent a response. Neither persuasion nor further explanation necessarily changes the relevant condition.
6. **Someone could act, but the act would mean something else to others.** Silence intended as refusal may be recorded as agreement. An act and its public uptake can diverge.
7. **The invitation changed the invitee.** Asking someone to speak as a representative can create responsibilities and loyalties that were absent before the invitation. A participant need not remain identical through participation.
8. **The first commitment altered what later commitments could mean.** A promise, followed by a refusal, differs from a refusal followed by a promise. The order does more than rearrange independently meaningful pieces.
9. **A comparison became possible only after a new distinction appeared.** “Everyone had five minutes” ceased to settle fairness when exposure to retaliation became expressible. A newly available distinction can change what earlier evidence supports.
10. **The available names suppressed a difference.** Calling every interruption “discussion” erased the difference between requesting clarification and preventing someone from finishing. A shared name can hide unequal acts.
11. **An absent participant changed what those present owed.** A decision concerning a child, an absent colleague, or a future inhabitant can carry consequences for someone who cannot enter the exchange. Presence and standing are different.
12. **A description made its own evidence.** Calling one participant unreliable caused others to discount their contributions, producing a record in which they apparently contributed little. The record cannot independently settle the description that helped create it.
13. **Two descriptions remained true while their connection failed.** Everyone spoke; some people were not heard. The error can lie in treating the first truth as sufficient for denying the second.
14. **No repair of the description could perform the missing act.** Correctly explaining that a door is locked does not open it. Understanding and changing what happens must remain distinguishable even where each enables the other.

The first strong continuation treats the meeting as a matter of transmission. A speaker has something to convey; the listeners need to receive and understand it. Its strongest form includes language differences, prior knowledge, interruption, and feedback. It is not defeated merely by showing that communication is complicated. It becomes inadequate when the intended content itself depends on what the exchange makes available. A participant may discover what they have to say only when another person names a distinction. There was no fully formed message waiting unchanged at the entrance.

The second continuation therefore treats the exchange as the formation of meanings and commitments. The important unit is an interaction through which something becomes sayable, binding, or contestable. On an account that treats a valid promise as undertaking an obligation, making it changes what the speaker owes; conveying an intention alone does not establish that undertaking. A rival can recognize changed expectations while disputing whether any independent obligation was created. The example therefore exposes competing accounts of what making a promise does; it does not settle their dispute. This continuation also has a limit. A commitment may be clearly formed while the person making it lacks food, access, or the ability to perform it. The enacted meaning does not create every enabling condition.

The third continuation begins with those enabling conditions. Who can remain awake, enter the room, tolerate the noise, understand the language, and speak without retaliation? Here changing a meeting can require changing bodies, schedules, ownership, or dependence. The claim is stronger than “context matters.” The described activity cannot occur without conditions that earlier descriptions treated as peripheral. Nevertheless, reducing the entire exchange to enabling conditions would omit differences between a request, a threat, and a promise made under identical lighting and nutrition. Material support is necessary for these acts without being a complete account of their identities.

These continuations can be combined where the dependency is specified. A newly expressible distinction changes a commitment; the commitment changes permission; permission makes an act possible; the act changes what others can observe. They cannot be combined by assuming that every difference is one more example of the same unnamed thing. A difference between understanding a warning and possessing an exit remains essential.

The strongest rival to this reconstruction is that all these distinctions were available in the original vocabulary and nothing substantial was gained by withholding it. That objection can be right. The value of withholding a word is not magical access to what language concealed. It is the removal of a shortcut that previously allowed an inference to pass without specifying its terms. If equal time never counted as equal participation, the example reveals no such gain. If it did, the exact improvement is the defeat of that substitution.

The withheld nouns may return only after their work can be stated without them. Does a word distinguish registration from understanding, join them under an explicit relation, or silently collapse them? Does it identify what must change, or merely announce that change is desirable? This requirement also remains revisable; vocabulary sometimes permits discoveries that cannot initially be paraphrased without loss. Provisional use can therefore precede complete clarification, while the word receives no exemption from subsequent examination.

What has become available is a set of noninterchangeable transitions: occurrence, registration, intelligibility, bearing, urgency, ability, act, uptake, and obligation. They need not form one fixed sequence; an obligation can precede understanding, and an act can reveal its own significance afterward. The remaining obstacle is to explain which transitions are required in a particular case and why, without assuming that this list already contains every way something can begin to matter.

Disposition: 1–6 survive as distinguishable occurrences. 7–12 survive as possible changes to participants, meanings, and available evidence. 13 defeats the inference from separate truths to adequate connection. 14 defeats the sufficiency of description for action. These accounts combine only where their relations are specified. Their collective completeness remains unproved.

8. Let relationships change what the subjects are.

The mistaken division need not be an inaccurate map of otherwise settled territories. It may be the presumption that there must first be independently identifiable territories for relationships to connect. That presumption requires examination without the opposite presumption that everything must dissolve into undifferentiated connection.

Fifteen accounts compete over how units of inquiry acquire their identities.

1. **Intrinsic kinds.** A subject concerns things sharing properties independently of our interests. Relationships supply information about those things but do not constitute their identities.
2. **Common causal origin.** Things belong together because a shared process produces them. Superficially similar effects can belong to different inquiries when their origins differ.
3. **Intervention dependence.** A subject is what changes together under an explicitly specified intervention. The relevant boundary can differ for changing a cause and predicting an observation.
4. **Explanatory sufficiency.** A unit is the smallest inclusion of relations sufficient for a particular explanation. Changing what needs explaining can change the unit without anyone making an error.
5. **Persistence through transformation.** A subject is identified by what remains trackable through specified changes. This makes its identity depend on which transformations matter, rather than only its present properties.
6. **Joint constitution.** Creditor and debtor, teacher and pupil, or sender and recipient acquire those identities through relations. One cannot define all such participants completely before describing the relation.
7. **A history with consequences.** The present properties of two things may match while their histories support different expectations or obligations. Inquiry follows a lineage of dependence rather than a static class.
8. **Coordination for action.** A subject bundles what must be considered together to accomplish something. A discipline organized for teaching can therefore differ from one organized for building or repairing.
9. **Institutional inheritance.** Departmental boundaries preserve training, funding, and professional jurisdiction. Their durability does not by itself show that they reflect the deepest divisions in what is studied.
10. **Shared means of access.** Things are grouped because the same instrument or method reveals them. A new means of access can expose dependencies the earlier grouping systematically missed.
11. **Question-dependent individuation.** Asking what a thing is for, what produced it, or who can change it can pick out different boundaries. “The same subject” may conceal several incompatible individuations.
12. **Emergence across scale.** A pattern can have stable properties that its isolated participants lack. The relevant subject becomes an organized activity, while the participants remain relevant to other questions.
13. **Relations without privileged endpoints.** Begin with transformations, dependencies, or exchanges; identify stable participants through repeated relations. Objects are recovered from how they enter the activity rather than supplied beforehand.
14. **Standing and exclusion.** A boundary determines whose experience and consequences count. A subject described as “workplace productivity” can omit unpaid recovery or domestic labor by how it is initially delimited.
15. **Several legitimate partitions.** There may be no uniquely correct division independent of purpose. The ambition becomes exact knowledge of when each division is warranted and what translation between divisions preserves or loses.

Consider an inquiry beginning with “attention.” One branch treats attention as a property of an individual. Another examines tasks, interruptions, rewards, sleep, language, and social permission. Simply placing links from an attention node to six other nodes preserves the original decision about what the central object is. A stronger revision asks whether the activity to be explained is an achievement of person, task, environment, and timing together. If so, the unit changes. Some failures previously located inside the person now belong to a relation that no isolated person possesses.

This is not a license to deny individual differences. The intrinsic account has a strong alternative: environmental relations are inputs to a genuine individual capacity, and causal analysis can separate them. To choose between these accounts, specify the claim whose unit is disputed. If “attention” means a person's ability across a stated range of environments, the individual capacity may remain an appropriate object. If it means the sustained coordination actually occurring in one activity, the environment cannot be dismissed as an external input merely by definition. These accounts may concern different objects under one inherited word. Combining them requires distinguishing those objects, not averaging their descriptions.

The joint-constitution account goes further. “Teacher” and “pupil” are intelligible through activities of teaching and learning, even though the people exist independently. Thus the relationship can constitute a subject's relevant identity without creating its entire material existence. This defeats the false choice between self-contained objects and a world with no objects. It also shows why a catalog organized around already named participants can repeatedly miss the transformation it seeks: the next discovery may change what a participant is counted as doing.

The account beginning with relations has its own concealed commitment. A relation must be differentiated from other relations. If every relation is first defined by endpoints drawn from the inherited catalog, nothing has changed. But if relations are primitive without criteria of identity, the vocabulary has merely moved the unexplained boundary. A usable departure therefore needs cases of sameness across changing endpoints: the same dependency can persist after a person is replaced; the same person can enter a materially different dependency. This gives relational individuation actual work without declaring it universally prior.

The plural-partition account offers another route. A subject division is warranted relative to a question and an operation, while cross-subject relations disclose when that warrant ends. Its strongest limitation is dependence on the supplied question. If the initial question contains the central mistake, purpose-relative organization can preserve it perfectly. Consequently, question formation must be among the activities capable of altering subject boundaries. The inquiry cannot put its organizing aim outside the relations it is studying.

There are warranted local eliminations. A fixed taxonomy is inadequate as the sole organizer wherever the relevant identities change through relations it cannot represent. An unqualified “everything is connected” is inadequate wherever it cannot distinguish dependencies that change the answer from connections that do not. A single imposed relational ontology is also unearned if several different individuations explain different aspects without contradiction.

For subjectsystems, the concrete implication is that a discovery cannot always arrive as a new entry or link. It may invalidate the identity conditions of existing entries. To represent such a revision accurately, the record must preserve the original claim with its original meanings, represent the revised successor separately, and specify which commitments, if any, carry across the change. It should also be able to record that two subjects were one activity divided for convenience and distinguish disappearance of a boundary from loss of the distinctions it previously protected. Changing what a noun denotes does not by itself preserve the claim expressed with it. Without these distinctions, accumulated organization can become a veto on the discoveries it was designed to support.

What is established is that subjects can be products of inquiry and relations, not merely its inputs. What remains unresolved is a general basis for choosing or generating the right unit when neither the aim nor the admissible relations are already secure. Replacing the old catalog with a universal catalog of relations does not settle that question.

Disposition: 1–2 remain live for some subjects. 3–5, 7–8, and 10–12 are compatible means of individuation under specified conditions. 6 defeats mandatory independence of participants. 9 and 14 expose possible distortions. 13 remains a live foundational rival, not a universal conclusion. 15 is warranted locally; its sufficiency when aims change remains unresolved. Exhaustion is unproved.

9. A sincerely pursued goal can express an inadequate understanding of what is wanted.

Sincerity establishes that someone is pursuing what they take themselves to want. It does not establish that their formulation exhausts what gives the pursuit its significance. The difficult issue is how a goal can change for reasons that matter without requiring the old goal to authorize every change or treating every later preference as superior.

Fifteen accounts locate the inadequacy differently.

1. **An inaccurate means-end belief.** Someone genuinely wants health and pursues an activity they falsely believe promotes it. The final aim remains intact; the route changes.
2. **A proxy mistaken for its point.** Someone pursues flawless scores because scores once indicated understanding. The measure becomes the goal even when it ceases to track what originally mattered.
3. **An impoverished description of the desired state.** “I want agreement” fails to distinguish willing understanding from coerced assent. The aim changes when its previously conflated realizations become visible.
4. **Unrecognized internal conflict.** A person sincerely wants independence and constant external reassurance. Neither avowal is insincere; their combined practical requirements may be incompatible in particular circumstances.
5. **A missing experiential distinction.** Someone cannot yet distinguish admiration from being understood. An encounter can make a desire articulable that was not an available candidate in their earlier deliberation.
6. **Desire shaped by deprivation.** A person can cease to want what has seemed inaccessible. Present preference alone may then understate what they would value with different opportunities; that counterfactual still requires support.
7. **Affective misweighting.** What feels urgent dominates what the person continues to endorse over time. The mismatch concerns practical force, not necessarily an incorrect belief about importance.
8. **An inherited demand experienced as one's own.** A person's ambition can preserve an authority whose claim they have never examined. Its origin does not automatically discredit it, but makes its authority a separate question.
9. **An identity sustained by pursuit.** Wanting to win can maintain a sense of being a certain kind of person. Abandoning the contest changes more than an isolated preference and may initially appear unintelligible.
10. **Value created through commitment.** Some undertakings become important partly because someone commits to them. A changed goal can constitute a new practical identity rather than reveal an eternally hidden authentic desire.
11. **Change through acquaintance.** Learning what a life or activity is like changes the person who evaluates it. Choosing cannot always be reduced to applying unchanged preferences to fuller information.
12. **Several irreducible goods.** A single aim can conceal values that resist one ranking. The inadequacy lies in demanding that one target represent everything worth pursuing.
13. **A goal shared imperfectly among people.** “Our aim” can hide different purposes and unequal authority. Reformulating it changes whose desire the collective activity expresses.
14. **A demand to eliminate exposure.** Seeking certainty or complete control can express a wish never again to depend on what one cannot anticipate. This is one possible interpretation of perfection, not a diagnosis of this inquiry.
15. **An inadequate form of wanting.** Treating every worthwhile relation as a state to acquire can miss activities whose value consists in participating, attending, or answering appropriately. The revision concerns how wanting is organized, not just its object.

The strongest familiar account combines the first three: discover the actual aim, correct beliefs, replace proxies, and pursue what the person really wanted all along. This deserves retention where the evidence supports it. If someone sought accurate understanding and treated citation counts as a reliable measure, exposing the broken link can warrant changing the measure without revising the value of understanding.

But the account becomes presumptuous when it invents a fully formed deeper desire to explain every change. Suppose an encounter makes a kind of obligation recognizable that previously had no place in the person's account of a good life. It need not be an old desire finally uncovered. Under possibilities five, ten, and eleven, the encounter creates a distinction, a commitment, or a capacity for concern. Goal formation is then partly developmental: the person becomes able to want something, and that changes what earlier aims mean.

The strongest incompatible rival says that these are changes of preference, not improvements. Without an enduring criterion, the later person has no automatic authority over the earlier one. That objection defeats the inference “I now endorse it, therefore it was better for me to become someone who endorses it.” Manipulation could produce the same structure of endorsement. The developmental account needs grounds that distinguish becoming able to recognize something from being trained to approve a replacement. Those grounds may involve the newly accessible reasons, consequences, and freedom to reject the transition. None alone is a universal certificate.

A third continuation questions the goal form itself. Suppose perfected perspective is understood as possessing a finished account that settles every possible future inquiry. This can make an unresolved question appear to be a defect merely because it interrupts completion. Yet a perfected understanding might correctly recognize that a question is indeterminate, lacks the presupposed object, or requires an act whose consequences do not yet exist. Recognizing this would not be a retreat into open-mindedness. It would be an exact achievement: knowing why a demanded determination is unwarranted and what, if anything, could make it warranted.

There remains a much stronger ambition than “stay flexible”: nothing relevant should be misrepresented, wrongly excluded, or subjected to an inappropriate demand. That formulation, however, is still a candidate. “Relevant,” “misrepresented,” and “inappropriate” do not become self-interpreting because the goal sounds less rigid. The inquiry must permit discovery to alter those terms too.

A responsible departure therefore distinguishes at least three claims: the prior formulation misdescribed an enduring concern; a newly encountered concern deserves standing alongside it; and the form of aiming itself obstructs what matters. These claims have different burdens. The first requires evidence of the enduring concern. The second requires reasons for the added standing without pretending it was always secretly desired. The third requires showing the specific exclusion produced by the form, rather than announcing that goals are limiting.

Combinations are warranted when they preserve these differences. A proxy may fail while the underlying concern also changes. A commitment can create value without making every commitment valuable. Several goods can resist complete ranking while some particular comparisons remain decisive. None of these conclusions permits eliminating the pursuit of perfection merely because its current wording is defective.

What is established is that sincerity and persistence cannot certify either the content or the form of a goal. Our pursuit may concern complete understanding, justified authority over our own inquiry, access to presently unavailable forms of significance, or some combination we have not yet correctly distinguished. The unresolved obstacle is how to warrant transformation of what matters when present endorsement, future endorsement, and conformity to an inherited ideal are all insufficient. That obstacle must remain inside the inquiry rather than being settled by declaring one version of our desire authentic.

Disposition: 1–3 survive for correction within a concern. 4 and 12 expose possible conflicts. 5, 10, and 11 remain compatible developmental accounts. 6–9 and 13–14 remain conditional explanations, not diagnoses. 15 remains a live challenge to the form of pursuit. Later endorsement as sufficient certification is eliminated. No listed concern is established as our final aim.

10. Improve what counts as improvement without appointing a new unquestionable judge.

A standard does two things that must be separated: it can supply a reason for preferring something, and it can decide which kinds of reasons are allowed to count. The central mistake occurs when its useful first role is silently promoted into unconditional authority over the second.

Fifteen alternatives reveal different ways that authority could fail.

1. **Predictive success.** Prefer the account that anticipates observations. This can reject understanding whose immediate contribution is changing which observations should be distinguished.
2. **Demonstrative certainty.** Prefer what follows from stated premises. This can reject the conceptual reconstruction needed to recognize that the premises or objects were incorrectly posed.
3. **Internal consistency.** Prefer mutually compatible claims. This can preserve an incomplete vocabulary that achieves consistency by being unable to express a consequential distinction.
4. **Simplicity.** Prefer fewer commitments. This can reject necessary distinctions as needless elaboration, while complexity can also hide a failure to understand.
5. **Explanatory reach.** Prefer an account that covers more cases. This can reward a vocabulary that calls unlike things the same and therefore appears universal.
6. **Practical success.** Prefer what achieves an aim. This can reject an understanding that reveals the aim itself to be mistaken.
7. **Experienced intelligibility.** Prefer what makes immediate sense. This can exclude understanding whose concepts are not yet available to the evaluator.
8. **Transformation of ability.** Prefer what enables previously unavailable action or discrimination. This can mistake increased power for improved judgment about its use.
9. **Retrospective endorsement.** Prefer what a later, informed person approves. This can fail when the transition produced the approval it then cites as justification.
10. **Agreement among capable inquirers.** Prefer what survives diverse examination. This can preserve an error shared by their training, access, or selection into the group.
11. **Accountability to affected people.** Prefer what those bearing consequences can challenge. This can reveal excluded reasons, while agreement among affected people need not establish factual correctness.
12. **Correction of characteristic failure.** Prefer a change that removes a demonstrated obstruction. This supplies a local reason without proving that the resulting account is best overall.
13. **Preservation across changes of representation.** Prefer what remains warranted under several ways of expressing it. This can expose dependence on arbitrary notation, but several representations may share the same omission.
14. **Generation of worthwhile possibilities.** Prefer an account that makes further understanding possible. This can distinguish a fertile reorganization from sterile closure, while fertility alone also admits endless distraction.
15. **Exact recognition of limits.** Prefer an account that correctly identifies where its authority ends. This can avoid false certainty, but mere modesty does not establish that the stated limits are real.

The strongest response is initially to combine these standards and prevent any one from dominating. But a weighted combination appoints a new judge: the choice of standards, weights, and conflict rules. Even a rule requiring unanimous agreement gives each included standard veto power and excludes absent standards. Combining judges does not remove the question of jurisdiction.

There is a deeper failure than unfair weighting. Sound standards can divide their work along the wrong units. Suppose a description of who controls a meeting is ranked against a description of how long each person speaks as competing accounts of participation. Neither must defeat the other: their relation can reveal a form of control that equal durations conceal. Increasing the fairness of their comparison preserves the mistaken demand that one account win or that their verdicts be averaged. The needed change dissolves that comparison and supplies a different object to understand. This possibility must remain available before any procedure begins eliminating candidates one by one.

One continuation seeks a superior criterion capable of explaining the others. For example, “prefer what supports accurate understanding” could subordinate simplicity, prediction, and consistency to their contribution to accuracy. This is sensible where accuracy has a determinate object and assessment. It does not resolve the present question when what counts as understanding or as the relevant object is itself disputed. Repeating the ambition at a higher level does not supply the missing discrimination.

A second continuation replaces ranking with specific demonstrations of inadequacy. If one account cannot distinguish coerced assent from informed agreement, and both occur within the class it purports to explain, its claim to explain agreement is incomplete. That conclusion does not require already possessing a perfected account of agreement. The starting account contributes a claim with implications; a difference it collapses matters to those implications. It can lose universal authority without every useful distinction it contains being discarded.

The strongest rival replies that deciding which distinctions matter still invokes a standard. Correct. This continuation avoids an all-purpose judge, not every act of judgment. Its force is conditional and specific: an account that undertakes to explain voluntary endorsement cannot treat the difference between coercion and voluntary endorsement as irrelevant without changing its undertaking. The argument establishes a defect relative to a claim. It does not compel someone who rejects that undertaking to value it. Universal normative authority has not been derived.

A third continuation allows transformation to establish access to reasons that were formerly unintelligible. Someone learning a mathematical distinction can later understand why their earlier objection missed the issue. The earlier vocabulary could not fully certify the understanding it lacked. The transition can therefore precede complete antecedent justification. But this does not license “whatever changes me is better.” The later account owes an explanation of the earlier objection, the new distinction, and why the transition warrants anything beyond its own endorsement. Where such an explanation remains unavailable, the transformation may be valuable but is not certified as perfected understanding.

This continuation preserves neither an absolute predecessor veto nor an absolute successor veto. A successor can explain why a predecessor's standard was too narrow; a predecessor's unanswered objection can still expose a successor's error. The authority belongs to the particular reason and its demonstrated bearing, provisionally understood, rather than to being early or late. That itself is an account open to criticism, not a mechanism proven to escape all circularity.

The appropriate eliminations are correspondingly exact. Eliminate the claim that later approval alone proves improvement. Eliminate the claim that success under a supplied aim settles whether the aim deserved pursuit. Eliminate the inference from joint agreement among existing standards to absence of a shared defect. Retain prediction, proof, coherence, consequences, and testimony wherever their bearing can be established. Their limits do not make them worthless, and their usefulness does not restore unconditional jurisdiction.

What would exceed our present standards? An account that initially looks less simple because it exposes a difference we cannot afford to omit; one that suspends an apparently decisive answer because the question conflates incompatible demands; one that changes what evidence can establish without denying any observation; one that reveals a concern whose significance our existing ranking cannot express. These are structural possibilities, not a claim that any deliberately difficult or unfamiliar account is superior.

What is established is the difference between using a standard and granting it unrestricted authority over admissible successors. We can remove particular overclaims, identify specific gains, and preserve unresolved disputes without pretending they have been resolved. The exact remaining obstacle is a warrant for final authority that neither assumes the disputed standards nor merely appeals to the successor's self-endorsement. No such warrant has been supplied here. A procedure that concealed this gap would reproduce the original mistake while announcing that it had overcome it.

Disposition: 1–8 and 10–15 survive as sources of particular reasons; each claim to unconditional jurisdiction is eliminated. 9 survives as evidence about later endorsement, not sufficient evidence of improvement. 12 supplies local eliminations, not final ranking. Combining the survivors remains conditional on correct units and relationships. Their completeness and any universally authoritative ordering remain unproved.
