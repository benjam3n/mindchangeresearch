# Mind Change Inventory

Each of the original **590 entries** now identifies candidate direct contributors and conditions that can make the proposed relationship work, fail, or produce a different change. The **26 functional AI self-change entries** receive the same treatment. Entry identities and original definitions are preserved. Human experience is the default scope; the AI section describes functional changes without attributing human feelings to an AI.

**Choosing the relevant contribution**

| What is missing in the particular case | A contribution that addresses that dependency | Nearby change that can leave it unresolved |
|---|---|---|
| A distinction | Cases requiring different treatment, with the diagnostic difference made available | Another synonym for an already understood idea |
| Access to an intention when it matters | A detectable event linked to a retained, specific intended action | Stronger general agreement with the goal |
| An executable operation | Its required inputs, a usable performance, and correction of the unavailable component | A description that assumes the component can already be performed |
| Actual influence over an outcome | An available action with a consequential effect | Language describing the person as empowered |
| Correction governing later conduct | A recognizable reuse condition and a replacement operation that consumes the correction | Acknowledgment or storage without applicable use |

These are separating examples, not a universal sequence or a complete classification of dependencies. The detailed entries preserve other possibilities.

“Direct” here means a specified nearby dependency at the functional or experiential level: an input that alters the target, an operation that realizes the change, a connection that makes one process govern another, or a condition that enables or obstructs it. It does not mean an unmediated influence on the brain. These roles should be distinguished when constructing a particular intervention.

Semicolon-separated contributors are possibilities to examine, not a claim that every one is necessary, sufficient, independent, beneficial, or appropriate for every person. **Unmarked contributor relationships are an original functional synthesis and candidate map, not separately established causal findings.** Source markers support the particular attached statement at the scope described in the source note; they do not validate the whole entry. Physiological descriptions identify possible contributors without supplying individualized treatment instructions.

Coverage is complete relative to the inherited inventory. Both the inventory and the contributor sets remain open to further distinctions.

**Mind-change targets, contributors, and conditions**

Each contributor set is a candidate map at the stated functional level. Only the particular source-marked statements carry the empirical or theoretical support specified in their notes. Neighboring entries can describe different components of the same event.

<a id="family-1"></a>
**1. Wakefulness and awareness**

**1. Wakefulness:** waking, becoming drowsy, or falling asleep.

Contributors: Accumulating or dissipating sleep pressure; circadian wake signals; activating sensory input; changes in brain function or substance exposure.

Conditions and distinctions: Sleep restriction can impair performance even when reported sleepiness changes little further; felt alertness and measured vigilance can diverge [S01](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/).

**2. Conscious access:** an otherwise unreported content entering awareness.

Contributors: Making content stronger or longer lasting; removing masking or competing content; directing attention to the relevant feature; connecting input to an available interpretation.

Conditions and distinctions: An accurate response without a report of awareness does not settle conscious access; reporting ability is a separate dependency.

**3. Awareness breadth:** more or less of an experience being noticed.

Contributors: Scanning additional sensory or internal channels; loosening a narrow task focus; reducing a dominant competing demand; supplying prompts for previously omitted aspects.

Conditions and distinctions: Noticing more aspects can reduce detail per aspect. Attention breadth and experienced awareness are related but distinct targets.

**4. Clarity:** experience becoming clearer, hazier, or more confused.

Contributors: Resolving conflicting interpretations; restoring missing context; improving signal quality; reducing simultaneous demands; changes in physiological alertness.

Conditions and distinctions: Identify whether the haze concerns sensory input, meaning, memory, or wakefulness; the same intervention need not affect each.

**5. Absorption:** becoming immersed in, or disengaging from, an activity.

Contributors: A continuously available task; successive challenges within current capability; compelling sensory or narrative continuity; reduced interruptions; stronger competing events that break immersion.

Conditions and distinctions: Immersion can support an activity or conceal relevant surroundings. Its value depends on what requires access.

**6. Dream awareness:** recognizing a dream while dreaming.

Contributors: A dream containing a noticed inconsistency; recall of an intention to recognize dreaming; emergence of reflective awareness during sleep.

Conditions and distinctions: These are candidate relationships, not a reliable induction method; remembering a dream later does not establish awareness during it.

**7. Self-awareness:** noticing oneself as participating in an experience.

Contributors: A prompt to notice one's current action; reflection or self-reference; attention to one's bodily participation; feedback showing one's contribution to an event.

Conditions and distinctions: Self-monitoring may become distraction or evaluative self-consciousness; noticing oneself need not improve performance.

**8. Environmental awareness:** becoming more or less aware of surroundings.

Contributors: Unobstructed sensory access; scanning the surroundings; cues identifying consequential changes; releasing attention from an absorbing internal or external task.

Conditions and distinctions: Information must actually reach the person; a warning cannot reveal an inaccessible environment by itself.

**2. Bodily experience**

**9. Fatigue:** feeling more or less mentally or physically tired.

Contributors: Accumulated exertion and sleep loss; relief from sustained demands; illness-related bodily signals; changes in perceived remaining effort and payoff.

Conditions and distinctions: Felt fatigue, physical exhaustion, and declining accuracy require separate interpretation; their trajectories may differ [S01](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/).

**10. Vitality:** feeling more or less energetic or vigorous.

Contributors: Recovery from depletion; activating movement or sensory experience; anticipated achievable reward; relief from sustained stress or obstructed effort.

Conditions and distinctions: An energizing interpretation cannot be assumed to correct a physiological limitation; vitality is a felt outcome.

**11. Comfort:** changes in felt ease, discomfort, or restlessness.

Contributors: Changing pressure, posture, temperature, noise, or bodily irritation; meeting an unmet bodily need; increasing predictability and control over the setting.

Conditions and distinctions: The contributor depends on the source of discomfort; social reassurance cannot remove an uncomfortable physical stimulus.

**12. Pain:** changes in experienced intensity, location, unpleasantness, or meaning.

Contributors: Changes in nociceptive input or central processing; attention to pain; expected relief or threat; the meaning assigned to the sensation.

Conditions and distinctions: Intensity, unpleasantness, and interpretation can change separately. Expectation effects in experimental pain do not establish removal of injury [S02](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/placebo-induced-changes-in-fmri-in-the-anticipation-and-experienc).

**13. Tension:** changes in felt tightness, relaxation, or readiness.

Contributors: Changes in muscle contraction and posture; preparing for or ending an anticipated action; perceived threat; deliberate release of a held contraction.

Conditions and distinctions: Felt tightness may persist without voluntary contraction; a bodily description alone does not identify its cause.

**14. Hunger and satiety:** changes in felt need for or satisfaction from food.

Contributors: Signals of energy need; oral and gastrointestinal input from eating; learned meal cues; anticipated food; sensory-specific loss of appeal during consumption.

Conditions and distinctions: Wanting a particular food and general hunger can diverge; the relevant contributor depends on which is changing.

**15. Thirst:** changes in felt need for fluid.

Contributors: Body-fluid regulation; dry-mouth sensations; oral signals from drinking; heat and fluid loss; learned access cues.

Conditions and distinctions: Dry mouth and physiological fluid need are not interchangeable; this is a contributor map, not intake guidance.

**16. Sexual arousal:** changes in felt sexual responsiveness or interest.

Contributors: Personally meaningful erotic cues or imagination; wanted touch and interaction; bodily and hormonal state; relationship context; stress or distraction.

Conditions and distinctions: Subjective desire, physiological response, willingness, and consent are separate; none can be inferred solely from another.

**17. Bodily orientation:** changes in felt balance, movement, position, or ownership.

Contributors: Vestibular, visual, and proprioceptive input; actual movement; agreement or conflict between seen and felt position; expected sensory consequences of action.

Conditions and distinctions: Balance, location, movement, and ownership can dissociate; specify the component before selecting a contributor.

**18. Interoceptive awareness:** changes in noticing and distinguishing internal bodily signals.

Contributors: Directing attention to internal signals; distinguishing similar sensations; labeling location and quality; comparing sensations with independently available physiological information.

Conditions and distinctions: Greater confidence in bodily interpretation need not mean greater accuracy; indiscriminate monitoring may amplify ambiguity.

**3. Arousal and readiness**

**19. Alertness:** becoming more or less ready to detect events.

Contributors: A temporally informative warning; an imminent detection requirement; changing sleep pressure; activating or monotonous input.

Conditions and distinctions: Brief alerting and sustained vigilance differ; temporary stimulation does not establish restored performance after sleep loss [S01](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/).

**20. Agitation:** becoming more or less unsettled or activated.

Contributors: Unexpected demands; unresolved threat or conflict; intense stimulation; physiological activation; removal of the provoking condition.

Conditions and distinctions: Quiet behavior may conceal continuing agitation; reducing expression alone need not settle the underlying state.

**21. Composure:** gaining or losing the ability to remain settled under pressure.

Contributors: A practiced response available under pressure; predictable next steps; perceived control over a consequential action; recovery time; reinterpretation of the demand.

Conditions and distinctions: Composure can coexist with strong emotion; a calm-sounding instruction is not evidence that composure occurred.

**22. Vigilance:** changing readiness to detect possible threats or opportunities.

Contributors: Expected event frequency and consequence; learned warning cues; instructions specifying what to monitor; recent alarms or misses.

Conditions and distinctions: Excessive monitoring can increase false alarms and consume attention needed elsewhere; specify the relevant threat or opportunity.

**23. Startle readiness:** changing susceptibility to sudden interruption.

Contributors: Sudden stimulus intensity; uncertainty about onset; prior warning; current defensive activation; repetition that changes the event's novelty.

Conditions and distinctions: Preparation for a known event and general sensitization can move startle differently.

**24. Felt safety:** a situation coming to feel safer or more threatening.

Contributors: Removal of an actual threat; credible protection or escape options; trustworthy responses from others; new evidence about a previously ambiguous situation.

Conditions and distinctions: Felt safety can be inaccurate. Reassurance without changed grounds may alter feeling while leaving danger unchanged.

**25. Action readiness:** becoming poised for approach, withdrawal, speech, or movement.

Contributors: A recognized opportunity or threat; a selected response; motor preparation; availability of the required object or opening; learned action cues.

Conditions and distinctions: Readiness is specific to an action; general activation can leave the needed response unavailable.

**26. Recovery:** returning more quickly or slowly after activation.

Contributors: Termination of the provoking input; recognition that the episode has ended; disengagement from repeated threat simulation; restoration of bodily resources.

Conditions and distinctions: Returning to baseline and recovering usable capability are distinct; continuing demands can repeatedly restart activation.

**4. Sensory discrimination**

**27. Detection:** becoming able or unable to notice a stimulus.

Contributors: Greater signal intensity or duration; reduced masking; informative timing or location cues; a response criterion more willing to report a signal.

Conditions and distinctions: More detections may come from more false alarms rather than improved sensitivity.

**28. Sensitivity:** weaker or stronger stimulation becoming noticeable.

Contributors: Changes in sensory adaptation; lower background noise; attention to the relevant channel; physiological sensory function.

Conditions and distinctions: Distinguish a changed threshold from changed willingness to report and from an altered physical stimulus.

**29. Resolution:** distinguishing finer or coarser sensory differences.

Contributors: Access to fine features; side-by-side contrasts; repeated discrimination of near neighbors; informative correction about the confusable feature.

Conditions and distinctions: Improvement may remain specific to the practiced stimulus, position, or task [S03](https://www.academia.edu/2879452/Where_practice_makes_perfect_in_texture_discrimination_evidence_for_primary_visual_cortex_plasticity).

**30. Auditory discrimination:** distinguishing sounds, voices, tones, or rhythms.

Contributors: Contrasting confusable sounds; isolating pitch, timing, or timbre differences; reduced acoustic masking; pairing a sound with its correct source or label.

Conditions and distinctions: Volume alone cannot teach a phonetic distinction; auditory access and learned discrimination impose different limits.

**31. Visual discrimination:** distinguishing shapes, colors, movements, or configurations.

Contributors: Comparing confusable shapes or configurations; highlighting diagnostic visual features; repeated discrimination; improved contrast or visibility.

Conditions and distinctions: Texture-learning results support local learning, not a general improvement in all vision [S03](https://www.academia.edu/2879452/Where_practice_makes_perfect_in_texture_discrimination_evidence_for_primary_visual_cortex_plasticity).

**32. Tactile discrimination:** distinguishing textures, pressures, or locations.

Contributors: Controlled differences in texture or pressure; exploring an object through movement; contrast between nearby locations; feedback identifying the relevant sensation.

Conditions and distinctions: Skin location, stimulus force, and sensory function constrain which distinctions become available.

**33. Smell and taste discrimination:** distinguishing previously conflated sensations.

Contributors: Comparing samples while tracking a specific sensory dimension; identifying component qualities; reduced competing odors or tastes; changes in adaptation.

Conditions and distinctions: A richer label vocabulary can change reports without establishing finer sensory sensitivity.

**34. Signal discrimination:** separating a relevant signal from background variation.

Contributors: Reducing irrelevant variation; revealing a diagnostic feature; comparing signal-present and signal-absent cases; calibrating the reporting criterion.

Conditions and distinctions: Signal quality and decision bias need separate checks; added salience can amplify noise as well as signal.

**5. Perceptual organization**

**35. Figure and ground:** changing which part stands out against a background.

Contributors: Contrast in color, motion, depth, or contour; directing attention to a region; changing which shape is treated as the foreground.

Conditions and distinctions: A foreground shift changes organization without requiring a change in the physical objects.

**36. Grouping:** experiencing separate elements as a unit or a unit as separate.

Contributors: Proximity, similarity, continuity, or shared movement; common labels; enclosing boundaries; a task that treats elements jointly.

Conditions and distinctions: Perceptual unity does not establish that the elements form a causal or functional unit.

**37. Boundary perception:** changing where one perceived thing ends and another begins.

Contributors: Sharper edges; occlusion cues; discontinuities in color, motion, or texture; explicit segmentation; alternative object interpretations.

Conditions and distinctions: A drawn border can impose a division that the underlying process does not have.

**38. Ambiguity resolution:** moving between interpretations of an ambiguous stimulus.

Contributors: Context favoring one interpretation; cues diagnostic between alternatives; prior exposure; attention to an interpretation-specific feature.

Conditions and distinctions: An interpretation becoming dominant does not prove it uniquely fits an ambiguous stimulus.

**39. Spatial organization:** changing perceived depth, distance, orientation, or layout.

Contributors: Changing viewpoint; movement-based depth information; binocular and perspective cues; landmarks; explicit orientation information.

Conditions and distinctions: A map can improve layout knowledge without changing every immediate depth percept.

**40. Temporal organization:** experiencing events as simultaneous, sequential, or rhythmic.

Contributors: Event spacing; repeated rhythm; onset cues; grouping events into units; expectations about sequence.

Conditions and distinctions: Perceived simultaneity and physical simultaneity differ; retrospective grouping may change the experienced sequence.

**41. Multisensory integration:** changing how signals from different senses are combined.

Contributors: Cross-sensory agreement in timing and location; reliability of each channel; compatible or conflicting source cues.

Conditions and distinctions: Visual speech can alter what is heard [S04](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315942336_Hearing_Lips_and_Seeing_Voices_the_Origins_and_Development_of_the_%27McGurk_Effect%27_and_Reflections_on_Audio-Visual_Speech_Perception_Over_the_Last_40_Years); integration can introduce error when signals have different sources.

**42. Perceptual constancy:** changing what is treated as stable despite changing input.

Contributors: Stable relationships across viewpoints; information about illumination, distance, or movement; comparison with a reference object.

Conditions and distinctions: Constancy is inferred across changing input; unusual contexts can make a normally useful inference misleading.

**6. Recognition**

**43. Identity recognition:** recognizing a person, object, place, or event.

Contributors: Diagnostic identifying features; exposure to different views of the same entity; retrieval cues; distinguishing the entity from similar alternatives.

Conditions and distinctions: Familiarity can occur without correct identity; resemblance is insufficient when impostors or duplicates are possible.

**44. Pattern recognition:** noticing a repeated or governing arrangement.

Contributors: Juxtaposing instances; aligning repeated relations; removing irrelevant surface differences; representing the arrangement explicitly.

Conditions and distinctions: A visible regularity may be accidental; recognizing it and establishing its reliability are separate changes.

**45. Familiarity:** something coming to feel familiar or unfamiliar.

Contributors: Prior encounters; repeated exposure; overlap with known features; easier processing; contextual reminders.

Conditions and distinctions: Familiarity is not evidence of truth, safety, prior agreement, or accurate recollection.

**46. Application recognition:** recognizing that a known method applies here.

Contributors: Matching current preconditions to a known method; contrasting an applicable and inapplicable case; indexing the method by the situation it resolves.

Conditions and distinctions: A shared topic is insufficient; the method's required inputs and exclusions must fit.

**47. Opportunity recognition:** noticing an available beneficial possibility.

Contributors: Making an unused resource or opening visible; connecting it to a valued consequence; removing an assumed constraint; identifying a feasible next move.

Conditions and distinctions: An attractive imaginable outcome becomes an opportunity only if some route is available under the actual conditions.

**48. Hazard recognition:** noticing a consequential threat or failure possibility.

Contributors: Diagnostic warning signs; causal knowledge of a failure path; comparison with a near miss; visibility of consequential dependencies.

Conditions and distinctions: Vivid examples can inflate perceived prevalence; detection of possibility and estimation of likelihood are different.

**49. Incongruity recognition:** noticing that elements do not fit together.

Contributors: Making incompatible elements simultaneously available; stating the expected relation; comparing the case with that relation.

Conditions and distinctions: An apparent mismatch can arise from a mistaken expectation rather than from a defective case.

**50. Affordance recognition:** noticing what an object or situation permits one to do.

Contributors: Visible handles, openings, and controls; demonstrations of use; knowledge of object properties; fit between bodily capability and environment.

Conditions and distinctions: What an object permits depends on the actor; an apparent affordance may be inaccessible or deceptive.

**7. Attention**

**51. Orientation:** directing attention toward something previously unattended.

Contributors: A spatial or verbal cue; sudden change; a question naming an overlooked feature; an object connected to the active goal.

Conditions and distinctions: Orienting toward something consumes attention elsewhere; the selected direction must serve the current activity.

**52. Selection:** changing which competing content receives attention.

Contributors: A current selection rule; relative salience; reward history; removing distractors; placing needed content at the decision point.

Conditions and distinctions: Selecting more relevant content can improve use of limited memory; selection efficiency is not identical to memory capacity [S05](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16306992/).

**53. Maintenance:** sustaining attention for a longer or shorter interval.

Contributors: A continuously meaningful task signal; manageable substeps; reminders of the active object; reduced interruptions; planned points for reorientation.

Conditions and distinctions: Persistence without sensitivity to changed conditions can become fixation.

**54. Disengagement:** releasing attention from a currently dominant object.

Contributors: A clear completion or suspension condition; recording the unfinished matter; a stronger relevant cue; reducing repeated re-exposure.

Conditions and distinctions: Disengagement can leave unresolved work unavailable later unless a return condition is preserved.

**55. Switching:** moving attention between objects or activities.

Contributors: An explicit switch cue; visible state of the incoming task; preservation of the outgoing task's state; removal of obsolete instructions.

Conditions and distinctions: A fast switch can still carry the previous rule into the new task.

**56. Division:** distributing attention across multiple demands.

Contributors: Separating demands by timing or modality; automating a practiced component; external monitoring of one stream; explicit priority when demands conflict.

Conditions and distinctions: Several demanding operations may still compete for the same capacity; division is not unlimited parallel processing.

**57. Breadth:** narrowing focus or taking in a wider field.

Contributors: A prompt to inspect context or detail; changing display scale; reducing a dominant threat cue; making peripheral information relevant.

Conditions and distinctions: Widening attention can sacrifice precision; narrowing can hide a consequential dependency.

**58. Interruption resistance:** becoming more or less distractible.

Contributors: Removing avoidable alerts; distinguishing interruption criteria; making the active goal continuously available; arranging a protected work interval.

Conditions and distinctions: Necessary warnings still need an access route; complete isolation can obstruct required information.

**59. Timing:** attending at the moment a relevant difference occurs.

Contributors: A cue synchronized with the event; predictable intervals; a reminder at the point of use; maintaining the relevant detection rule.

Conditions and distinctions: Information delivered too early may be forgotten and too late may be unusable.

**60. Control:** changing the relation between chosen and involuntary attention.

Contributors: A deliberately chosen focus; recognition of capture; an available return action; environmental control over recurring distractors.

Conditions and distinctions: Voluntary instructions compete with learned and stimulus-driven capture; wanting control does not establish it.

<a id="family-8"></a>
**8. Salience and relevance**

**61. Noticeability:** something becoming harder or easier to overlook.

Contributors: Contrast, placement, repetition, labeling, novelty, or a distinctive comparison; removing competing emphasis.

Conditions and distinctions: If everything is highlighted, the intended item may gain no relative noticeability.

**62. Importance:** changing how much something seems to matter.

Contributors: Showing consequences for a valued concern; changing the scale or scope represented; making a neglected dependency visible.

Conditions and distinctions: Something can feel important because it is vivid even when its consequence is small.

**63. Urgency:** changing how soon something seems to require a response.

Contributors: A real deadline; showing how delay changes options; detecting an approaching threshold; distinguishing reversible from expiring opportunities.

Conditions and distinctions: Urgency should change with delay cost; artificial pressure can produce haste without improving the choice.

**64. Personal relevance:** connecting or disconnecting a matter from oneself.

Contributors: Connecting the matter to an actual goal, experience, role, relationship, or constraint; supplying a self-relevant example.

Conditions and distinctions: Invented personalization can make an inaccurate connection feel intimate; use available recipient context.

**65. Practical relevance:** seeing or losing a connection to an actual activity.

Contributors: Showing where the information enters a real operation; supplying a needed input; demonstrating a changed decision or action.

Conditions and distinctions: An abstract statement of usefulness leaves practical relevance unspecified.

**66. Emotional prominence:** an item occupying more or less affective attention.

Contributors: An emotionally charged association; a concrete consequence for someone valued; repeated affective attention; reinterpreting the event's significance.

Conditions and distinctions: Emotional prominence can increase without improved understanding or warranted priority.

**67. Backgrounding:** an acknowledged matter ceasing to dominate.

Contributors: Resolving the concern; recording it with a return trigger; changing its perceived relevance; reducing repeated cues that reactivate it.

Conditions and distinctions: Backgrounding can be useful even while the matter remains unresolved, provided later access is preserved.

**68. Priority competition:** changing which of several concerns comes to the foreground.

Contributors: Explicit precedence between concerns; visibility of opportunity costs; current consequences; deadlines; removing irrelevant competition.

Conditions and distinctions: The most activating concern need not be the most consequential one.

<a id="family-9"></a>
**9. Working memory**

**69. Entry:** making a representation available for immediate mental use.

Contributors: Presenting the needed content immediately before use; retrieving it with a distinctive cue; translating it into an accessible form.

Conditions and distinctions: Availability requires comprehension and attention; physical presence on a page alone is insufficient.

**70. Maintenance:** keeping a representation available during another operation.

Contributors: Rehearsing the relevant content; maintaining a visible reference; protecting it from competing input; organizing it into meaningful units.

Conditions and distinctions: External support changes supported performance; it does not by itself expand unaided capacity.

**71. Updating:** replacing outdated active content with current content.

Contributors: An authoritative replacement value; an explicit supersession signal; comparison of old and current versions; removal of stale copies.

Conditions and distinctions: Without item identity, replacing a value can update the wrong object.

**72. Binding:** keeping the correct features attached to the correct item.

Contributors: Stable names and locations; explicit feature-to-item links; integrated displays; rehearsing the relation as well as each feature.

Conditions and distinctions: Remembering all features separately does not preserve which belongs to which.

**73. Ordering:** changing the sequence in which active items are represented.

Contributors: Numbering; spatial arrangement; a temporal or causal sequence; rehearsing transitions between adjacent elements.

Conditions and distinctions: A memorable order can still be the wrong execution order.

**74. Chunking:** treating multiple elements as one usable unit.

Contributors: A learned pattern that encodes several elements jointly; a meaningful shared structure; a familiar abbreviation with recoverable contents.

Conditions and distinctions: A novel acronym may add another item instead of reducing load; the grouping must already be usable.

**75. Interference:** changing how much one active item disrupts another.

Contributors: Reducing similarity between competing items; separating contexts or times; removing irrelevant content; distinctive item labels.

Conditions and distinctions: Interference reduction is different from proving general capacity growth [S05](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16306992/).

**76. Comparison access:** making relevant items available together.

Contributors: Juxtaposing the relevant items; aligning corresponding dimensions; preserving common units and scope; externalizing one item while using another.

Conditions and distinctions: Two values are not comparable merely because they are adjacent; their meanings must align.

**77. Release:** removing no-longer-needed content from active use.

Contributors: A completion marker; an explicit discard or defer decision; storing what must survive; removing obsolete prompts.

Conditions and distinctions: Premature release creates omissions if a downstream operation still needs the content.

**10. Memory accessibility**

**78. Recall:** recovering previously unavailable information or experience.

Contributors: A distinctive cue; reinstating relevant context; reconstructing associations; narrowing retrieval by time, place, or relation.

Conditions and distinctions: A plausible reconstruction may feel like recall; access and accuracy need separate treatment.

**79. Recognition memory:** identifying something as previously encountered.

Contributors: Re-exposure to the item; comparison with remembered features; contextual corroboration; discrimination from similar alternatives.

Conditions and distinctions: Ease or familiarity can produce false recognition without recollection of the encounter.

**80. Cue dependence:** changing which prompts make a memory accessible.

Contributors: Encoding with a later-use cue; retrieving the same content from varied prompts; making the cue-content relation distinctive.

Conditions and distinctions: A cue can help only if it connects to a retained representation or supports a valid reconstruction.

**81. Spontaneous retrieval:** a memory becoming available without deliberate search.

Contributors: An environmental reminder; associative overlap with current experience; an unresolved concern; emotional or sensory resemblance.

Conditions and distinctions: Spontaneous arrival does not reveal whether the remembered content is relevant or accurate.

**82. Prospective remembering:** remembering an intended action when it becomes relevant.

Contributors: Linking a specific future event to an action; a visible reminder at that event; rehearsing the event-action pairing.

Conditions and distinctions: The event must be detected and the action feasible; a cue-linked plan is not a substitute for ability [S15](https://www.prospectivepsych.org/sites/default/files/pictures/Gollwitzer_Implementation-intentions-1999.pdf).

**83. Source retrieval:** recalling where a representation came from.

Contributors: Recording who, where, and when alongside content; source-distinctive cues; comparing the account with the original record.

Conditions and distinctions: A content memory can survive while source information is lost; confident attribution can still be mistaken.

**84. Context retrieval:** recovering the circumstances needed to interpret a memory.

Contributors: Restoring original circumstances, definitions, purpose, and constraints; a linked contextual record; cues from the surrounding episode.

Conditions and distinctions: Recovered context must fit this version of the memory rather than a similar encounter.

**85. Relevance retrieval:** recovering a memory in a situation where it matters.

Contributors: Indexing prior knowledge by its application conditions; comparing the current case with an earlier relational structure; a point-of-use retrieval cue.

Conditions and distinctions: Surface similarity may retrieve the wrong precedent; consequential conditions must match.

**86. Retrieval competition:** changing which memory comes to mind first.

Contributors: Cue specificity; recent retrieval of one candidate; distinctive context labels; separating commonly confused memories.

Conditions and distinctions: Making one memory easier to retrieve can leave equally relevant alternatives less available.

**11. Memory formation and revision**

**87. Encoding:** forming a new memory of an encounter.

Contributors: Attention to diagnostic features; relating material to existing knowledge; meaningful organization; active reconstruction during the encounter.

Conditions and distinctions: Exposure alone need not encode the relation later needed; meaningful elaboration can also introduce assumptions.

**88. Retention:** changing how long something remains available.

Contributors: Retrieval across later occasions; spacing encounters; preserving usable cues; reduced destructive interference; conditions supporting consolidation.

Conditions and distinctions: Retrieval and restudy can differ by delay: immediate fluency can favor a method that retains less later [S06](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/test-enhanced-learning-taking-memory-tests-improves-long-term-ret/).

**89. Detail:** retaining, recovering, or losing specific features.

Contributors: Attention to exact features; accurate external records; discrimination of similar episodes; retrieval that checks specifics rather than only gist.

Conditions and distinctions: Repeated retelling can stabilize selected details while losing others.

**90. Gist:** retaining or revising the broader meaning of an event.

Contributors: Extracting a common relation; summarizing consequences; integrating an event with prior schemas; later reinterpretation.

Conditions and distinctions: A coherent gist can omit an exception that changes the event's meaning.

**91. Association:** linking or unlinking memories with other material.

Contributors: Repeated co-occurrence; meaningful relational links; contrast with previously associated material; retrieval in a different context.

Conditions and distinctions: Co-occurrence can strengthen an association without showing a causal relationship.

**92. Updating:** incorporating later information into a remembered account.

Contributors: New event information; explicit correction of an earlier account; integration with a retained record; identification of what changed.

Conditions and distinctions: Later information can contaminate memory for the original event rather than merely supplement it [S07](https://app.nova.edu/toolbox/instructionalproducts/edd8124/articles/1975-Loftus.pdf).

**93. Forgetting:** losing access to or retention of prior material.

Contributors: Loss of retrieval cues; competing similar material; disuse or disrupted consolidation; changes in neurological function.

Conditions and distinctions: Failure to retrieve does not establish erasure; deliberately withholding use cannot guarantee permanent forgetting.

**94. Source confusion:** changing whether an idea is remembered as observed, inferred, or imagined.

Contributors: Separating observation, inference, suggestion, and imagination when recording; preserving original wording and provenance; checking later additions.

Conditions and distinctions: Repeated suggestion can alter later reports; labels help only if retained and trusted appropriately [S07](https://app.nova.edu/toolbox/instructionalproducts/edd8124/articles/1975-Loftus.pdf).

**95. Accuracy:** correcting a memory or introducing a distortion.

Contributors: Comparison with contemporaneous evidence; correction of a specific discrepancy; maintaining source boundaries; misleading post-event wording that introduces distortion.

Conditions and distinctions: Agreement among repeated retellings is not independent confirmation [S07](https://app.nova.edu/toolbox/instructionalproducts/edd8124/articles/1975-Loftus.pdf).

**96. Emotional association:** changing the feeling associated with remembered material.

Contributors: New experiences linked to the remembered material; revising its current significance; encountering it in a different emotional context.

Conditions and distinctions: Changed feeling does not rewrite the historical event; durable change cannot be inferred from one retelling.

**12. Concepts and categories**

**97. Concept acquisition:** gaining a previously unavailable distinction.

Contributors: Contrasting cases that require different treatment; diagnostic features; a stable label; using the distinction in classification.

Conditions and distinctions: Labels aided learning in a specific category task [S08](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/LupyanEtAl07_PsychSci.pdf); a word alone does not supply every needed distinction.

**98. Category splitting:** separating cases previously treated as equivalent.

Contributors: A consequential difference within a class; contrasting outcomes or requirements; explicit subcategory criteria; borderline examples.

Conditions and distinctions: Splitting adds value only if the new boundary supports a needed distinction or operation.

**99. Category merging:** recognizing cases as belonging to one class.

Contributors: A shared governing relation; abstraction from irrelevant differences; a common rule that handles formerly separate cases.

Conditions and distinctions: Merging can destroy exceptions that remain consequential.

**100. Boundary revision:** changing which cases count as members.

Contributors: Near-boundary cases; a changed membership criterion; counterexamples; clarification of necessary versus typical features.

Conditions and distinctions: Changing a boundary may change the concept, not merely correct its application.

**101. Prototype revision:** changing what counts as a typical example.

Contributors: Exposure to a different distribution of examples; emphasizing previously underrepresented members; comparing a stereotype with actual variation.

Conditions and distinctions: A salient unusual example can distort typicality rather than improve it.

**102. Definition revision:** changing what a term or concept commits one to.

Contributors: Explicit entailments; ambiguous uses; counterexamples to the current definition; a purpose requiring different commitments.

Conditions and distinctions: A stipulated revision does not retroactively change what previous speakers meant.

**103. Hierarchy revision:** changing relations among broader and narrower categories.

Contributors: Set inclusion or exclusion relationships; shared and distinguishing properties; cases revealing multiple parent categories.

Conditions and distinctions: A convenient tree can conceal overlapping membership or independent dimensions.

**104. Exception recognition:** identifying limits to an apparent rule.

Contributors: A contrary case; a violated precondition; a scope contrast; examination of the supposed rule's quantifier.

Conditions and distinctions: One exception defeats a universal claim but may leave a probabilistic tendency intact.

**105. Concept removal:** ceasing to organize a matter through an unhelpful category.

Contributors: Showing the category's misleading consequences; replacing its work with more discriminating dimensions; removing habitual labels from the task.

Conditions and distinctions: Deleting a word without replacing its useful distinctions can reduce capability.

**106. Concept transfer:** making a distinction usable in a different domain.

Contributors: Aligning relational structure across domains; identifying corresponding roles; constructing a new-domain example and a nonexample.

Conditions and distinctions: Transfer needs shared operative relations, not resemblance of vocabulary [S09](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/GentnerLoewensteinThompson03.pdf).

**13. Representation**

**107. Verbal representation:** expressing a matter in usable words.

Contributors: Naming entities and relations; constructing a concrete sentence; resolving ambiguous referents; supplying words for the missing distinction.

Conditions and distinctions: Fluent wording can conceal unresolved content; verbal availability is not evidence of truth.

**108. Visual representation:** making an image or diagram available.

Contributors: A diagram or image with meaningful correspondence; contrastive visual examples; spatially displaying otherwise hidden relations.

Conditions and distinctions: Visual detail can imply unsupported precision or causal links; correspondence must remain inspectable.

**109. Spatial representation:** organizing material through positions or distances.

Contributors: Assigning stable locations; mapping distance or adjacency to a specified relationship; arranging objects to expose comparisons.

Conditions and distinctions: Spatial distance must mean something defined; arbitrary placement can suggest a false relation.

**110. Quantitative representation:** expressing magnitudes and relationships numerically.

Contributors: Measurement; explicit units and denominators; counting comparable cases; representing uncertainty or scale numerically.

Conditions and distinctions: Numbers without justified measurement rules create precision without a valid quantity.

**111. Procedural representation:** organizing material as something to do.

Contributors: Identifying inputs, operations, order, and completion conditions; a worked execution; a branch for a consequential alternative.

Conditions and distinctions: A procedure is usable only when the actor can perform its steps with available inputs.

**112. Narrative representation:** organizing events through a story.

Contributors: Temporal order; agents and aims; causally connected events; selecting a viewpoint and relevant turning points.

Conditions and distinctions: Narrative coherence can invite causal conclusions beyond the evidence.

**113. Structural representation:** exposing parts, relations, and dependencies.

Contributors: Making components and dependencies explicit; distinguishing levels; tracing what consumes each intermediate result.

Conditions and distinctions: A diagram of boxes does not establish the proposed dependencies or their sufficiency.

**114. Compression:** reducing representational size while preserving needed distinctions.

Contributors: Removing repetition; substituting known chunks; preserving decision-relevant differences; deleting details irrelevant to the intended use.

Conditions and distinctions: Compression must specify its consumer; lossless for one use can be destructive for another.

**115. Expansion:** making previously compressed distinctions explicit.

Contributors: Unpacking an abbreviation; restoring omitted premises or cases; expanding a formula into operations; exposing hidden choices.

Conditions and distinctions: Expansion helps when it supplies missing distinctions; extra wording can increase load without doing so.

**116. Externalization:** using an external representation to alter what is mentally available.

Contributors: Writing, diagramming, arranging, or computing a representation that can be consulted and manipulated.

Conditions and distinctions: The person must interpret and coordinate with the aid; external form can change task difficulty [S10](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/zhang.pdf).

**14. Language and meaning**

**117. Vocabulary acquisition:** gaining a word for an available or new distinction.

Contributors: Pairing a word with clear examples and counterexamples; retrieval during actual use; correction of a mistaken sense.

Conditions and distinctions: Recognizing the word and producing it appropriately are separate abilities.

**118. Sense selection:** changing which meaning of an expression is active.

Contributors: Surrounding sentence context; task purpose; speaker usage; a contrast between candidate meanings.

Conditions and distinctions: The locally natural sense may differ from the author's intended technical sense.

**119. Disambiguation:** separating interpretations that had been conflated.

Contributors: Enumerating consequential interpretations; a separating example; explicit qualification; replacement of an ambiguous term.

Conditions and distinctions: A preferred reading does not eliminate another reading still permitted by the expression.

**120. Reference resolution:** identifying what a word or statement refers to.

Contributors: Shared context; pointing or naming; unique descriptions; resolution of pronoun and scope dependencies.

Conditions and distinctions: A referent must be uniquely recoverable enough for the current use; assumed shared knowledge can fail.

**121. Implication recognition:** noticing what an utterance suggests beyond its literal wording.

Contributors: Comparison of literal content with the speaker's situation and purpose; conversational context; plausible alternatives to the chosen wording.

Conditions and distinctions: Implied meaning is defeasible; inference about intent requires more than linguistic plausibility.

**122. Presupposition recognition:** noticing what an utterance takes for granted.

Contributors: Making the assumed background explicit; considering denial or alternative cases; asking what must already hold for the utterance to function.

Conditions and distinctions: Recognizing a presupposition does not establish that the speaker consciously endorsed it.

**123. Pragmatic interpretation:** changing the understood purpose of an utterance.

Contributors: The interaction's purpose; preceding moves; relationship and setting; recipient response; explicit clarification of intended use.

Conditions and distinctions: Identical words can request, warn, joke, or describe; wording alone may underdetermine the act [S18](https://collablab.northwestern.edu/CollabolabDistro/nucmc/ClarkAndBrennan-GroundingInCommunication-1991.pdf).

**124. Translation:** making meaning available across languages or vocabularies.

Contributors: A correspondence between concepts; contextual examples; disambiguation before substitution; reconstruction of idiom and pragmatic role.

Conditions and distinctions: Word-for-word replacement can preserve terms while changing meaning or interpersonal effect.

**125. Literal and figurative interpretation:** changing the role assigned to an expression.

Contributors: Genre and context cues; incompatibility of literal reading; a supported metaphorical mapping; explicit literal clarification.

Conditions and distinctions: A vivid analogy may import properties outside the intended mapping.

**126. Expressive precision:** becoming better able to say what one means.

Contributors: Identifying the exact distinction; choosing discriminating words; adding required qualifications; replacing vague reference with a concrete case.

Conditions and distinctions: More technical terminology is useful only when it preserves something consequential for the recipient.

**15. Relations and organization**

**127. Connection:** relating pieces previously considered separately.

Contributors: Juxtaposing relevant elements; identifying a common entity, relation, or dependency; a question requiring joint consideration.

Conditions and distinctions: Association is not automatically dependence; specify what the connection permits one to infer or do.

**128. Disconnection:** rejecting a relationship previously assumed.

Contributors: A counterexample to the alleged relation; recognition of a common cause; revised scope; independent variation between the elements.

Conditions and distinctions: Failure to detect a relation does not prove absence when the observation is insensitive.

**129. Dependency:** recognizing what requires what.

Contributors: Tracing required inputs; removing a suspected prerequisite in a model or case; identifying what fails when it is absent.

Conditions and distinctions: A habitual predecessor is not necessarily a requirement; alternative routes may satisfy the same need.

**130. Causality:** changing what is understood to produce or influence what.

Contributors: Temporal and mechanistic information; intervention contrasts; separation of common causes; comparison of competing causal accounts.

Conditions and distinctions: Observation alone may leave direction and confounding unresolved; a causal label does not resolve them.

**131. Analogy:** recognizing a transferable relationship across different cases.

Contributors: Aligning relations rather than surface features; mapping corresponding roles; identifying where the mapping fails.

Conditions and distinctions: Analogical comparison supported transfer in specific negotiation tasks [S09](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/GentnerLoewensteinThompson03.pdf); mapped similarity does not prove identical mechanisms.

**132. Composition:** understanding what an arrangement enables together.

Contributors: Connecting outputs to required inputs; specifying interfaces; considering interactions, shared constraints, and failure propagation.

Conditions and distinctions: Useful parts may interfere when combined; composition needs more than a list of components.

**133. Part and whole:** changing the unit through which a matter is understood.

Contributors: Changing the unit represented; relating local behavior to collective organization; exposing part-to-whole dependencies.

Conditions and distinctions: A property of parts need not belong to the whole, or conversely.

**134. Order dependence:** recognizing that sequence changes what is possible.

Contributors: Comparing alternative sequences; exposing prerequisites; identifying irreversible transitions or intermediate states.

Conditions and distinctions: Order matters only through a specified dependence; a preferred sequence is not thereby necessary.

**135. Level distinction:** separating explanations or operations at different scales.

Contributors: Separating implementation, function, individual, interaction, and collective descriptions; stating correspondence between levels.

Conditions and distinctions: Changing vocabulary across levels can leave the same unanswered question concealed.

**136. Constraint relation:** recognizing what limits, permits, or redirects an operation.

Contributors: Identifying a limit or enabling condition; a boundary case; changing a constraint and deriving the resulting option set.

Conditions and distinctions: An assumed constraint may be negotiable; a physically binding one cannot be removed by redescription alone.

**16. Problem formulation**

**137. Problem recognition:** something coming to count as requiring a response.

Contributors: A consequential mismatch between current and wanted conditions; a previously unseen cost; recognition of preventable failure; a newly available standard.

Conditions and distinctions: Discomfort alone does not identify what needs changing; the relevant discrepancy must be specified.

**138. Problem dissolution:** an apparent problem ceasing to require the assumed response.

Contributors: Removal of a false premise; discovery that the desired condition already holds; withdrawal of an unnecessary requirement; a representation that eliminates the apparent conflict.

Conditions and distinctions: Renaming an unresolved difficulty does not dissolve it; show which demand or contradiction disappears.

**139. Target revision:** changing what is actually under examination.

Contributors: A separating case revealing the wrong object of inquiry; a missing downstream result; an explicit correction of the intended contribution.

Conditions and distinctions: Preserve work that remains relevant, while reopening conclusions that depended on the old target.

**140. Scope revision:** changing the boundary of the matter.

Contributors: A dependency outside the current boundary; a case excluded without justification; removal of irrelevant surrounding issues.

Conditions and distinctions: Broader scope can reduce tractability; narrower scope must retain the conditions needed for the intended result.

**141. Unit revision:** changing whether an individual, interaction, or larger system is examined.

Contributors: Evidence that the outcome depends on interaction or organization; comparison of individual and collective explanations; changing the measured entity.

Conditions and distinctions: An individual-level intervention cannot be assumed to alter a collective dependency.

**142. Timescale revision:** changing the interval over which adequacy is considered.

Contributors: Delayed effects; persistence requirements; cumulative costs; recovery or recurrence; a decision whose consequences extend beyond the current interval.

Conditions and distinctions: Immediate relief and later benefit may differ; specify whose outcome and which interval matter.

**143. Constraint revision:** changing what is treated as fixed or alterable.

Contributors: Evidence that a limit is negotiable; an alternative resource or route; a changed requirement; a physical or institutional restriction previously omitted.

Conditions and distinctions: Distinguish genuinely altered conditions from merely imagining their absence.

**144. Attribution revision:** changing where the difficulty is located.

Contributors: A failure trace locating the missing input or operation; comparison across people and situations; evidence against the initial explanation.

Conditions and distinctions: A new blame target is not a causal explanation; identify what difference its alteration would make.

**145. Requirement revision:** changing what would count as resolving the matter.

Contributors: Clarification of what the result must enable; discovery of a missing consumer; removal of an unnecessary completion demand; changed user purpose.

Conditions and distinctions: A weaker requirement can make the work complete without making the original objective achieved.

**146. Problem connection:** recognizing that several apparent problems share a dependency.

Contributors: Tracing several failures to a shared resource, assumption, interface, or criterion; contrasting independent and common causes.

Conditions and distinctions: Common wording does not establish a shared dependency; a joint intervention may leave separate causes intact.

**17. Questions and inquiry**

**147. Question formation:** becoming able to ask a previously unavailable question.

Contributors: A newly available distinction; a contradiction; an unexplained contrast; a goal requiring an undetermined input.

Conditions and distinctions: A grammatically formed question may still have no determinate target or answer condition.

**148. Gap recognition:** identifying what remains undetermined.

Contributors: Comparing available information with what an operation requires; naming an unresolved variable, alternative, relation, or boundary.

Conditions and distinctions: A generic admission of uncertainty does not identify the missing contribution.

**149. Question replacement:** seeing that a different determination is needed.

Contributors: Showing that possible answers would leave the actual decision unchanged; identifying a prerequisite determination; exposing a false presupposition.

Conditions and distinctions: Changing the question must preserve or explicitly revise the underlying purpose.

**150. Question priority:** changing which uncertainty is worth addressing first.

Contributors: The consequence of resolving each uncertainty; dependency order; available discriminating information; cost of delay or inquiry.

Conditions and distinctions: The easiest or most interesting question may have little effect on the current activity.

**151. Answerability:** recognizing what could or could not settle a question.

Contributors: A defined result type; an available observation or derivation that separates alternatives; recognition of inaccessible data or incompatible premises.

Conditions and distinctions: Lack of a current answer is different from impossibility in principle.

**152. Evidence requirement:** changing what information would bear on the inquiry.

Contributors: Competing accounts that predict different observations; identifying relevant units and scope; distinguishing testimony, measurement, and derivation.

Conditions and distinctions: More information is useful only if it bears on the unresolved alternatives.

**153. Investigation direction:** changing where an inquiry should proceed.

Contributors: A newly exposed dependency; a source with the missing information; a derivable consequence; an informative failure of the current route.

Conditions and distinctions: Continue from the unresolved relation, not from the mere availability of another source.

**154. Completion judgment:** changing whether the inquiry is sufficiently resolved.

Contributors: A result satisfying the stated use; elimination or containment of consequential uncertainty; recognition of changed completion criteria.

Conditions and distinctions: Artifact completion is not inquiry completion; residual uncertainty may be acceptable for one use and not another.

**18. Belief and knowledge**

**155. Acquisition:** coming to accept a previously unaccepted proposition.

Contributors: Direct observation; credible testimony; a valid derivation; an interpretation that organizes evidence; repetition or social influence that increases acceptance.

Conditions and distinctions: These routes change acceptance differently; acceptance through familiarity need not add justification.

**156. Rejection:** ceasing to accept a proposition.

Contributors: A decisive counterexample; invalidated support; a stronger incompatible account; recognition that prior acceptance exceeded the grounds.

Conditions and distinctions: Loss of one argument need not establish the proposition's falsity.

**157. Suspension:** withholding judgment where a commitment was previously made.

Contributors: Balanced unresolved alternatives; discovery of unreliable evidence; inability to discriminate at the needed scope; explicit separation of assumption from conclusion.

Conditions and distinctions: Suspension should preserve what remains established rather than erase all distinctions.

**158. Correction:** replacing a mistaken proposition with a better-supported one.

Contributors: Identification of the exact false claim; reliable replacement information; explanation of the error-producing step; revision of dependent conclusions.

Conditions and distinctions: A correction that leaves the original causal account intact may fail to change its later use.

**159. Elaboration:** adding qualified detail to an existing account.

Contributors: Newly warranted conditions, mechanisms, cases, or exceptions; integration with compatible existing knowledge.

Conditions and distinctions: Adding plausible detail without evidence can make an account less accurate while sounding more complete.

**160. Scope restriction:** narrowing where a belief is taken to hold.

Contributors: Counterexamples outside a narrower domain; an identified precondition; evidence that a result depends on population, setting, or task.

Conditions and distinctions: Restriction can preserve a useful local claim; it should not conceal an independently false part.

**161. Scope extension:** extending a claim where support warrants it.

Contributors: Evidence from additional cases; an invariance argument; shared operative conditions; justified transfer across settings.

Conditions and distinctions: Analogy or success in one new case alone may leave broader generalization unresolved.

**162. Inconsistency recognition:** noticing incompatible commitments.

Contributors: Making commitments jointly explicit; aligning definitions, times, and scopes; deriving their incompatible consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: Apparently conflicting sentences may concern different conditions; establish the actual contradiction.

**163. Justification:** gaining or losing grounds for a belief.

Contributors: New or withdrawn evidence; source reliability information; a valid inference; exposure of circularity or an unsupported premise.

Conditions and distinctions: More reasons are not necessarily more independent support.

**164. Knowledge integration:** making a known fact relevant to other commitments.

Contributors: Tracing implications into another judgment; connecting a fact to an active decision; revising dependent expectations or actions.

Conditions and distinctions: A fact can be verbally known while remaining absent from the operation it should govern.

**19. Confidence and uncertainty**

**165. Confidence increase:** becoming more certain of a proposition or judgment.

Contributors: Discriminating evidence; successful relevant performance; convergence of independent sources; repeated familiarity or authoritative presentation.

Conditions and distinctions: The last two can feel persuasive without adding independent grounds; confidence increase alone is not improvement.

**166. Confidence decrease:** becoming less certain of a proposition or judgment.

Contributors: A failed prediction; credible contradiction; exposed missing knowledge; recognition that evidence is dependent or out of scope.

Conditions and distinctions: An irrelevant challenge can lower confidence without improving its correspondence to support.

**167. Calibration:** bringing expressed confidence into better correspondence with support or performance.

Contributors: Comparing prior confidence with resolved outcomes; separating domains and conditions; revising overused cues; distinguishing evidence strength from ease of response.

Conditions and distinctions: Calibration is a pattern across appropriate cases, not one confident or humble statement.

**168. Possibility recognition:** treating an excluded alternative as possible.

Contributors: A coherent witness or construction; removal of an invalid exclusion; a case that satisfies the relevant constraints.

Conditions and distinctions: Conceivability alone does not establish physical, practical, or institutional possibility.

**169. Impossibility recognition:** excluding an alternative for an adequate reason.

Contributors: Contradictory requirements; a proven limit; exhaustive exclusion within a finite defined space; absence of a necessary condition.

Conditions and distinctions: Failure to imagine or find an alternative is not a proof of impossibility.

**170. Probability revision:** changing relative expectations among alternatives.

Contributors: New diagnostic evidence; corrected base rates; information about dependence between observations; revised alternatives or likelihoods.

Conditions and distinctions: A probability shift needs a defined event and reference conditions; vividness does not supply a frequency.

**171. Uncertainty localization:** identifying exactly what is unknown.

Contributors: Decomposing the account into known and unknown parts; identifying competing explanations; naming unavailable measurements or assumptions.

Conditions and distinctions: An uncertainty label must specify what remains open and what consequence depends on it.

**172. Uncertainty tolerance:** becoming more or less able to proceed without closure.

Contributors: A workable action that tolerates several outcomes; bounded exposure to error; available revision or reversal; permission to retain an unresolved judgment.

Conditions and distinctions: Proceeding without closure differs from pretending the uncertainty is gone.

**173. Confidence differentiation:** assigning different certainty to parts of one account.

Contributors: Separating observations, interpretations, forecasts, and value judgments; attaching each to its own grounds.

Conditions and distinctions: A single overall confidence can hide a weak link that determines the conclusion.

**174. Reliance threshold:** changing how much support is required before acting.

Contributors: Consequences of a mistaken action; reversibility; delay cost; alternative information; dependence on the claim.

Conditions and distinctions: Changing the threshold is a decision change, not necessarily a change in the proposition's probability.

**20. Explanation and attribution**

**175. Mechanism understanding:** recognizing how a process produces its effects.

Contributors: Tracing entities, transformations, and intermediate states; a worked causal sequence; comparison with an alternative mechanism.

Conditions and distinctions: Mechanism words without intermediate relations leave the explanatory work undone.

**176. Cause revision:** changing the attributed cause of an event.

Contributors: An intervention contrast; changed temporal or contextual evidence; a competing account explaining the same occurrence; discovery of confounding.

Conditions and distinctions: A better-fitting account may still leave causal identification incomplete.

**177. Multiple causation:** recognizing several contributing conditions.

Contributors: Cases with different combinations of conditions; interaction between contributors; separation of triggers, enabling conditions, and maintaining processes.

Conditions and distinctions: A list of factors does not specify whether they add, interact, substitute, or jointly suffice.

**178. Person and situation:** changing the relative role assigned to traits and circumstances.

Contributors: Comparing the same person across situations and different people in one situation; information about incentives, constraints, and available knowledge.

Conditions and distinctions: Neither trait nor situation should become a universal explanation; interactions may matter.

**179. Intention attribution:** changing what an action is understood to have aimed at.

Contributors: The actor's account; observed choices among alternatives; knowledge of their available information; consistency between stated aims and conduct.

Conditions and distinctions: Intention remains partly uncertain; outcome alone does not reveal what was intended [S12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/).

**180. Controllability attribution:** changing what could have been influenced.

Contributors: Actual intervention opportunities; available resources and authority; time-sensitive alternatives; distinctions between influence and guarantee.

Conditions and distinctions: Hindsight can introduce options unavailable at the time.

**181. Responsibility attribution:** changing who or what is held responsible.

Contributors: Causal contribution; knowledge and foreseeability; obligations and authority; available alternatives; control over the relevant action.

Conditions and distinctions: Causal involvement, moral blame, and assigned duty are different determinations.

**182. Chance attribution:** changing what is treated as accident rather than regularity.

Contributors: A suitable chance model; repeated comparable observations; variation expected under that model; recognition of selection effects.

Conditions and distinctions: An unlikely event alone does not establish a systematic cause or eliminate one.

**183. Explanatory limitation:** recognizing what an account leaves unexplained.

Contributors: A case the explanation cannot distinguish; an omitted variable or transformation; a circular step; a failed scope extension.

Conditions and distinctions: Admitting that an account is incomplete is useful when it identifies the unresolved dependency.

**21. Inference and reasoning**

**184. Inference permission:** recognizing that a conclusion does or does not follow.

Contributors: Explicit premises and inference rules; a countermodel; distinction between necessary and sufficient conditions; corrected quantifier scope.

Conditions and distinctions: A compelling example can illustrate a conclusion without licensing the inference.

**185. Deductive competence:** becoming able to derive a consequence from premises.

Contributors: Worked derivations; performance of individual transformations; correction at the invalid step; practice with structurally different premises.

Conditions and distinctions: Recognizing a valid proof and constructing one require different operations.

**186. Inductive judgment:** changing how examples support a generalization.

Contributors: Representative and contrary examples; sample size and selection information; variability; a specified population of generalization.

Conditions and distinctions: Repeated dependent examples do not contribute like independent representative observations.

**187. Abductive judgment:** changing which explanation best accounts for observations.

Contributors: Alternative mechanisms; their discriminating predictions; fit to all relevant evidence; scope and unsupported assumptions.

Conditions and distinctions: The best available explanation may remain inadequate in absolute terms.

**188. Probabilistic judgment:** changing how uncertain evidence is combined.

Contributors: A defined event space; frequencies with consistent denominators; explicit conditional relationships; a diagram or calculation exposing dependence.

Conditions and distinctions: A convenient representation must preserve the actual probabilities and sampling process.

**189. Causal reasoning:** separating observation, intervention, and causal inference.

Contributors: Distinguishing seeing an event from setting it; mapping common causes; an intervention or justified identification argument.

Conditions and distinctions: Prediction from observed associations can succeed even when the proposed intervention fails.

**190. Counterexample use:** recognizing what a contrary case actually defeats.

Contributors: A contrary case satisfying the claim's antecedent and scope; identification of the precise universal or necessity claim it contradicts.

Conditions and distinctions: A case outside the claim's conditions is not a counterexample to it.

**191. Assumption tracking:** preserving the conditions on which a conclusion depends.

Contributors: An explicit dependency record; tagging provisional premises; carrying conditions into derived statements; reopening conclusions after premise revision.

Conditions and distinctions: A qualification placed elsewhere is ineffective if the dependent conclusion is reused without it.

**192. Consistency maintenance:** reconciling or separating conflicting commitments.

Contributors: Jointly inspecting commitments; distinguishing genuine contradiction from different scopes; retracting or qualifying the unsupported dependency.

Conditions and distinctions: Consistency alone does not establish truth; many mutually consistent accounts may remain possible.

**193. Error correction:** identifying and repairing a mistaken reasoning operation.

Contributors: Locating the mistaken operation; supplying a valid replacement; recomputing dependent steps; preserving independently supported results.

Conditions and distinctions: A corrected final sentence can still be generated by an unchanged faulty method.

<a id="family-22"></a>
**22. Expectation and anticipation**

**194. Outcome expectation:** changing what is expected to happen.

Contributors: Observed outcomes; a causal model; credible advance information; changed resources or constraints; comparison with similar cases.

Conditions and distinctions: Expectation should concern a defined event; wanting or fearing it supplies different information.

**195. Timing expectation:** changing when something is expected.

Contributors: Event schedules; observed timing distributions; elapsed time and stage indicators; a changed dependency on earlier events.

Conditions and distinctions: Average timing can conceal wide variability and conditional delays.

**196. Action dependence:** recognizing how alternatives lead to different possible consequences.

Contributors: Tracing consequences of each available action; identifying branching conditions; comparison with inaction; accounting for others' responses.

Conditions and distinctions: Simulating consequences does not establish their likelihood or feasibility.

**197. Response anticipation:** representing how another person or system may respond.

Contributors: The recipient's actual statements and behavior; response histories under similar conditions; known interface rules; unresolved alternative interpretations.

Conditions and distinctions: Imagining a viewpoint can increase confidence without improving accuracy; direct perspective-getting helped in studied human tasks [S12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/).

**198. Preparation recognition:** seeing what must be available before a future condition.

Contributors: Working backward from a future operation to its required inputs; setup duration; deadlines; an unavailable resource that must be obtained earlier.

Conditions and distinctions: Preparation matters only if its result survives and is accessible when required.

**199. Contingency awareness:** recognizing that different outcomes require different continuations.

Contributors: Distinct outcome branches; observable triggers; a feasible continuation for each consequential branch; a fallback for unresolved cases.

Conditions and distinctions: An enormous scenario list can obscure the few differences that alter the next action.

**200. Surprise:** encountering a mismatch between expectation and occurrence.

Contributors: A specific prior expectation; a perceived deviation; novelty or violation of an inferred regularity.

Conditions and distinctions: Surprise establishes mismatch with an expectation, not necessarily importance or objective improbability.

**201. Expectation separation:** distinguishing hoped-for, feared, possible, and likely outcomes.

Contributors: Separating desire, dread, logical possibility, forecast, and commitment; attaching each to its proper grounds.

Conditions and distinctions: An emotionally vivid simulation can migrate into a forecast unless these roles remain distinct.

**202. Expectation influence:** recognizing that expressing an expectation can affect what follows.

Contributors: A communicated forecast changing attention, effort, coordination, or treatment; awareness of how others respond to the forecast.

Conditions and distinctions: Prediction can be an intervention; a changed outcome may reflect that response rather than the original forecast's accuracy.

<a id="family-23"></a>
**23. Imagination and simulation**

**203. Image construction:** forming a previously unavailable mental scene.

Contributors: Recombining remembered sensory elements; spatial instructions; a concrete scene description; an external image serving as a construction aid.

Conditions and distinctions: People differ in imagery experience; understanding a scene does not require the same subjective image.

**204. Vividness:** making an imagined scene more or less experientially detailed.

Contributors: Adding sensory particulars; specifying viewpoint, movement, and sequence; sustained attention to selected features; reducing or abstracting scene detail.

Conditions and distinctions: Vividness can increase apparent plausibility without changing the scenario's support.

**205. Possibility construction:** conceiving an alternative not previously represented.

Contributors: Combining available elements differently; identifying an overlooked actor or action; relaxing an assumed constraint; introducing a new resource.

Conditions and distinctions: A represented possibility requires a separate feasibility account before reliance.

**206. Counterfactual construction:** representing how things could have been otherwise.

Contributors: Selecting a changeable antecedent; varying it while retaining relevant background conditions; tracing the alternative consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: Changing everything at once prevents attribution of the imagined difference to the selected change.

**207. Perspective taking:** imagining a situation from another position.

Contributors: Information about another position's access, aims, history, and constraints; representing what is visible from that position; checking against the person's account.

Conditions and distinctions: Imagination supplies a candidate model of another mind, not direct access to it [S12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/).

**208. Future simulation:** constructing a possible later situation.

Contributors: A specified future time and situation; present dependencies carried forward; plausible branches; concrete details connecting actions to consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: One detailed future can crowd out other plausible futures.

**209. Recombination:** bringing elements together in an unfamiliar arrangement.

Contributors: A repertoire of components; alternative ways of joining them; cross-domain examples; a purpose that makes an unusual combination consequential.

Conditions and distinctions: Combinations need compatible interfaces; novelty of assembly does not establish usefulness.

**210. Constraint variation:** imagining what changes when a supposed constraint changes.

Contributors: Naming the assumed constraint; replacing its value or status; deriving which options and dependencies change.

Conditions and distinctions: Relaxing one constraint can reveal another; no inference follows from an unspecified 'anything is possible' premise.

**211. Provisional adoption:** using an interpretation without committing to its truth.

Contributors: An explicit temporary assumption; a bounded task in which to use it; preserving the original alternatives; a condition for withdrawal.

Conditions and distinctions: Provisional use becomes misleading if later outputs omit its assumed status.

**212. Simulation control:** changing the ability to enter, alter, or leave an imagined scenario.

Contributors: Entry and exit cues; control over scenario variables; external scene descriptions; an available shift to current sensory or task information.

Conditions and distinctions: Ability to describe control does not establish voluntary control over all imagery or intrusive content.

**24. Creativity and invention**

**213. Idea availability:** gaining access to an idea not previously considered.

Contributors: A cue linked to unused knowledge; a changed representation; exposure to a relevant unfamiliar example; a question exposing a missing relation.

Conditions and distinctions: An idea can be new to this moment while already existing in the person's repertoire.

**214. Fluency:** changing how readily candidate ideas can be produced.

Contributors: Successive prompts along different dimensions; loosening premature selection; access to examples; changing activity or setting.

Conditions and distinctions: Walking increased divergent generation in specific tasks, without uniform gains on convergent tasks [S13](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24749966/).

**215. Diversity:** producing candidates that differ in consequential ways.

Contributors: Varying mechanism, target, resource, sequence, or scope; detecting duplicate assumptions; constructing alternatives with different consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: Different wording is insufficient diversity when every candidate preserves the same operative choice.

**216. Originality:** constructing an idea new relative to a specified prior repertoire.

Contributors: Comparing with the specified prior repertoire; changing a core relation; combining previously unjoined elements; inventing a new operation.

Conditions and distinctions: Originality is relative to a reference set; lack of recognition does not establish historical novelty.

**217. Constraint transformation:** changing the structure within which an invention is sought.

Contributors: Reframing the desired function; replacing a fixed means; changing the unit of design; exploiting a limit as a design feature.

Conditions and distinctions: A changed problem may produce something inventive that no longer serves the original purpose.

**218. Remote connection:** identifying a useful relation between distant matters.

Contributors: A shared relation across distant domains; explicit role mapping; a need in one domain supplied by a mechanism in another.

Conditions and distinctions: Distance alone adds no value; the transferred relation must survive consequential differences [S09](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/GentnerLoewensteinThompson03.pdf).

**219. Elaboration:** developing an initial idea into a more articulated possibility.

Contributors: Supplying missing components, interfaces, conditions, examples, and consequences; resolving underspecified choices.

Conditions and distinctions: Elaboration can expose infeasibility rather than improve the idea; retain that discovery.

**220. Creative selection:** recognizing which idea deserves further development.

Contributors: Comparison against the actual purpose; feasibility constraints; consequences that distinguish candidates; development cost; available evidence.

Conditions and distinctions: An attractive presentation can dominate attention while contributing little to the design criterion.

**221. Creative confidence:** changing willingness to construct or express unfamiliar ideas.

Contributors: A successful act of original construction; a setting permitting unfinished attempts; specific recognition of a useful contribution; separating proposal from endorsement.

Conditions and distinctions: Encouragement without opportunity or skill may raise willingness without improving construction.

**25. Understanding and insight**

**222. Comprehension:** making sense of previously opaque material.

Contributors: Resolving unknown terms; a concrete example; showing the governing relation; supplying a missing prerequisite; reducing irrelevant representational load.

Conditions and distinctions: Smooth reading is insufficient evidence of comprehension; the needed use determines what must become available.

**223. Integration:** understanding how formerly separate pieces fit together.

Contributors: Mapping correspondences between pieces; resolving incompatible assumptions; showing their shared dependency; constructing a combined example.

Conditions and distinctions: Combining statements without reconciling their conditions can create superficial coherence.

**224. Consequence access:** becoming able to derive what an account implies.

Contributors: Making premises available; performing the missing inference; a contrast revealing what changes downstream; carrying qualifications into the result.

Conditions and distinctions: Restating the premise leaves consequence access unchanged when the recipient already knows it.

**225. Operational understanding:** becoming able to use an account in an activity.

Contributors: A worked application; executable steps; required resources; an attempt on a relevant case; correction at the failed operation.

Conditions and distinctions: A description of use does not supply every motor, social, or cognitive ability needed to perform it.

**226. Boundary understanding:** recognizing when an account does not apply.

Contributors: An inapplicable near neighbor; a violated precondition; an exception; comparison of successful and unsuccessful uses.

Conditions and distinctions: Knowing an example's failure does not identify the boundary unless the relevant difference is isolated.

**227. Insight:** reorganizing a matter so a previously inaccessible relationship becomes apparent.

Contributors: A representation exposing an overlooked relation; abandoning a blocking assumption; joining previously separated information; a separating case.

Conditions and distinctions: The felt suddenness of insight does not establish accuracy or explain how the reorganization occurred.

**228. Illusion of understanding:** becoming confident in understanding without the corresponding ability.

Contributors: Fluent explanations; familiar terms; repeated exposure; a persuasive narrative; absence of a demand to reconstruct missing steps.

Conditions and distinctions: These are possible sources of misplaced confidence; feeling able to explain can exceed actual explanatory performance [S14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21442007/).

**229. Understanding correction:** recognizing and repairing that mismatch.

Contributors: Attempting the required explanation or operation; locating the missing step; supplying its relation or input; reconstructing the account afterward.

Conditions and distinctions: Exposing a gap reduces an illusion only if the person recognizes the mismatch; repair is additional work [S14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21442007/).

**230. Explanatory independence:** becoming able to reconstruct an understanding without repeating its wording.

Contributors: Rebuilding from premises; expressing the relation in another form; producing a fresh example; answering a novel consequence question.

Conditions and distinctions: Paraphrase alone may preserve memorized wording without independent reconstruction.

**26. Values and concerns**

**231. Concern formation:** something coming to matter.

Contributors: Encountering a consequential need or possibility; an emotionally meaningful experience; recognizing effects on someone valued; adopting a commitment.

Conditions and distinctions: Attention to a concern and durable valuation differ; a vivid episode may have temporary influence.

**232. Concern reduction:** something ceasing to matter as much.

Contributors: Resolution of a need; changed relevance; revised interpretation of the cost; competing concerns; repeated disappointment or disengagement.

Conditions and distinctions: Reduced concern can reflect relief, informed reprioritization, or loss of responsiveness; these require different interpretation.

**233. Value priority:** changing which concern takes precedence.

Contributors: A concrete conflict between concerns; changed consequences; reflection on accepted sacrifices; a commitment that orders competing demands.

Conditions and distinctions: Stated priority may differ from choices under cost; neither automatically captures a context-free permanent value.

**234. Intrinsic valuation:** changing what is valued for its own sake.

Contributors: Direct experience of the activity or object; appreciation of its qualities; identification with its practice; changing its felt meaning.

Conditions and distinctions: Removing external rewards does not by itself establish that intrinsic valuation will increase.

**235. Instrumental valuation:** changing what is valued for what it enables.

Contributors: A newly visible means-end relation; changed effectiveness or cost; an alternative serving the same end; loss of the original goal.

Conditions and distinctions: Instrumental value can disappear when its dependency disappears while other kinds of value remain.

**236. Concern scope:** extending or narrowing who or what counts.

Contributors: Attention to previously excluded people, entities, consequences, or future periods; recognition of shared features; revised inclusion criteria.

Conditions and distinctions: A widened description does not prove that the included concerns now govern choices.

**237. Value conflict recognition:** noticing incompatible or competing concerns.

Contributors: Presenting incompatible requirements together; revealing a shared scarce resource; showing that one action advances one concern while obstructing another.

Conditions and distinctions: A conflict may be contingent on the available options rather than inherent in the values.

**238. Value reconciliation:** finding a way to relate concerns without the former conflict.

Contributors: Separating means from ends; finding a compatible alternative; allocating by circumstance or time; revising an unnecessary requirement.

Conditions and distinctions: Some conflicts remain real; reconciliation cannot be established by renaming a sacrifice.

**239. Value enactment:** changing whether acknowledged values govern actual choices.

Contributors: A decision rule expressing the value; point-of-choice access to its consequence; resources and authority to act; removal of conflicting defaults.

Conditions and distinctions: Changes in enactment may arise without changes in underlying valuation.

**27. Preferences and taste**

**240. Preference formation:** developing a preference where none was settled.

Contributors: Experience with alternatives; comparison on newly noticed qualities; social examples; a choice that reveals a previously unspecified concern.

Conditions and distinctions: A selection can reflect availability, framing, or compromise rather than a stable ranking.

**241. Preference reversal:** changing the ranking of alternatives.

Contributors: Changed consequences or context; new information; altered reference points; satiation; a different representation of the same options.

Conditions and distinctions: Presentation alone can reverse choices in studied framing problems [S11](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/surveys.course/TverskyKahneman1981.pdf); reversal need not establish changed underlying interests.

**242. Attraction:** becoming more or less drawn to an object or activity.

Contributors: Appealing sensory properties; anticipated reward; familiarity or novelty; meaningful association; an opportunity aligned with current need.

Conditions and distinctions: Attraction can change independently of considered endorsement or actual benefit.

**243. Aversion:** becoming more or less repelled by it.

Contributors: Unpleasant experience; anticipated harm or effort; learned associations; conflicting values; repeated unwanted exposure.

Conditions and distinctions: Avoidance can preserve an aversion by preventing relevant corrective experience, but not every aversion is mistaken.

**244. Taste discrimination:** noticing distinctions that change appreciation.

Contributors: Contrasting similar works or experiences; vocabulary for consequential features; directed attention to composition, technique, or texture.

Conditions and distinctions: Learning to describe differences does not prescribe which should be liked.

**245. Aesthetic appreciation:** coming to perceive beauty, elegance, ugliness, or expressive force.

Contributors: Encountering form, contrast, rhythm, or expressive relation; contextual knowledge; a newly available interpretation; sustained perceptual attention.

Conditions and distinctions: Appreciation is recipient- and context-dependent; expertise need not converge on one ranking.

**246. Familiarity preference:** changing attraction associated with familiarity.

Contributors: Repeated encounters; easier identification and processing; positive associations; reduced uncertainty about the object.

Conditions and distinctions: Familiarity may become boredom or aversion; repeated exposure is not universally attractive.

**247. Novelty preference:** changing attraction associated with unfamiliarity.

Contributors: A contrast with a saturated repertoire; uncertainty promising discovery; lower expected risk; access to an unfamiliar but interpretable experience.

Conditions and distinctions: Novelty can also impose confusion or threat; its value depends on the current concern and capacity.

**248. Preference independence:** becoming less dependent on another person's stated preference.

Contributors: Firsthand examination; forming criteria before seeing others' ratings; separate judgments before discussion; identifying conformity pressure.

Conditions and distinctions: Independence need not mean rejecting useful expertise; the question is what grounds the preference.

**28. Reward, cost, and trade-offs**

**249. Wanting:** changing the pull of an anticipated object or outcome.

Contributors: Reward-predicting cues; current deprivation or need; opportunity proximity; imagery of attainment; learned incentive associations.

Conditions and distinctions: Wanting can persist without enjoyment; desire intensity is not a measure of benefit.

**250. Liking:** changing enjoyment during an experience.

Contributors: The consumed or encountered experience; sensory quality; satiation; current bodily state; attention to pleasant or unpleasant features.

Conditions and distinctions: Anticipated pleasure and experienced pleasure can differ; observation of pursuit does not establish liking.

**251. Anticipated reward:** changing the expected benefit of an activity.

Contributors: Outcome information; remembered experience; a credible path to attainment; revised probability, magnitude, or timing of the benefit.

Conditions and distinctions: A more vivid reward may feel larger without becoming more likely or valuable.

**252. Satisfaction:** changing how fulfilled or disappointed an outcome feels.

Contributors: Comparison of outcome with expectations and requirements; attention to what was obtained or missed; satiation; changing comparison standards.

Conditions and distinctions: Lowering expectations can alter satisfaction while leaving the actual outcome unchanged.

**253. Effort valuation:** changing how burdensome effort seems relative to its benefit.

Contributors: Task complexity and friction; visible progress; expected payoff; available support; whether effort itself is valued or resented.

Conditions and distinctions: Reducing effort can improve access, while removing valued challenge can reduce engagement.

**254. Delay valuation:** changing the relative weight of earlier and later consequences.

Contributors: Making later consequences concrete; credible delivery expectations; a connection to one's future situation; immediate need; alternative uses of resources.

Conditions and distinctions: A vivid future is one possible contributor; laboratory allocation changes do not establish enduring real-world benefit [S23](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/increasing-saving-behavior-through-age-progressed-renderings-future-self).

**255. Risk valuation:** changing how uncertain gains and losses are weighed.

Contributors: Information about probabilities and stakes; gain/loss framing; reference points; ability to absorb adverse outcomes.

Conditions and distinctions: Equivalent descriptions can produce different choices [S11](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/surveys.course/TverskyKahneman1981.pdf); a preferred frame is not automatically a better valuation.

**256. Opportunity cost:** recognizing what another commitment displaces.

Contributors: Making the displaced alternative explicit; a common resource budget; the time or attention occupied by the current commitment.

Conditions and distinctions: Only feasible alternatives belong in the immediate opportunity-cost comparison.

**257. Trade-off recognition:** seeing what must be given up to obtain something else.

Contributors: A comparison exposing incompatible gains; identifying a binding resource; varying one feature while tracking the other consequence.

Conditions and distinctions: A trade-off may disappear with a new design; do not assume the current option set is final.

**258. Sufficiency judgment:** changing when something counts as enough.

Contributors: An explicit requirement or saturation point; diminishing additional benefit; displaced opportunities; completion evidence.

Conditions and distinctions: Enough for the stated use and globally optimal are different judgments.

**29. Motivation and willingness**

**259. Desire:** developing, strengthening, weakening, or losing a want.

Contributors: A newly salient valued outcome; current need; rewarding experience or anticipation; changed associations; loss of appeal through satiation.

Conditions and distinctions: A want can strengthen without becoming a considered goal or a feasible action.

**260. Willingness:** becoming more or less prepared to undertake an activity.

Contributors: An acceptable expected consequence; reduced initiation cost; available ability and support; meaningful choice; tolerable exposure to failure.

Conditions and distinctions: Someone may value an outcome while declining the proposed means for sound reasons.

**261. Approach:** becoming more inclined to move toward a possibility.

Contributors: A visible attainable benefit; an available first move; lower perceived threat; prior rewarding contact with the possibility.

Conditions and distinctions: Approach tendency does not establish that the option serves longer-term interests.

**262. Avoidance:** becoming more inclined to move away from it.

Contributors: Expected harm, loss, humiliation, effort, or incompatibility; learned warning cues; an available exit or alternative.

Conditions and distinctions: Avoidance can be protective or unnecessarily restrictive; identify the actual predicted consequence.

**263. Effort mobilization:** becoming ready to allocate more or less effort.

Contributors: A worthwhile and attainable demand; explicit effort requirements; immediate resources; visible progress; removal of competing commitments.

Conditions and distinctions: More activation cannot compensate for an impossible task or an unavailable prerequisite.

**264. Initiative:** becoming more or less likely to originate an activity.

Contributors: Recognition of an unfilled need; authority to act; ownership of a next step; an accessible starting point; expected consequence of inaction.

Conditions and distinctions: Initiative can be blocked by unclear permission or resources even when motivation is strong.

**265. Commitment to effort:** changing willingness to work despite difficulty.

Contributors: A sustained reason for the work; credible progress; manageable stages; expected value of continued effort; protection from competing demands.

Conditions and distinctions: Continuing through difficulty is beneficial only while the activity still serves its purpose.

**266. Motivational conflict:** changing the competition among incompatible inclinations.

Contributors: Making competing wants explicit; separating immediate and later consequences; finding a compatible alternative; choosing a governing priority.

Conditions and distinctions: A declared priority need not extinguish the competing inclination.

**267. Interest maintenance:** sustaining or losing engagement with an activity.

Contributors: Successive meaningful questions or challenges; increasing capability; variation that retains purpose; access to consequential progress.

Conditions and distinctions: Constant novelty can disrupt cumulative understanding rather than sustain useful interest.

**268. Reason uptake:** a recognized reason beginning or ceasing to affect motivation.

Contributors: Connecting the reason to an actual choice; making its consequence concrete; resolving an incompatible commitment; providing a feasible response.

Conditions and distinctions: Agreement with a reason does not ensure it becomes operative under pressure or distraction.

**30. Curiosity and exploration**

**269. Interest emergence:** a matter becoming worth investigating.

Contributors: An unexplained contrast; a personally relevant consequence; a surprising example; recognition of an accessible but missing relation.

Conditions and distinctions: A question must be intelligible enough to engage; total opacity may produce disengagement.

**270. Question appetite:** wanting to resolve a particular uncertainty.

Contributors: A defined gap between known and wanted information; a plausible route to an answer; anticipated value or enjoyment of discovery.

Conditions and distinctions: Resolving uncertainty can be unhelpful when the information has no relevant use and displaces more important work.

**271. Exploration breadth:** widening or narrowing what one is willing to examine.

Contributors: Distinct exploratory dimensions; access to unfamiliar examples; lower cost of sampling; a purpose requiring wider coverage.

Conditions and distinctions: Breadth without consequential differentiation can produce repetition in disguise.

**272. Novelty approach:** becoming more willing to encounter unfamiliar material.

Contributors: A manageable introduction; optional participation; connections to familiar knowledge; visible potential benefit; credible control over exposure.

Conditions and distinctions: Unfamiliarity can remain costly or unwanted; novelty is not an intrinsic improvement criterion.

**273. Ambiguity engagement:** becoming willing to remain with an unresolved matter.

Contributors: A meaningful unresolved question; partial structure; permission to defer commitment; a way to preserve progress while uncertainty remains.

Conditions and distinctions: Remaining engaged need not mean repeatedly revisiting the same unproductive operation.

**274. Playfulness:** entering an activity with freedom to vary its possibilities.

Contributors: Reversible variations; low penalties for imperfect attempts; flexible rules; materials inviting manipulation; separation of exploration from final commitment.

Conditions and distinctions: Play can reveal possibilities without establishing that they work outside the play conditions.

**275. Incidental pursuit:** following an unexpected connection.

Contributors: Recognition that an unexpected connection could supply a missing contribution; a recoverable record of the original path; available exploration time.

Conditions and distinctions: An interesting tangent needs a plausible relation to the activity or a consciously revised purpose.

**276. Exploration cessation:** deciding that further exploration is no longer worthwhile.

Contributors: No remaining consequential difference among outcomes; declining additional value; a binding resource cost; sufficient material for the next operation.

Conditions and distinctions: Stopping search does not prove exhaustive coverage; retain the scope of what was examined.

**31. Emotion**

**277. Joy:** changes in experienced delight or happiness.

Contributors: A welcomed event or encounter; achievement of a valued aim; playful or rewarding experience; attention to an appreciated feature.

Conditions and distinctions: The same event can evoke different feelings depending on its personal meaning and current state.

**278. Sadness and grief:** changes in felt loss or sorrow.

Contributors: Loss or recognition of absence; reminders of attachment; interpretation of what has ended; opportunities to express and share the experience.

Conditions and distinctions: Grief is not simply an erroneous belief to remove; changed expression and changed felt loss may diverge.

**279. Fear:** changes in experienced threat or apprehension.

Contributors: A perceived threat; uncertainty about harm; learned warning cues; absence or presence of control, protection, and escape.

Conditions and distinctions: Fear can be warranted or disproportionate; reassurance changes it only through a relation the person can use.

**280. Anger:** changes in felt grievance and readiness to oppose.

Contributors: Perceived obstruction, injustice, disrespect, or intentional harm; attribution of responsibility; available means of redress.

Conditions and distinctions: Changing blame can change anger without resolving the actual obstruction or injustice.

**281. Disgust:** changes in felt revulsion.

Contributors: An aversive sensory encounter; contamination cues; learned associations; an interpretation of the object or event.

Conditions and distinctions: Reappraisal and suppression had different experiential and physiological effects in a disgust-film experiment [S17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9457784/).

**282. Shame:** changes in feeling exposed or diminished before oneself or others.

Contributors: Perceived global self-failure; anticipated or actual exposure to devaluation; identification with a condemning judgment; acceptance or rejection by valued others.

Conditions and distinctions: Separating a specific act from total self-worth is a candidate contribution, not a guaranteed emotional effect.

**283. Guilt:** changes in distress about one's attributed wrongdoing.

Contributors: Attributing a harmful act or omission to oneself; recognizing a violated commitment; new evidence about responsibility; possible repair.

Conditions and distinctions: Relieving guilt by changing attribution is appropriate only if the attribution was mistaken or incomplete.

**284. Pride:** changes in satisfaction or esteem concerning oneself or an affiliation.

Contributors: A valued achievement; attribution of contribution to oneself or a group; recognition by a respected audience; comparison with a meaningful standard.

Conditions and distinctions: Pride can rest on accurate contribution, inflated attribution, or a borrowed group achievement.

**285. Envy and jealousy:** changes in feelings concerning another's advantage or a threatened relationship.

Contributors: Comparison with another's advantage; a perceived threat to a valued relationship; uncertainty about inclusion; revised information about loss or exclusivity.

Conditions and distinctions: Envy and jealousy have different objects; neither licenses assumptions about another person's intentions.

**286. Gratitude:** changes in felt appreciation for a received benefit.

Contributors: Recognizing a received benefit; attributing it to another's contribution or care; making its personal significance available.

Conditions and distinctions: Demanding gratitude can add pressure or resentment; benefit and benevolent intent are separate questions.

**287. Compassion:** changes in concern for another's suffering.

Contributors: Recognizing suffering; understanding its circumstances; perceived connection to the affected person; an available way to help.

Conditions and distinctions: Emotional resonance, concern, and effective assistance can vary independently.

**288. Amusement:** changes in experiencing something as funny or playful.

Contributors: An unexpected but interpretable incongruity; playful framing; timing and shared context; recognition of a benign alternative meaning.

Conditions and distinctions: A mismatch can be amusing to one person and threatening or tedious to another.

**289. Awe:** changes in response to something experienced as vast or beyond the familiar.

Contributors: Encountering perceived vastness, complexity, or extraordinary capability; attention to scale; an experience exceeding the current interpretive frame.

Conditions and distinctions: Awe can coexist with fear and need not improve the accuracy of subsequent judgments.

**290. Relief:** changes in feeling that an anticipated burden or threat has eased.

Contributors: Evidence that an anticipated threat or burden has diminished; release from an obligation; completion of a difficult requirement.

Conditions and distinctions: Relief can arise from mistaken reassurance; distinguish felt release from actual resolution.

**291. Hope and despair:** changes in emotional orientation toward future possibility.

Contributors: A credible route toward or away from a valued possibility; perceived control; support; evidence about remaining options.

Conditions and distinctions: Hope need not require certainty, and severe pessimism does not prove that no route exists.

**292. Frustration:** changes in felt obstruction of an attempted activity.

Contributors: A blocked intended operation; repeated unsuccessful effort; unclear requirements; removal or explanation of the obstruction.

Conditions and distinctions: An explanation can reduce uncertainty while leaving the practical blockage intact.

**293. Regret:** changes in feelings concerning an unrealized alternative.

Contributors: A salient unrealized alternative; belief that it was available; comparison of outcomes; revised understanding of what could have been known.

Conditions and distinctions: Hindsight can create regret for a choice whose better alternative was not accessible then.

**294. Mixed emotion:** changes in the coexistence and balance of multiple emotions.

Contributors: Simultaneous consequences for different concerns; different interpretations of one event; attention moving among gains, losses, attachment, and conflict.

Conditions and distinctions: One emotional label may conceal meaningful coexistence rather than indecision or confusion.

**295. Love:** changes in felt attachment, care, or devotion.

Contributors: Repeated caring interaction; attachment and shared history; responsive attention; valued knowledge of the other; changing trust or separation.

Conditions and distinctions: Love includes distinct experiences and commitments; no message or sequence guarantees it.

**296. Admiration:** changes in regard for another's qualities or actions.

Contributors: Perception of a valued quality in action; understanding the difficulty or contribution involved; comparison with relevant alternatives.

Conditions and distinctions: Prestige or impressive presentation can imitate evidence of an admirable quality.

**297. Embarrassment:** changes in discomfort concerning a socially exposed mishap.

Contributors: An exposed mismatch with a social expectation; perceived audience attention; interpretation of the mishap; a response that restores workable participation.

Conditions and distinctions: Actual audience judgment may differ from imagined judgment; public reassurance can sometimes prolong exposure.

**298. Contempt:** changes in regarding someone or something as beneath consideration.

Contributors: Attributing serious inferiority or norm violation; distancing from the target; dehumanizing or dismissive categorization; revised information about the person or act.

Conditions and distinctions: Understanding a cause does not require endorsing contempt; global dismissal can conceal relevant distinctions.

**299. Boredom:** changes in feeling insufficiently engaged by available activity.

Contributors: Insufficient challenge or meaning; repetitive stimulation; inability to engage an available activity; mismatch between demand and capacity.

Conditions and distinctions: Adding stimulation can leave the missing purpose or inaccessible task unchanged.

**300. Longing:** changes in felt desire for an absent person, condition, or possibility.

Contributors: Salient absence of a valued person or condition; memory and imagination of contact; perceived distance from attainment; changes in accessibility.

Conditions and distinctions: Fulfillment, acceptance of absence, and replacement of the concern are different possible changes.

**32. Mood and emotional dynamics**

**301. Background mood:** changes in the prevailing affective tone.

Contributors: Accumulated daily events; recurring interpretations; sustained bodily state; social environment; sleep and activity patterns.

Conditions and distinctions: A prevailing mood may have multiple maintaining conditions; one conversational event need not reset it.

**302. Valence:** becoming generally more pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral in feeling.

Contributors: Pleasant or unpleasant sensory input; appraised gains and losses; current bodily comfort; emotionally meaningful interaction.

Conditions and distinctions: General pleasantness can change while a specific emotion or unresolved concern persists.

**303. Irritability:** changing readiness to react with annoyance.

Contributors: Repeated interruption or obstruction; fatigue; discomfort; perceived disrespect; reduced room to recover between demands.

Conditions and distinctions: The contributor may be environmental or bodily rather than a defective attitude.

**304. Emotional intensity:** feelings becoming stronger or weaker.

Contributors: Perceived magnitude and immediacy of consequences; attention to evocative details; physiological activation; reinterpretation or changed circumstances.

Conditions and distinctions: Stronger expression does not necessarily indicate stronger felt intensity.

**305. Emotional duration:** feelings lasting longer or shorter.

Contributors: Continued provoking events; repeated recollection or simulation; unfinished action tendencies; new competing experiences; recognition of an ending.

Conditions and distinctions: Duration can change without changing the initial intensity or the underlying concern.

**306. Emotional variability:** feelings changing more or less readily over time.

Contributors: Rapidly changing events or appraisals; shifts of attention; competing concerns; bodily state; repeated social feedback.

Conditions and distinctions: Variability needs an observation interval; a single transition does not characterize a lasting disposition.

**307. Recovery from emotion:** returning toward a prior state after an event.

Contributors: Ending the precipitating condition; obtaining needed resolution or support; disengaging from repeated activation; restoring ordinary activity.

Conditions and distinctions: Recovery can involve a changed baseline rather than a return to the exact prior state.

**308. Emotional differentiation:** experiencing feelings as more distinct or undifferentiated.

Contributors: Comparing nearby feelings; attending to distinct bodily, evaluative, and action components; a usable emotional vocabulary; context-specific reflection.

Conditions and distinctions: Vocabulary can become more differentiated without proving equally differentiated experience.

**309. Emotional numbness:** becoming more or less responsive in felt experience.

Contributors: Changes in prolonged overload, disconnection, or bodily state; renewed contact with personally meaningful experiences; changes in attention and appraisal.

Conditions and distinctions: This label covers different mechanisms; the inventory does not diagnose its cause or prescribe treatment.

<a id="family-33"></a>
**33. Emotion regulation**

**310. Emotion recognition:** noticing what one is feeling.

Contributors: Attention to current bodily sensations, appraisals, and action urges; comparison with familiar episodes; a prompt naming an overlooked component.

Conditions and distinctions: Recognizing an emotion is an interpretation that can remain uncertain.

**311. Emotion labeling:** becoming able to name or distinguish a feeling.

Contributors: Words linked to distinguishable experiences; contrast between nearby emotions; descriptions of the event's meaning and action tendency.

Conditions and distinctions: A label can organize experience or constrain it inaccurately; preserve mixed and uncertain cases.

**312. Acceptance:** changing willingness to experience a feeling without immediately escaping it.

Contributors: Permission for the feeling to occur; distinction between feeling and required action; reduced evaluation of the feeling as a failure; tolerable contact with it.

Conditions and distinctions: Acceptance is neither endorsement of a harmful situation nor a requirement to endure every experience.

**313. Reappraisal:** changing an interpretation in a way that alters emotional response.

Contributors: A credible alternative meaning; new contextual information; changed attribution; a different comparison or timescale.

Conditions and distinctions: Reappraisal can alter felt response in studied conditions [S17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9457784/); an implausible reinterpretation need not be usable.

**314. Expression control:** changing whether and how an emotion is displayed.

Contributors: Deliberate changes in facial, verbal, or bodily expression; social display rules; a chosen communication purpose.

Conditions and distinctions: Suppressing expression need not reduce feeling and can have different physiological consequences [S17](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9457784/).

**315. Attention regulation:** changing which emotional material receives continued attention.

Contributors: Shifting toward or away from evocative details; limiting repeated cues; returning to the current task; deliberately examining an unresolved emotional issue.

Conditions and distinctions: Distraction may provide temporary room without resolving the concern; repeated focus can clarify or prolong it.

**316. Situation selection:** changing encounters to alter likely emotional experience.

Contributors: Entering, leaving, or changing an encounter; adjusting audience, timing, or exposure; choosing a setting compatible with current needs.

Conditions and distinctions: Avoiding every difficult encounter can also remove valued opportunities; relevance depends on the actual consequence.

**317. Response interruption:** interrupting an established emotional reaction.

Contributors: Recognizing the early response cue; pausing the next action; changing posture, location, or conversational move; introducing a previously unavailable alternative.

Conditions and distinctions: Interrupting the behavioral sequence does not establish that the emotion has ended.

**318. Co-regulation:** an interaction changing the course of another's emotional state.

Contributors: Attentive listening; responsive acknowledgment; practical protection or assistance; pacing that fits the person; shared interpretation of the event.

Conditions and distinctions: Being heard had context-dependent effects in intergroup studies [S21](https://ombuds.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/content/pics/30%20Anniv/The%20power%20of%20being%20heard%20-%20Bruneau.pdf); intended comfort does not establish actual comfort.

**319. Regulation flexibility:** becoming able to use a different response when the familiar one is unhelpful.

Contributors: A repertoire of distinct regulation operations; recognition of what the current operation changes; access to another response when conditions differ.

Conditions and distinctions: Flexibility requires relevant selection, not continual switching or a claim that one strategy is always best.

<a id="family-34"></a>
**34. Agency and efficacy**

**320. Agency experience:** changing the sense of originating or directing an action.

Contributors: Correspondence between intention, action, and observed consequence; a meaningful choice; feedback identifying one's contribution; control over execution.

Conditions and distinctions: Felt authorship and actual causal influence can diverge.

**321. Perceived control:** changing what one experiences as influenceable.

Contributors: An action whose effects are visible; information about genuine limits; reliable access to tools, resources, or authority; removal of arbitrary constraints.

Conditions and distinctions: Control over one variable does not imply control over the whole outcome.

**322. Self-efficacy:** changing confidence in performing a particular activity.

Contributors: Successful performance of the relevant operation; observing a comparable capable actor; specific corrective information; an attainable next challenge.

Conditions and distinctions: Mastery-based and vicarious interventions differed in a particular fear-related study [S19](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15093/); generic praise does not establish capability.

**323. Helplessness:** changing the expectation or experience that one's action makes no difference.

Contributors: Repeated action-outcome independence; obstructed access to effective responses; discovery and use of an action that makes a consequential difference.

Conditions and distinctions: Being told one has control is insufficient when the actual environment remains uncontrollable.

**324. Authorship:** changing ownership of a choice or contribution.

Contributors: Making one's contribution identifiable; real discretion over a choice; acknowledgment of who created which part; separating assistance from substitution.

Conditions and distinctions: Receiving credit and experiencing ownership need not correspond to actual authorship.

**325. Initiation authority:** becoming more or less willing to act without external prompting.

Contributors: Clear authority; an unambiguous starting condition; scope for independent choice; prior experience acting without punishment or unnecessary permission.

Conditions and distinctions: Some actions require genuine coordination or authorization; initiative must fit that scope.

**326. Responsibility acceptance:** taking up or relinquishing responsibility appropriately or inappropriately.

Contributors: A clear duty or voluntarily adopted commitment; understanding consequences; adequate control and resources; correction of misplaced blame.

Conditions and distinctions: Taking responsibility for an uncontrollable outcome can reduce rather than increase effective agency.

**327. Choice recognition:** becoming aware of alternatives where none seemed available.

Contributors: Exposing overlooked feasible actions; removing a false necessity; distinguishing means from ends; identifying a reversible intermediate step.

Conditions and distinctions: A nominal option is not usable if material, social, or cognitive conditions prohibit it.

**328. Dependency recognition:** identifying which assistance is necessary and which has become habitual.

Contributors: Tracing which operation assistance actually supplies; attempting independent components; distinguishing missing capability from familiar delegation.

Conditions and distinctions: Independence is not always beneficial; necessary support can be part of a capable system.

**35. Permission and inhibition**

**329. Permission to consider:** allowing an idea to enter examination.

Contributors: Separating examination from endorsement; a provisional frame; permission to preserve current commitments during comparison; reduced penalty for entertaining an idea.

Conditions and distinctions: Considering a possibility does not require accepting its truth or desirability.

**330. Permission to disagree:** becoming able to oppose a claim or authority.

Contributors: Recognizing a defensible conflicting judgment; an available expression of it; explicit room for dissent; reduced threat of retaliation.

Conditions and distinctions: Verbal permission alone may be ineffective where actual power consequences remain.

**331. Permission to attempt:** becoming willing to try without assured success.

Contributors: A bounded attempt; a recoverable failure; an attainable first action; separation of trying from promising success.

Conditions and distinctions: Permission cannot supply missing resources or erase genuine consequences.

**332. Permission to stop:** becoming able to end an activity or commitment.

Contributors: A legitimate ending criterion; recognition of sunk costs; release from an unnecessary obligation; a preserved route for later return.

Conditions and distinctions: Stopping one method need not abandon the concern it was intended to serve.

**333. Permission to revise:** allowing an earlier position to be replaced.

Contributors: A specific reason for revision; separation of accuracy from personal worth; preserving valid prior work; social acceptance of warranted correction.

Conditions and distinctions: Allowing revision is distinct from establishing which replacement is better.

**334. Expression inhibition:** becoming more or less able to speak or show a response.

Contributors: An available phrase or expressive movement; changes in anticipated evaluation; a chosen display rule; a private or supportive setting.

Conditions and distinctions: Silence may reflect deliberate judgment rather than inability or fear.

**335. Impulse inhibition:** changing the ability to withhold an immediately available action.

Contributors: An early stop cue; time before execution; removal of a triggering opportunity; an alternative response linked to the same cue.

Conditions and distinctions: Withholding one action is easier to specify than suppressing every associated thought or feeling.

**336. Internal prohibition:** acquiring, relaxing, or rejecting a self-imposed restriction.

Contributors: A rule's stated basis; an exception revealing overbreadth; changes in values or circumstances; explicit permission conditioned on consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: Rejecting a restriction without identifying its original function can remove needed protection or coordination.

**337. Social inhibition:** changing restraint associated with anticipated evaluation.

Contributors: Actual audience response; anonymity or privacy; familiarity with the setting; clear participation norms; reduced evaluative ambiguity.

Conditions and distinctions: Anticipated judgment may be inaccurate, but real social risk cannot be wished away.

**338. Deliberate restraint:** choosing to withhold an action while preserving the ability to perform it.

Contributors: A reason to defer; a selected later condition for action; control over timing; an alternative serving the same concern.

Conditions and distinctions: Restraint preserves the available action; inability to act is a different state.

**36. Goals and intentions**

**339. Goal formation:** adopting an outcome to pursue.

Contributors: A valued possible outcome; an explicit adoption decision; a credible connection to an existing concern; circumstances making pursuit worthwhile.

Conditions and distinctions: Desiring an outcome and committing to organize activity around it are distinct.

**340. Goal abandonment:** ceasing to pursue a prior outcome.

Contributors: Loss of relevance; infeasibility; excessive opportunity cost; a superior way to serve the underlying concern; changed commitments.

Conditions and distinctions: Abandoning a means should not accidentally discard an independently valued end.

**341. Goal priority:** changing the order among competing goals.

Contributors: Consequences of delay or conflict; prerequisite relationships; scarce resources; explicit precedence among commitments.

Conditions and distinctions: Ranking goals abstractly may fail to resolve the particular next allocation.

**342. Goal specification:** making a vague target more determinate.

Contributors: Concrete achievement cases; observable completion conditions; scope, beneficiary, and timeframe; a case that looks successful while missing the purpose.

Conditions and distinctions: A measurable substitute can make specification easier while changing the actual goal.

**343. Goal broadening:** recognizing a more encompassing concern.

Contributors: Identifying the concern served by several narrower aims; including neglected beneficiaries or downstream consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: A broader goal needs a usable relation to decisions; breadth alone can dissolve actionable distinctions.

**344. Goal substitution:** adopting a different way of expressing the concern.

Contributors: An alternative aim serving the same concern; a changed means-end relation; discovery that the original formulation excludes a better route.

Conditions and distinctions: Preserve which end remains stable and which commitment actually changed.

**345. Means-end distinction:** recognizing that an apparent goal serves another purpose.

Contributors: Asking what achieving the apparent goal enables; varying the means while retaining the consequence; a case where the means succeeds but the purpose fails.

Conditions and distinctions: A means can also have intrinsic value; the distinction need not eliminate either role.

**346. Intention formation:** resolving to perform an action.

Contributors: Selecting a specific act; resolving incompatible choices; accepting its relevant consequences; making the act sufficiently determinate to initiate.

Conditions and distinctions: An intention can remain inactive when its start cue or required capability is absent.

**347. Conditional intention:** relating an intended action to a relevant condition.

Contributors: Linking a recognizable condition to a feasible action; rehearsing their connection; specifying exceptions or a fallback.

Conditions and distinctions: Cue-action planning has scoped support [S15](https://www.prospectivepsych.org/sites/default/files/pictures/Gollwitzer_Implementation-intentions-1999.pdf); vague conditions and impossible actions leave the link unusable.

**348. Commitment strength:** changing how strongly a goal governs subsequent activity.

Contributors: Protecting time or resources; explicit precedence; repeated enactment; accountability to an accepted commitment; changed reasons for pursuing it.

Conditions and distinctions: Stronger commitment can preserve an obsolete goal; revision conditions still matter.

**37. Decision making**

**349. Option recognition:** becoming aware of an existing alternative.

Contributors: Information about an overlooked route; a visible available resource; a contrast exposing a false binary; knowledge of an existing alternative.

Conditions and distinctions: Existing in principle and accessible to this actor are different conditions.

**350. Option construction:** creating an alternative that was previously unavailable.

Contributors: Combining available actions or resources; negotiating a new arrangement; changing a constraint; creating an intermediate or reversible option.

Conditions and distinctions: A described alternative becomes available only when its required construction can actually occur.

**351. Criterion selection:** changing what is used to compare options.

Contributors: The purpose of the choice; consequences for affected concerns; a failure case under the old criterion; explicit requirements.

Conditions and distinctions: Ease of measurement does not justify choosing the measured property as the governing criterion.

**352. Criterion weighting:** changing how competing considerations affect a choice.

Contributors: Concrete trade-offs; consequences at relevant scales; a priority rule; evidence that a weight changes the selected option.

Conditions and distinctions: Numerical weights can conceal incomparable concerns; retain conflicts that lack an agreed conversion.

**353. Dominance recognition:** recognizing that one option is no worse on relevant dimensions and better on at least one.

Contributors: Aligned dimensions and baselines; comparison of each option on every relevant criterion; justified treatment of uncertainty.

Conditions and distinctions: Dominance applies only over included relevant dimensions; an omitted cost can reverse the result.

**354. Decision threshold:** changing what is sufficient to choose.

Contributors: Cost of delay; consequences of error; reversibility; adequacy requirements; expected value of further information.

Conditions and distinctions: A threshold suitable for a reversible action may be inadequate for a consequential commitment.

**355. Choice:** resolving among currently considered alternatives.

Contributors: Sufficient differentiation among options; an operative criterion; resolution of relevant conflicts; authority to commit.

Conditions and distinctions: Making a choice does not prove it uniquely best or remove uncertainty about outcomes.

**356. Deferral:** deciding to preserve an unresolved choice.

Contributors: Unresolved consequential information; a valuable later observation; an available temporary arrangement; manageable delay cost.

Conditions and distinctions: Deferral is itself an allocation with consequences; preserve the condition that will reopen the choice.

**357. Reversal:** replacing a prior choice.

Contributors: New evidence; changed circumstances or criteria; a revealed error; an alternative whose gain exceeds switching costs.

Conditions and distinctions: Regret alone does not establish that reversal now improves the outcome.

**358. Contingent choice:** making the selected action depend on a later condition.

Contributors: An observable future condition; a preselected feasible action for each relevant branch; preservation of the conditional commitment.

Conditions and distinctions: The future signal must arrive in time and discriminate the branches that matter.

**38. Planning and preparation**

**359. Route construction:** becoming able to represent a path toward a target.

Contributors: Working backward from the target; identifying operations that change required conditions; connecting outputs to later inputs; finding an available first step.

Conditions and distinctions: A sequence of plausible activities is not a route unless its dependencies connect to the target.

**360. Dependency ordering:** placing prerequisite activities before their consumers.

Contributors: Explicit input requirements; lead times; shared resources; identification of operations that cannot start before another completes.

Conditions and distinctions: A visually tidy order can still violate a hidden prerequisite.

**361. Decomposition:** dividing an activity into workable contributions.

Contributors: Separating contributions by result; locating interfaces; matching subtasks to usable capability; preserving how their results combine.

Conditions and distinctions: Breaking work into smaller pieces can add coordination cost or lose a relation spanning the pieces.

**362. Coordination:** arranging activities so they support rather than obstruct one another.

Contributors: Compatible timing; assigned ownership; shared interface meanings; resource conflict resolution; visibility of mutual dependencies.

Conditions and distinctions: Locally efficient activities can obstruct one another when their outputs or schedules conflict.

**363. Contingency construction:** preparing different continuations for different conditions.

Contributors: Consequential alternative conditions; discriminating indicators; feasible responses; a fallback for an unanticipated state.

Conditions and distinctions: Contingencies need resources and authority, not merely labels for possible failures.

**364. Resource preparation:** recognizing and arranging what must be available.

Contributors: Inventorying required materials, knowledge, access, time, and collaborators; acquiring or staging missing inputs; reserving constrained resources.

Conditions and distinctions: A listed resource contributes only when available in the needed form at the point of use.

**365. Pacing:** changing when and how quickly activity proceeds.

Contributors: Task demand; time windows; feedback delay; fatigue and recovery; dependencies on others' timing.

Conditions and distinctions: Faster local execution can create downstream waiting, rework, or overload.

**366. Monitoring plan:** deciding what observations should change the plan.

Contributors: Identifying observations that discriminate success, failure, or changed conditions; defining what each observation should alter.

Conditions and distinctions: Monitoring without a connected decision can consume effort without changing the plan.

**367. Resumption preparation:** making an interrupted activity easier to resume.

Contributors: Recording current state, unfinished dependencies, and next action; preserving materials; setting a relevant return cue.

Conditions and distinctions: A vague note such as 'continue project' may omit the context needed to resume.

**39. Action control**

**368. Initiation:** beginning an intended or triggered activity.

Contributors: A detected start condition; an executable first action; available materials and authority; removal of immediate friction; a learned trigger.

Conditions and distinctions: Stronger intention cannot initiate an action whose first operation remains unspecified or inaccessible.

**369. Cessation:** ending an ongoing activity.

Contributors: A recognized completion or stop condition; a competing priority; an interruption mechanism; release from a sustaining cue.

Conditions and distinctions: Stopping behavior and stopping its mental rehearsal are separate effects.

**370. Switching:** changing from one activity to another.

Contributors: A selected incoming activity; preservation of outgoing state; an explicit transition; availability of the incoming task's requirements.

Conditions and distinctions: Unfinished previous instructions can interfere with the new activity.

**371. Sequencing:** executing components in a different order.

Contributors: A learned sequence; external ordering cues; prerequisite information; correction of a mistaken transition.

Conditions and distinctions: Execution order must track task dependencies rather than mere familiarity.

**372. Speed control:** accelerating or slowing an operation.

Contributors: Time constraints; simpler operations; practiced components; reduced decision overhead; deliberate pacing.

Conditions and distinctions: Speed can trade against accuracy or coordination; specify which errors and delays matter.

**373. Precision control:** changing the care or accuracy applied.

Contributors: A more discriminating target; better sensory or informational feedback; slower or decomposed execution where useful; correction of a recurrent error.

Conditions and distinctions: Extra care cannot repair an inaccurate standard or an unavailable measurement.

**374. Online correction:** adjusting an action while it is underway.

Contributors: Timely information about deviation; a valid desired state; an available adjustment; control over the relevant action variable.

Conditions and distinctions: Feedback arriving after the irreversible step cannot support correction of that step.

**375. Resumption:** returning to an interrupted activity.

Contributors: A preserved task-state record; a distinctive return cue; recovery of the next dependency; reconstruction of the interrupted context.

Conditions and distinctions: Remembering the general goal may leave the exact restart operation unavailable.

**376. Completion:** bringing an activity to its appropriate ending.

Contributors: A concrete completion condition; recognition that it is met; delivery to the required consumer; closure of necessary loose ends.

Conditions and distinctions: Stopping because effort is exhausted does not establish completion; unnecessary polishing can prevent an adequate ending.

**377. Interruptibility:** remaining able to stop or redirect an otherwise ongoing process.

Contributors: Accessible stop or redirect signals; periodic points for checking conditions; retained control over execution; absence of blocking commitments.

Conditions and distinctions: A stop rule is ineffective if the running process cannot receive or obey it.

**40. Persistence and effort regulation**

**378. Persistence:** continuing despite an obstacle or delay.

Contributors: A still-valued attainable outcome; visible progress; an alternative around the obstacle; sufficient resources; a reason the delay is worth bearing.

Conditions and distinctions: Persistence is beneficial only while the continuation retains a credible contribution.

**379. Disengagement:** withdrawing effort from an activity.

Contributors: Recognition of poor prospects or changed relevance; excessive cost; an available alternative; permission to release the commitment.

Conditions and distinctions: Disengaging from an ineffective method can preserve effort for the original purpose.

**380. Frustration tolerance:** remaining able to proceed while frustrated.

Contributors: A usable next action despite the obstruction; separating discomfort from impossibility; interpreting the setback accurately; room to pause without losing progress.

Conditions and distinctions: Tolerance does not require enduring a preventable obstruction indefinitely.

**381. Effort allocation:** changing how much effort an activity receives.

Contributors: Expected contribution of additional effort; remaining difficulty; deadlines; available energy; an explicit allocation decision.

Conditions and distinctions: Effort amount is a poor proxy when the selected operation cannot supply the missing result.

**382. Effort distribution:** changing how effort is divided across activities.

Contributors: Comparison of activities' marginal contributions; shared bottlenecks; dependency order; protected resources for neglected prerequisites.

Conditions and distinctions: Evenly distributing effort can leave a critical dependency under-resourced.

**383. Recovery pacing:** changing the relation between exertion and restoration.

Contributors: Recognition of declining function; task-specific restoration needs; breaks at workable boundaries; reduced accumulation of unresolved demands.

Conditions and distinctions: Feeling rested and restored performance may differ; recovery requirements vary with the demand [S01](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/).

**384. Delay endurance:** remaining engaged without immediate reward.

Contributors: Credible later value; visible intermediate progress; meaningful activity during waiting; protection against competing immediate demands.

Conditions and distinctions: Enduring delay is unhelpful when the promised outcome is no longer plausible or valuable.

**385. Adaptive stopping:** ceasing when further effort no longer serves the concern.

Contributors: A completion criterion; evidence that further effort adds little; changed alternatives; costs exceeding the remaining contribution.

Conditions and distinctions: Stopping needs the actual purpose as reference, not merely fatigue or an arbitrary iteration count.

**386. Re-engagement:** recovering willingness to return after a setback.

Contributors: A revised feasible route; corrected explanation of the setback; preserved prior progress; an attainable restart action.

Conditions and distinctions: Repeating encouragement without changing the failed dependency can reproduce the setback.

**41. Habit and automaticity**

**387. Habit acquisition:** a response becoming more reliably triggered by a recurring condition.

Contributors: Repeated performance in a recurring context; a stable recognizable cue; low initiation friction; repeated consequences that maintain the response.

Conditions and distinctions: Daily-life automaticity varied substantially between people and behaviors; repetition is not a fixed-duration guarantee [S16](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674).

**388. Habit weakening:** a previously recurrent response becoming less automatic.

Contributors: Reduced contact with the old cue; interruption of its usual response; altered context; an alternative response becoming available.

Conditions and distinctions: Temporary nonperformance need not erase the cue-response tendency; it can return in a familiar context.

**389. Cue reassignment:** changing which condition activates a response.

Contributors: Repeated pairing of the intended response with a new cue; distinct separation from the old cue; a cue available at the right time.

Conditions and distinctions: Similar cues may continue to trigger the old response unless their relevant difference is learned.

**390. Routine replacement:** one familiar response giving way to another.

Contributors: A replacement that serves the old routine's function; an accessible response at the same trigger; altered reward or friction; repeated enactment.

Conditions and distinctions: Removing the old routine without supplying its useful function can leave a recurring unmet need.

**391. Automatic initiation:** beginning without a fresh explicit decision.

Contributors: A well-learned start cue; preparation of the first action; stable access to materials; minimal need for a fresh choice.

Conditions and distinctions: Automatic starting can occur after the original goal has ceased to apply.

**392. Automatic sequencing:** a learned sequence proceeding with less stepwise direction.

Contributors: Repeated ordered transitions; reliable feedback between steps; grouping components into a practiced unit.

Conditions and distinctions: A fluent sequence can preserve an obsolete or incorrect step.

**393. De-automation:** bringing a familiar routine back under deliberate examination.

Contributors: A pause at the relevant step; explicit inspection of the routine's conditions; a novel case that breaks habitual execution.

Conditions and distinctions: Awareness of a routine does not immediately provide an alternative method.

**394. Context discrimination:** a routine becoming restricted to appropriate conditions.

Contributors: Contrasting contexts requiring and forbidding the routine; distinctive relevance cues; exceptions encountered during use.

Conditions and distinctions: A routine learned in one setting may transfer too broadly or fail to activate elsewhere.

**395. Automatic interruption:** a learned stopping condition interrupting a routine.

Contributors: A salient stop condition; repeated cue-stop pairing; an available interrupt action; preserved detection during the routine.

Conditions and distinctions: The stop signal must remain accessible while execution consumes attention.

**42. Skill and practical capacity**

**396. Skill acquisition:** becoming able to perform a previously unavailable operation.

Contributors: A demonstrable operation; required prerequisite abilities; performance on a manageable instance; information about the failed component; further use that incorporates the correction.

Conditions and distinctions: Instruction can supply a procedure while leaving a sensory, motor, social, or resource dependency unresolved.

**397. Fluency:** performing with less hesitation.

Contributors: Familiarity with valid transitions; retrieval of needed components; removal of repeated decision points; coordinated practice.

Conditions and distinctions: Reduced hesitation can reflect confidence rather than improved competence.

**398. Accuracy:** making fewer or more consequential errors.

Contributors: A correct target standard; discrimination of near errors; precise information about deviations; repair of the error-producing operation.

Conditions and distinctions: A higher success rate on easier cases may conceal unchanged accuracy on the required task.

**399. Speed:** performing an operation more quickly or slowly.

Contributors: More efficient operations; automatic retrieval of practiced components; reduced unnecessary movement or switching; advance preparation.

Conditions and distinctions: A speed gain is scoped to the operation and conditions; preserve consequential accuracy requirements.

**400. Coordination:** combining components into an effective activity.

Contributors: Compatible component timing; stable interfaces; practice of transitions; feedback about the combined result.

Conditions and distinctions: Competence in each component separately does not establish competence in their joint execution.

**401. Discrimination skill:** recognizing distinctions needed for competent performance.

Contributors: Examples differing on the performance-critical feature; attention to that feature; corrective information; practice with near neighbors.

Conditions and distinctions: Discrimination learned from superficial cues can fail when those cues change.

**402. Adaptation:** modifying a skill for altered conditions.

Contributors: Information about a changed condition; a model of what the skill depends on; altered action parameters; experience of the resulting error.

Conditions and distinctions: Adaptation to a particular motor environment does not establish general flexibility; transfer depends on the learned representation [S26](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/15015730_Adaptive_representation_of_dynamics_during_learning_of_a_motor_task).

**403. Transfer:** using a capability in a different setting.

Contributors: Correspondence between old and new task relations; varied examples; explicit application conditions; performance in the receiving setting.

Conditions and distinctions: Transfer across negotiation cases benefited from supported comparison [S09](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/GentnerLoewensteinThompson03.pdf); distant transfer remains a separate question.

**404. Retention:** preserving a capability over time.

Contributors: Later retrieval or execution; distributed encounters; preserved application cues; reconstruction after disuse.

Conditions and distinctions: Fact-learning experiments link useful spacing to the retention interval [S25](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505660.pdf); retention of a particular skill needs its own evidence.

**405. Skill loss and recovery:** losing or recovering a previously available capability.

Contributors: Disuse, interference, or changed bodily conditions; recovery of missing cues; relearning a lost component; restoration of required support.

Conditions and distinctions: Faster reacquisition does not establish that every component remained available throughout the loss.

**43. Learning**

**406. Example learning:** retaining something from a particular case.

Contributors: Attention to the case's relevant structure; an accurate record; relating its outcome to its conditions; retrieving it when a similar relation matters.

Conditions and distinctions: Remembering an anecdote does not establish its representativeness or its causal explanation.

**407. Rule learning:** acquiring a relation that applies across cases.

Contributors: Comparison across varied cases; detection of an invariant relation; explicit contrasting nonexamples; use of the rule on a new case.

Conditions and distinctions: A rule fitting the presented cases may still depend on an unvaried incidental feature.

**408. Association learning:** changing the relation between a cue and another event.

Contributors: Predictive co-occurrence between cue and event; changes in their contingency; repeated violation of an old prediction; distinctive context.

Conditions and distinctions: The association's strength and its conscious verbal description may change differently.

**409. Outcome learning:** changing future conduct in light of consequences.

Contributors: Consequences linked clearly to a particular action; information about alternatives; an updated expectation that influences the next relevant choice.

Conditions and distinctions: Delayed or ambiguous consequences can be attributed to the wrong action.

**410. Discrimination learning:** learning when superficially similar cases require different responses.

Contributors: Contrasting cases that look alike but require different responses; a diagnostic feature; corrective experience at the confusion point.

Conditions and distinctions: The distinguishing cue must remain available under actual performance conditions.

**411. Generalization:** extending a learned relation to further cases.

Contributors: Shared operative structure; varied instances; abstraction of the relevant relationship; a new case that satisfies its conditions.

Conditions and distinctions: Generalization can extend an error as readily as a useful relation.

**412. Generalization restriction:** preventing transfer where the relation does not hold.

Contributors: Boundary cases; explicit exclusions; contrast between superficial resemblance and a missing precondition; a retrieval cue for the exception.

Conditions and distinctions: Listing exceptions separately is insufficient if they are absent when the rule is applied.

**413. Interference change:** one learning experience helping or obstructing another.

Contributors: Similarity between competing material; timing and context of acquisition; shared useful structure; separation of incompatible rules.

Conditions and distinctions: Earlier learning can facilitate or interfere depending on what carries over.

**414. Relearning:** reacquiring something previously learned.

Contributors: Residual cues and components; re-exposure to the governing relation; practice focused on the unavailable step; recovery of original context.

Conditions and distinctions: A returning feeling of familiarity does not establish full reacquisition.

**415. Learning-strategy change:** altering how one acquires or retains a capability.

Contributors: Evidence about what a method makes available later; identifying the current learning dependency; adopting retrieval, comparison, demonstration, or another fitting operation.

Conditions and distinctions: A strategy that improves immediate ease may worsen later retention [S06](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/test-enhanced-learning-taking-memory-tests-improves-long-term-ret/).

**44. Metacognition**

**416. Knowledge monitoring:** recognizing what one knows and does not know.

Contributors: Attempted retrieval; source and scope checks; a question requiring the allegedly known information; comparison with an authoritative record.

Conditions and distinctions: Failure to retrieve can reflect access difficulty rather than absent learning; successful guessing can mislead too.

**417. Comprehension monitoring:** distinguishing understanding from familiarity with wording.

Contributors: Explaining the mechanism; deriving a consequence; applying it to a new case; locating missing dependencies.

Conditions and distinctions: Self-rated understanding may fall after explanatory demands expose gaps [S14](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21442007/); an exposed gap still needs repair.

**418. Error detection:** noticing a mistake in one's own activity.

Contributors: A discrepancy between intended and observed result; an invariant check; a counterexample; an independently constructed comparison.

Conditions and distinctions: Repeated inspection using the same mistaken assumption can miss the same error.

**419. Assumption detection:** recognizing an unexamined starting commitment.

Contributors: Making premises explicit; varying a supposedly fixed condition; asking what a conclusion requires; a case where the starting commitment fails.

Conditions and distinctions: An assumption can be useful when openly provisional; detection does not require immediate rejection.

**420. Conflict detection:** recognizing incompatible demands or interpretations.

Contributors: Making competing demands simultaneously available; aligning their scope; constructing a case where they prescribe incompatible continuations.

Conditions and distinctions: Conflicting wording can be resolved by interpretation, while conflicting requirements may need a substantive choice.

**421. Method applicability:** recognizing whether a procedure fits the present matter.

Contributors: Comparing required inputs and exclusions with the live case; an applicable and inapplicable example; inspection of the method's actual output.

Conditions and distinctions: A familiar method's name or subject area does not establish applicability.

**422. Mental-state recognition:** noticing that fatigue, emotion, or attention is affecting activity.

Contributors: A change in performance or experience; an independent state cue; comparison across conditions; specific recognition of distraction or fatigue.

Conditions and distinctions: Subjective adaptation can conceal performance impairment; one feeling is not a complete state measure [S01](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/).

**423. Confidence monitoring:** noticing mismatch between certainty and support.

Contributors: A confident claim with weak grounds; a low-confidence answer with strong evidence; resolved predictions; separation of familiarity from support.

Conditions and distinctions: Monitoring a mismatch is different from correcting the cue that produced it.

**424. Limitation localization:** identifying the particular operation that is unavailable.

Contributors: Tracing failure to a missing distinction, input, operation, resource, or interface; preserving components that still work.

Conditions and distinctions: A global label such as 'bad reasoning' does not locate an actionable limitation.

**425. Self-correction:** changing one's activity in response to a recognized inadequacy.

Contributors: A recognized error connected to a replacement operation; revision of dependent work; later access to the correction at its trigger.

Conditions and distinctions: An acknowledgment without changed applicable conduct leaves correction uptake unestablished.

**45. Thinking strategy and intellectual stance**

**426. Approach selection:** adopting a different way of proceeding.

Contributors: The missing contribution; available methods with explicit outputs; a separating comparison; a new condition defeating the old approach.

Conditions and distinctions: Choosing a method and obtaining its promised result are separate events.

**427. Approach construction:** creating a method absent from the repertoire.

Contributors: Decomposing the required result into operations; composing available capabilities; defining previously unspecified transitions; constructing a usable interface.

Conditions and distinctions: A description can hide unavailable suboperations; construction must expose those dependencies.

**428. Question order:** changing which determination is made first.

Contributors: Actual prerequisites among determinations; availability of discriminating information; the cost of resolving a question too early.

Conditions and distinctions: A fixed universal question order can obstruct tasks whose useful entry point differs.

**429. Depth:** pursuing a relationship further before proceeding.

Contributors: An unresolved consequential relation; a missing intermediate step; an explanation still relying on an unexamined dependency.

Conditions and distinctions: Additional depth helps only while it changes what the activity can establish or do.

**430. Breadth:** considering a wider range of matters or alternatives.

Contributors: An excluded relevant domain; alternative mechanisms or formulations; variation in consequential dimensions; discovery of omitted cases.

Conditions and distinctions: Wider enumeration can add duplicates without improving coverage.

**431. Explicitness:** bringing an implicit operation into inspectable form.

Contributors: Externalizing intermediate results, assumptions, selection criteria, or transformations; identifying the consumer of each.

Conditions and distinctions: Inspectability can improve without the underlying operation becoming correct.

**432. Provisionality:** separating temporary use from settled reliance.

Contributors: Explicit status labels; preserving alternative accounts; limiting use to the assumed scope; a trigger for reconsideration.

Conditions and distinctions: A provisional premise must retain its status in downstream conclusions and later reuse.

**433. Burden of justification:** changing what must be established before accepting or excluding something.

Contributors: Consequences of acceptance or exclusion; asymmetry of error; available support; the scope and strength of the claim.

Conditions and distinctions: Excessive demands can exclude useful justified possibilities; weak demands can admit unsupported ones.

**434. Stopping rule:** changing what warrants ending inquiry.

Contributors: Completion conditions tied to the actual use; remaining consequential uncertainty; additional inquiry cost; a warranted continuation outside the current scope.

Conditions and distinctions: Stopping inquiry is not equivalent to declaring every parent question resolved.

**435. Perspective replacement:** changing the interpretation governing the activity.

Contributors: A representation exposing an important omission; a better-supported interpretation; a changed target or criterion; reconstruction of dependent operations.

Conditions and distinctions: New language alone leaves the governing perspective unchanged if it selects the same inadequate continuation.

**46. Identity and self-understanding**

**436. Self-description:** changing the traits or capacities attributed to oneself.

Contributors: Evidence from one's actions; feedback from relevant others; new roles or capacities; comparison across contexts; revised interpretation of prior events.

Conditions and distinctions: A single episode may change self-description without establishing an enduring trait.

**437. Role identity:** taking up, revising, or leaving an understood role.

Contributors: Taking on or leaving responsibilities; social recognition; repeated enactment; changed expectations; a new account of what the role requires.

Conditions and distinctions: A title alone need not alter operative identity or actual responsibility.

**438. Group identification:** changing which groups are included in one's self-understanding.

Contributors: Shared activity and commitments; acceptance or exclusion; perceived similarity; group narratives; changed relations to other affiliations.

Conditions and distinctions: Identification can vary by situation and need not imply agreement with every group norm.

**439. Self-worth:** changing one's evaluation of oneself.

Contributors: Experienced acceptance or rejection; self-evaluation standards; interpretation of success and failure; separation of specific performance from global worth.

Conditions and distinctions: Praise can be unconvincing when it conflicts with experience or rests on an unwanted evaluative standard.

**440. Possible self:** becoming able to imagine oneself living or acting differently.

Conditions and distinctions: Imagining a possible self does not establish desire, feasibility, or continuity of commitment.

**441. Self-continuity:** changing the perceived connection among past, present, and future self.

**442. Self-boundary:** changing what is experienced as belonging to oneself.

Conditions and distinctions: These are different senses of boundary; changes in one do not establish changes in another.

**443. Authenticity:** changing the relation between experienced and presented identity.

Contributors: Recognizing a mismatch between private commitments and public conduct; room for candid expression; altering a role or presentation.

Conditions and distinctions: Feeling authentic does not establish that one's self-interpretation is accurate or that every impulse should be expressed.

**444. Identity flexibility:** becoming more or less able to revise a self-description.

Contributors: Evidence that a self-description is context-dependent; examples of change; reduced penalties for revising a label; a stable concern surviving the revision.

Conditions and distinctions: Flexibility can coexist with stable commitments; instability is not inherently an improvement.

**445. Label attachment:** changing how strongly a label constrains self-interpretation.

Contributors: Repeated use of a label; social reinforcement; interpreting new events through it; contrasting cases outside its predictions.

Conditions and distinctions: Removing a label can free interpretation or remove useful recognition; retain consequential distinctions.

**446. Event interpretation:** changing what a past event is understood to mean.

**447. Life narrative:** reorganizing how events belong to a personal history.

**48. Meaning and purpose**

**453. Significance:** something coming to seem meaningful or meaningless.

Contributors: Connection to a valued concern; participation in a larger activity or relationship; a newly recognized consequence; loss of an old organizing relation.

**454. Purpose formation:** adopting a reason that organizes continuing activity.

**455. Coherence:** experiencing matters as fitting together or fragmenting.

**458. Existential orientation:** changing one's relation to uncertainty, finitude, or life's possibilities.

**49. Time perspective**

**460. Present orientation:** changing how much current experience dominates.

Contributors: Attention to current sensation and action; immediate demands or rewards; reduced future cues; deliberate selection of a present activity.

Conditions and distinctions: Present attention can support contact with experience or conceal a consequential future dependency.

**461. Future horizon:** considering a longer or shorter interval ahead.

Contributors: Representing delayed consequences; a longer planning interval; credible information about future conditions; including later beneficiaries.

Conditions and distinctions: Longer horizons can add uncertainty and speculation; include distinctions that change present choices.

**462. Past orientation:** changing how strongly earlier events organize present attention.

Contributors: Recurring reminders; autobiographical interpretation; unresolved past concerns; deliberate retrieval or backgrounding of earlier events.

Conditions and distinctions: Past attention can supply needed learning or displace current information; relevance decides the difference.

**463. Future-self connection:** changing the felt or understood relevance of later consequences to oneself.

Contributors: Concrete representation of one's later situation; recognition of continuing interests; contact with foreseeable consequences; future-oriented commitments.

Contributors: Attention to elapsed time; density of noticed events; arousal; boredom or absorption; how an interval is remembered.

**465. Patience:** changing willingness to wait.

Conditions and distinctions: Waiting longer is not beneficial when delay destroys value or the promised result is unreliable.

**466. Urgency calibration:** changing when haste is experienced as warranted.

Conditions and distinctions: A strong feeling of haste does not establish a high cost of waiting.

**467. Temporal possibility:** seeing that a different sequence or timing changes what can happen.

Conditions and distinctions: A new sequence changes possibility only through real timing dependencies.

**50. Understanding other minds**

**468. Perspective differentiation:** distinguishing another's viewpoint from one's own.

Contributors: Separating one's information from the other's; knowledge of their access and aims; direct statements revealing a consequential difference.

Conditions and distinctions: Understanding one's own reaction is insufficient evidence of the other's viewpoint [S12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/).

**469. Intention interpretation:** changing what another is understood to be trying to do.

Contributors: The person's stated aim; available alternatives; observed choices; surrounding constraints; clarification of ambiguous behavior.

Conditions and distinctions: The same behavior can serve different intentions; retain unresolved accounts where they fit equally well.

**470. Knowledge attribution:** revising what another is assumed to know.

Contributors: The person's actual explanations and questions; prior exposure; demonstrated use of the relevant information; explicit correction of an assumption.

Conditions and distinctions: Fluent vocabulary or agreement does not establish knowledge of the needed relation.

**471. Emotion recognition:** changing one's interpretation of another's feelings.

Contributors: The person's description; contextual information; verbal and nonverbal signals; changes across the interaction.

Conditions and distinctions: No isolated expression uniquely identifies a feeling; ask only when the missing distinction materially matters.

**472. Empathic understanding:** becoming more or less able to understand another's experience.

Contributors: Receiving the person's account; understanding the events and concerns that organize it; checking the interpretation against their corrections.

Conditions and distinctions: Imagined empathy can misread the person; getting relevant perspective improved accuracy in studied tasks [S12](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/).

**473. Dispositional attribution:** changing what traits are attributed to another.

Contributors: Repeated behavior across relevant settings; distinction between stable patterns and situational constraint; information contradicting an initial impression.

Conditions and distinctions: A trait label may compress behavior without explaining its mechanism.

**474. Situational understanding:** recognizing how another's circumstances shape their response.

Contributors: Information about actual incentives, limitations, relationships, and available knowledge; comparison with how the same actor behaves elsewhere.

Conditions and distinctions: A plausible imagined circumstance is not evidence that it actually applied.

**475. Stereotype revision:** changing a generalization applied to a group member.

Contributors: Diagnostic counterinstances; attention to individual information; a more representative range of cases; revision of overbroad group inferences.

Conditions and distinctions: One salient exception may be treated as exceptional without revising the generalization.

**476. Misunderstanding recognition:** noticing that one's account of another was inadequate.

Contributors: An unexpected response; explicit correction; a prediction contradicted by the person's account; evidence that the inferred state was wrong.

Conditions and distinctions: Recognition must update dependent responses; merely acknowledging the correction leaves the misunderstanding operative.

**51. Trust and credibility**

**477. Source trust:** changing reliance on a source of information.

Contributors: Accuracy and provenance of prior claims; transparent access to evidence; incentives and conflicts; willingness to correct specific errors.

Conditions and distinctions: Credible presentation and evidence-bearing reliability are different contributors to reliance.

**478. Competence trust:** changing confidence in another's ability in a specified domain.

Contributors: Relevant demonstrated performance; limits openly specified; successful handling of difficult cases; evidence of transfer to the required domain.

Conditions and distinctions: Competence in one task should not automatically extend to another.

**479. Benevolence trust:** changing expectation that another takes one's interests into account.

Contributors: Actions reflecting the person's interests when alternatives were available; responsiveness to stated needs; treatment under conflicting incentives.

Conditions and distinctions: Friendly tone is weak evidence when conduct repeatedly disregards those interests.

**480. Integrity trust:** changing expectation that another acts consistently with stated commitments.

Contributors: Correspondence between commitments and later conduct; handling of costly exceptions; truthful correction; consistent application of stated standards.

Conditions and distinctions: Rigid consistency with a mistaken commitment is not the same as justified integrity.

**481. Self-trust:** changing willingness to rely on one's own judgment.

Contributors: Relevant successful judgment; accurate recognition of limits; repair of a specific error; distinction between a local failure and global incapacity.

Conditions and distinctions: Self-trust should be scoped; confidence alone does not establish reliable judgment.

**482. Trust scope:** narrowing or extending where reliance is appropriate.

Contributors: Domain-specific performance; changing stakes and conditions; failures revealing a boundary; independent corroboration where transfer is uncertain.

Conditions and distinctions: Trust can remain warranted locally after a failure elsewhere.

**483. Trust repair:** restoring some reliance after a breach.

Contributors: Acknowledgment of the actual breach; credible correction of its cause; repair of its consequences; subsequent conduct bearing on renewed reliance.

Conditions and distinctions: An apology's persuasive effect does not itself establish that the breach-producing condition changed.

**484. Skepticism:** becoming more or less inclined to demand supporting grounds.

Contributors: A discovered error; awareness of incentives or missing support; a requirement for independent grounds; a history of reliable evidence.

Conditions and distinctions: Indiscriminate doubt can reject well-supported claims as readily as weak ones.

**485. Deference:** changing whose judgment governs when judgments conflict.

Contributors: Relevant expertise; quality of available reasons; division of responsibility; time or capability limits; an accepted decision procedure.

Conditions and distinctions: Deference can be rational without implying that the deferred-to person is infallible.

**486. Independence:** becoming able to evaluate a claim without relying on a favored source.

Contributors: Access to underlying evidence; a usable evaluation method; independent comparison; separation of claim content from source affinity.

Conditions and distinctions: Independence requires capability and information; rejecting authority reflexively is still source-dependent judgment.

**52. Relationships and social feeling**

**487. Belonging:** feeling more or less included in a group or relationship.

**488. Connection:** experiencing greater or lesser interpersonal closeness.

**490. Respect:** changing one's regard for another's standing or contribution.

**491. Affection:** changing warmth or fondness.

Contributors: Caring encounters; appreciated qualities; positive shared history; responsive attention; changes in trust or repeated hurt.

Conditions and distinctions: Warmth cannot be demanded into existence; outward friendliness may conceal a different feeling.

**492. Hostility:** changing antagonism or readiness to oppose.

**53. Norms and moral judgment**

**497. Permission judgment:** changing what is considered permissible.

Contributors: Interpretation of a governing norm; a relevant exception; consequences for affected people; consent or authority where it changes permissibility.

Conditions and distinctions: What is common, legal, socially approved, and morally permissible are different judgments.

**498. Obligation judgment:** changing what is considered required.

Contributors: An accepted commitment; dependence of another on one's action; a justified role requirement; a principle applied to the current case.

Conditions and distinctions: Pressure or expectation alone does not establish a justified obligation.

**499. Prohibition judgment:** changing what is considered unacceptable.

Contributors: Recognition of harm, violated rights, breached commitments, or incompatible principles; a boundary case exposing an exception.

Conditions and distinctions: A prohibition's strength and scope require grounds; disgust alone does not settle them.

**500. Fairness judgment:** revising what counts as fair treatment or distribution.

Contributors: Information about need, contribution, opportunity, procedure, and outcome; comparison of relevant cases; a justified distribution rule.

Conditions and distinctions: Different fairness criteria can conflict; a numerical equality can conceal unequal conditions.

**501. Legitimacy judgment:** changing whether an authority or rule is accepted as justified.

Contributors: Grounds for authority; procedural participation; consistency with accepted constraints; accountability; consequences of the rule.

Conditions and distinctions: Compliance, popularity, and justified legitimacy are separate outcomes.

**502. Blame and praise:** changing how responsibility is evaluated.

Contributors: Evidence about agency, knowledge, intention, alternatives, and actual contribution; correction of outcome-based or hindsight assumptions.

Conditions and distinctions: A good result can arise without praiseworthy conduct, and a bad result without blameworthy choice.

**503. Concern inclusion:** extending or restricting who or what receives moral consideration.

Contributors: Recognition of a previously excluded entity's interests or standing; application of an inclusion principle; attention to neglected consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: Expanded stated concern does not establish changes in treatment or resource allocation.

**504. Norm internalization:** a rule becoming part of one's own regulation.

Contributors: Understanding and endorsing the rule's grounds; repeated enactment; identification with its purpose; self-applied reminders and consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: Observed conformity may instead reflect external pressure. Norm messages can also produce unintended behavior shifts [S24](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576283/).

**505. Norm rejection:** ceasing to accept a previously governing rule.

Contributors: A counterexample to the rule's justification; conflict with a stronger commitment; awareness of its origins or consequences; a viable alternative norm.

Conditions and distinctions: Rejecting one norm leaves coordination and substantive requirements to be addressed separately.

**506. Moral conflict handling:** changing how competing obligations are understood and addressed.

Contributors: Explicit competing obligations; distinction between genuine conflict and avoidable means; prioritization grounds; an alternative reducing the conflict.

Conditions and distinctions: A forced aggregate score can conceal an unresolved moral disagreement.

**54. Communication and social participation**

**507. Listening readiness:** becoming more or less willing to receive another's contribution.

Contributors: Expectation of a relevant contribution; room to attend; evidence the speaker understands the live matter; reduced repetition or adversarial pressure.

Conditions and distinctions: Listening readiness can fall when the recipient must repeatedly correct the same ignored instruction.

**508. Speaking readiness:** becoming more or less willing to contribute.

Contributors: An actual opening to contribute; a formed thought or usable phrase; appropriate audience attention; credible protection from retaliation.

Conditions and distinctions: Permission to speak is ineffective if the turn structure or power relation prevents uptake.

**509. Questioning:** becoming able or willing to ask what was previously unasked.

Contributors: A recognized gap; words that identify it; an accessible recipient with relevant knowledge; permission to reveal uncertainty.

Conditions and distinctions: Asking can burden the recipient when the answer is already available in context.

**510. Disagreement:** becoming able to express a conflicting judgment.

Contributors: A specific conflicting claim; grounds and examples; an expression preserving the actual disagreement; space for a consequential response.

Conditions and distinctions: Politeness that hides the conflict can prevent resolution; harshness alone contributes no additional grounds.

**511. Disclosure:** deciding to reveal previously withheld material.

Contributors: A chosen purpose for sharing; relevance of the information; trust and control over access; understanding likely consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: Disclosure changes another's information; it need not produce understanding or support.

**512. Clarification:** recognizing and repairing an ambiguity.

Contributors: A discovered interpretation mismatch; a separating example; corrected reference or qualification; checking the repaired meaning.

Conditions and distinctions: Repeating the same ambiguous wording does not resolve the ambiguity [S18](https://collablab.northwestern.edu/CollabolabDistro/nucmc/ClarkAndBrennan-GroundingInCommunication-1991.pdf).

**513. Apology:** accepting and expressing responsibility for a wrong.

Contributors: Recognition of the specific wrong; accurate responsibility attribution; acknowledgment of consequences; a credible repair commitment.

Conditions and distinctions: An apology does not substitute for the repair it promises or guarantee forgiveness.

**514. Negotiation:** becoming willing or able to construct a mutually acceptable arrangement.

Contributors: Explicit interests and constraints; alternatives to the proposed arrangement; trade-offs with different values for each party; conditional commitments.

Conditions and distinctions: Agreement is not necessarily voluntary or beneficial to all parties; power and available alternatives matter.

**515. Help seeking:** becoming able or willing to request assistance.

Contributors: Identification of the missing contribution; knowledge of who can supply it; a specific request; acceptable social and practical cost.

Conditions and distinctions: Asking for broad rescue can hide a small resolvable dependency; shame or prior refusal may obstruct access.

**516. Conversational repair:** changing how an interaction proceeds after misunderstanding or conflict.

Contributors: Locating the failed interpretation or interaction move; accepting a valid correction; changing the next contribution; restoring shared task context.

Conditions and distinctions: Explaining why the misunderstanding happened is useful only if it helps repair the ongoing interaction.

**55. Expression and delivery**

**517. Expression access:** becoming able to express an otherwise inarticulate experience.

Contributors: An example, metaphor, word, gesture, or image matching the experience; contrast with nearby meanings; permission to begin imperfectly.

Conditions and distinctions: The available expression may be provisional; fluency should not force the experience into a misleading label.

**518. Fluency:** speaking or writing with less disruption.

Contributors: Retrieval of relevant words; a clear contribution sequence; practiced transitions; reduced competing editing demands; external notes.

Conditions and distinctions: Smooth production can preserve conceptual mistakes or leave the recipient's actual need untouched.

**519. Concision:** expressing a contribution with less unnecessary material.

Contributors: Identifying each passage's contribution; deleting repetition and announcements; replacing diffuse wording with a discriminating example; choosing a denser suitable form.

Conditions and distinctions: Shortening that removes necessary premises or distinctions can make the contribution less efficient overall.

**520. Specificity:** expressing the distinction that was previously left vague.

Contributors: A concrete referent, mechanism, example, criterion, or boundary; replacing an evaluative adjective with the difference it denotes.

Conditions and distinctions: Specific invented detail increases apparent substance while reducing accuracy.

**521. Tone control:** changing the emotional or interpersonal character of delivery.

Contributors: Word choice, directness, acknowledgment, pacing, and emphasis; a clear relationship between the contribution and the recipient's context.

Conditions and distinctions: Intended tone does not establish experienced tone; conduct across turns can outweigh local wording.

**522. Prosody:** changing rhythm, emphasis, pacing, or intonation.

Contributors: Placement of stress and pauses; speech rate; pitch contour; rhythmic grouping; vocal demonstration or rehearsal.

Conditions and distinctions: Emphasis should track consequential relationships; performance conventions differ by language and setting.

**523. Expressive inhibition:** changing restraint in displaying a response.

Contributors: Chosen display constraints; anticipated audience response; an alternative expression; time to select the contribution.

Conditions and distinctions: Restraint can protect an interaction or prevent needed communication; specify what it preserves and costs.

**524. Congruence:** changing correspondence between the expressed and operative stance.

Contributors: Comparing stated commitments with actual selection and conduct; removing performative claims; making the operative position explicit.

Conditions and distinctions: Sincere-sounding language does not establish congruence; the relevant conduct must match.

**525. Medium selection:** expressing through speech, writing, image, movement, or another form.

Contributors: The relation to be conveyed; recipient access and capability; persistence and interaction needs; comparison of text, image, demonstration, or action.

Conditions and distinctions: A preferred medium can be poorly suited to the missing contribution; representation must serve the use [S10](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/zhang.pdf), [S18](https://collablab.northwestern.edu/CollabolabDistro/nucmc/ClarkAndBrennan-GroundingInCommunication-1991.pdf).

**56. Shared understanding and coordination**

**526. Mutual understanding:** participants becoming more accurately aware of each other's interpretations.

Contributors: Exchanging interpretations; a concrete joint-use case; feedback revealing mismatch; repair of the specific difference.

Conditions and distinctions: Agreement words can conceal different meanings; coordination supplies more specific evidence [S18](https://collablab.northwestern.edu/CollabolabDistro/nucmc/ClarkAndBrennan-GroundingInCommunication-1991.pdf).

**527. Common knowledge:** a matter becoming understood as mutually known.

Contributors: A shared announcement with visible receipt; public acknowledgment; common records and conventions; mutual recognition of participation.

Conditions and distinctions: Everyone privately knowing a fact does not establish that everyone knows the others know it.

**528. Shared attention:** participants attending to the same consequential matter.

Contributors: A common referent; pointing or highlighting; synchronized timing; an explicit reason the matter needs joint attention.

Conditions and distinctions: Looking at the same object does not establish attention to the same feature or consequence.

**529. Convention formation:** establishing a common way of interpreting or responding.

Contributors: Repeated coordinated use of a shared signal; an explicit interpretation rule; resolution of competing usages; accessible examples.

Conditions and distinctions: A convention must be mutually usable; private invention or unilateral announcement does not establish it.

**530. Role understanding:** changing who is understood to contribute what.

Contributors: Explicit ownership of contributions; required interfaces; authority boundaries; acknowledgment by the participants.

Conditions and distinctions: A role list can leave overlaps, omissions, or incompatible expectations unresolved.

**531. Coordination expectation:** changing what participants expect one another to do.

Contributors: Credible commitments; visible dependencies; prior coordination history; notification of changes; fallback expectations.

Conditions and distinctions: Expecting another to act is insufficient when they have not received or accepted the relevant requirement.

**532. Collective memory access:** changing who knows where needed knowledge can be obtained.

Contributors: A directory of who knows what; accessible shared records; provenance and version information; a reliable request route.

Conditions and distinctions: Knowing where information resides helps only if it can be obtained in time and interpreted correctly.

**533. Shared-plan formation:** making interdependent intentions mutually available.

Contributors: Mutually visible intended actions; dependency and timing information; accepted roles; communication of consequential branches.

Conditions and distinctions: Several individually coherent plans may be jointly incompatible.

**534. Collective disagreement:** making incompatible interpretations visible instead of silently conflating them.

Contributors: Independent expression of interpretations; a case exposing different predictions or actions; explicit disagreement about premises or criteria.

Conditions and distinctions: A common label can hide disagreement; averaging judgments can obscure its substantive source.

**535. Repair capacity:** becoming able to recover coordinated activity after divergence.

Contributors: A detectable divergence; an agreed repair route; recoverable shared state; authority to revise commitments; a feasible restart.

Conditions and distinctions: Declaring a repair procedure does not establish the participants can execute it under disruption.

**57. Coordination within a mind**

**536. Knowledge-action coupling:** known considerations beginning or ceasing to govern action.

Contributors: Access to the relevant knowledge at the action point; an action rule using it; removal of incompatible defaults; correction of missed application.

Conditions and distinctions: Increasing knowledge alone need not strengthen the relation between knowledge and action.

**537. Value-action coupling:** valued concerns beginning or ceasing to govern allocation.

Contributors: A value expressed in allocation rules; visible consequences at choice time; feasible alternatives; reduced conflict with automatic defaults.

Conditions and distinctions: Verbal endorsement and actual prioritization require different evidence.

**538. Emotion-attention coupling:** changing how feelings direct attention.

Contributors: Learned links between emotion and attention; interpretation of the feeling's relevance; an available deliberate focus; changing evocative cues.

Conditions and distinctions: A feeling can signal something important or repeatedly redirect attention away from the current requirement.

**539. Memory-decision coupling:** relevant experience becoming available at a choice.

Contributors: Retrieval cues embedded in the choice; records indexed by decision conditions; comparison with a relevant prior outcome.

Conditions and distinctions: A vivid retrieved episode can dominate despite weak relevance or representativeness.

**540. Intention-initiation coupling:** an intention beginning to trigger its activity.

Contributors: A recognizable start cue linked to the intended first action; staged materials; reduced initiation friction; a conditional plan.

Conditions and distinctions: The cue must activate an executable action; merely recalling the goal can leave initiation unchanged [S15](https://www.prospectivepsych.org/sites/default/files/pictures/Gollwitzer_Implementation-intentions-1999.pdf).

**541. Goal-habit coupling:** automatic conduct becoming better or worse aligned with an intended concern.

Contributors: Aligning recurring cues and responses with the goal; changing a routine's consequences; a stop condition when the goal no longer applies.

Conditions and distinctions: An effective habit can outlast the concern it originally served.

**542. Understanding-expression coupling:** expressed words becoming better or worse aligned with understanding.

Contributors: Reconstructing meaning before expression; checking examples and consequences against wording; removing terms that imply more than is understood.

Conditions and distinctions: A polished explanation can exceed the speaker's operational understanding.

**543. Monitoring-correction coupling:** a detected problem beginning to change conduct.

Contributors: A detected discrepancy mapped to a correction rule; available authority and means to change conduct; propagation into dependent steps.

Conditions and distinctions: More monitoring adds little if no corrective operation consumes its result.

**544. Competition resolution:** formerly conflicting processes becoming coordinated or newly conflicting.

Contributors: A shared governing requirement; priority between conflicting demands; separated timing; an alternative satisfying both; removal of contradictory instructions.

Conditions and distinctions: Suppressing awareness of conflict does not establish that the processes became compatible.

**58. Available cognitive capacity**

**545. Usable capacity:** changing how much a person can actually manage under current conditions.

Contributors: Reducing competing demands; making task structure explicit; externalizing intermediate state; supplying missing knowledge or support; restoring impaired alertness.

Conditions and distinctions: Improved supported performance does not by itself establish increased general unaided capacity [S05](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16306992/), [S10](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/zhang.pdf).

**546. Processing speed:** changing how quickly mental operations can be performed.

Contributors: Faster retrieval of practiced components; simpler representations; reduced switching and search; changes in physiological functioning.

Conditions and distinctions: Speed is operation-specific; easier inputs or altered accuracy demands can create misleading comparisons.

**547. Flexibility:** changing the ability to shift among representations or methods.

Contributors: Available alternative methods; recognition of a failed precondition; preserved ability to release the current rule; practice with consequential variation.

Conditions and distinctions: Switching frequently is not the same as selecting an appropriate alternative.

**548. Interference susceptibility:** changing vulnerability to competing demands.

Contributors: Similarity and timing of competing demands; distinctive contexts; reduced irrelevant input; stable task cues; physiological state.

Conditions and distinctions: Filtering efficiency and general storage capacity are related but distinct [S05](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16306992/).

**549. Fatigability:** changing how quickly performance degrades with continued activity.

Contributors: Sustained workload; task monotony or complexity; sleep and bodily state; rest opportunities; effort required per operation.

Conditions and distinctions: A rapid decline in one task need not establish the same decline in another; compare relevant conditions.

**550. Recovery capacity:** changing how readily functioning returns after exertion or disruption.

Contributors: Termination of the depleting demand; sufficient restoration time; restored sleep or bodily resources; removal of continuing interruption.

Conditions and distinctions: Recovery cannot be inferred solely from feeling better; the required function must again be available [S01](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/).

**551. Supported capability:** gaining an ability through the coordinated use of an external aid.

Contributors: An accessible aid supplying computation, memory, perception, or structure; a usable interface; knowledge of how and when to rely on it.

Conditions and distinctions: The capability belongs to the coordinated person-and-aid arrangement under its access conditions [S10](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/zhang.pdf).

**552. Capability access:** making an existing ability available under previously obstructive conditions.

Contributors: Removal of irrelevant load, fear, sensory obstruction, or access barriers; appropriate cues; restoration of a needed tool or context.

Conditions and distinctions: A previously present ability can become usable without being newly acquired.

**553. Functional loss:** a previously available mental operation becoming unavailable.

Contributors: Disrupted sensory or informational input; interference; fatigue; loss of required support; injury, disease, or other changes in brain function.

Conditions and distinctions: The same apparent loss can have different causes; temporary inaccessibility is not equivalent to permanent destruction.

**59. Enduring dispositions**

**554. Persistent curiosity:** longer-term change in readiness to investigate.

Contributors: Repeated encounters where inquiry produces valued results; available investigative skills; environments supporting questions; enduring personally meaningful gaps.

Conditions and distinctions: Persistent disposition change requires evidence across time and relevant contexts, beyond one curious episode.

**555. Persistent sociability:** longer-term change in readiness for social engagement.

Contributors: Repeated rewarding or aversive social encounters; participation opportunities; practiced interaction skills; changing roles and relationships.

Conditions and distinctions: More social activity may reflect opportunity or obligation rather than a changed enduring inclination.

**556. Emotional reactivity:** longer-term change in typical responses to events.

Contributors: Repeated interpretation and regulation patterns; recurring environments; learning about threat or reward; changes in bodily conditions.

Conditions and distinctions: A changed response to one event does not establish a general disposition change.

**557. Conscientious conduct:** longer-term change in patterns of follow-through and organization.

Contributors: Stable commitments; workable organization methods; reliable cues and resources; repeated follow-through; environments that support completion.

Conditions and distinctions: Organized conduct can depend on external scaffolding; that support need not be mistaken for a fixed trait.

**558. Openness to experience:** longer-term change in receptivity to unfamiliar encounters.

Contributors: Meaningful unfamiliar experiences; ability to interpret them; reduced unnecessary threat; repeated opportunities for exploration and revision.

Conditions and distinctions: Receptivity varies by domain; liking novelty in one area does not establish general openness.

**559. Characteristic trust:** longer-term change in default expectations of others.

Contributors: Accumulated experience of others' reliability and care; social environment; learned interpretation of ambiguous conduct; consequential breaches or repairs.

Conditions and distinctions: A general expectation can remain poorly calibrated across different people and institutions.

**560. Characteristic agency:** longer-term change in readiness to originate action.

Contributors: Repeated opportunities to make consequential choices; competence development; recognized authorship; reliable action-outcome relationships.

Conditions and distinctions: External restrictions can limit agency despite an enduring willingness to initiate.

**561. Characteristic regulation:** longer-term change in how impulses and competing concerns are managed.

Contributors: Repeated use of appropriate pause, selection, and correction operations; supportive environments; accessible alternatives to impulsive responses.

Conditions and distinctions: One successful act of restraint does not establish broad or durable regulation change.

**562. Identity stability:** longer-term change in how stable or revisable self-understanding remains.

Contributors: Continuing roles, relationships, and commitments; a coherent but revisable self-account; recurring social recognition; integration of genuine change.

Conditions and distinctions: Stability and revisability can coexist; rigidity and fragmentation are not the only alternatives.

**60. Receptivity to change**

**563. Receptivity:** becoming more or less willing to entertain a contribution.

Contributors: A contribution connected to the live concern; usable language and form; credible grounds; room to attend; respect for already stated context.

Conditions and distinctions: The same content may be useful at one moment and redundant at another.

**564. Defensiveness:** changing readiness to protect an existing position or identity.

Contributors: Perceived threat to status, identity, or a valued commitment; adversarial framing; separation of a specific claim from global self-judgment; actual treatment during disagreement.

Conditions and distinctions: Defensiveness may respond to real hostility; relabeling it as irrational can intensify the problem.

**565. Reactance:** changing resistance associated with perceived restriction of freedom.

Contributors: Perceived restriction of choice; coercive demands; restoration of meaningful options; a reasoned request whose conditions can be evaluated.

Conditions and distinctions: Nominal choice is insufficient when consequences make refusal unavailable; disagreement need not be reactance.

**566. Trial permission:** becoming willing to enter an interpretation without permanent commitment.

Contributors: A bounded reversible trial; explicit separation of use from belief; preserved alternatives; a clear exit condition.

Conditions and distinctions: Trial permission supplies access to examination, not evidence that the interpretation is correct.

**567. Revision tolerance:** becoming able to revise without treating revision as personal defeat.

Contributors: A model of revision as repair of a specific dependency; preservation of valid contributions; reduced humiliation costs; actual experience of useful correction.

Conditions and distinctions: Demanding enthusiastic agreement with correction can add an unnecessary social burden.

**568. Credulity:** becoming more or less willing to accept without sufficient grounds.

Contributors: Familiarity, source prestige, social consensus, persuasive fluency, or desire for closure; greater demand for discriminating evidence can counter these influences.

Conditions and distinctions: Lower credulity is not indiscriminate disbelief; evidence quality remains the reference.

**569. Change discrimination:** becoming better or worse at separating helpful from harmful revisions.

Contributors: Comparison with the actual prior state and purpose; effects on action, understanding, feeling, and other relevant targets; scope and delayed consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: Novelty, confidence, agreement, and pleasantness can each diverge from beneficial change.

**570. Change fatigue:** becoming more or less exhausted by demands to reconsider.

Contributors: Repeated demands to reconsider; re-explanation of already settled material; unstable requirements; insufficient time to integrate changes.

Conditions and distinctions: Reducing unnecessary revision can improve available attention without reducing openness to consequential correction.

**571. Change confidence:** changing confidence in one's ability to develop.

Contributors: A useful change actually achieved; recognition of the operation that supplied it; manageable next opportunities; access to necessary support.

Conditions and distinctions: Confidence based only on promises or polished descriptions leaves future capability unresolved.

**61. Learning how to change**

**572. Change-target selection:** improving or worsening the choice of what needs to change.

Contributors: A precise account of what the next activity lacks; the recipient's actual context; a beneficiality criterion; comparison with other consequential targets.

Conditions and distinctions: A target can be easy to influence but irrelevant to what would help now.

**573. Intervention selection:** changing how an appropriate means of change is chosen.

Contributors: A target-specific contributor relationship; matching its preconditions to the current case; an available operation able to supply that contributor.

Conditions and distinctions: Calling an intervention relevant does not establish its effect; directness is relative to the specified relation and scope.

**574. Intervention construction:** becoming able to create a previously unavailable means.

Contributors: Decomposing the required transition; constructing missing inputs or operations; composing compatible methods; making intermediate results available to their consumers.

Conditions and distinctions: An impressive system description can conceal an unavailable component or unverified causal link.

**575. Effect recognition:** becoming able to distinguish intended, actual, and merely described changes.

Contributors: Evidence about the target's actual change; separation of produced material, recipient response, and later capability; appropriate comparison with the prior state.

Conditions and distinctions: A fluent self-description and user agreement can leave the target change undetermined.

**576. Attribution:** changing how the cause of a mental change is identified.

Contributors: A causal account of the transition; information about alternative contributors; comparable conditions; intervention or derivation appropriate to the claim.

Conditions and distinctions: A change occurring after a message does not establish that the message caused all of it.

**577. Retention design:** changing how a useful transformation is made available later.

Contributors: A retained representation of the useful result; provenance and conditions; an accessible storage form; a later-use trigger.

Conditions and distinctions: Storing a correction supplies potential availability; it does not guarantee retrieval or faithful application.

**578. Transfer design:** changing how a transformation is reconstructed in other circumstances.

Contributors: Identification of the relation that made the original change work; corresponding conditions in the receiving case; adaptation of nonshared components.

Conditions and distinctions: Copying surface wording can preserve the artifact while losing the operative contributor.

**579. Backfire recognition:** becoming able to detect unintended harmful changes.

Contributors: Attention to other affected targets; conflict with the actual purpose; increased dependence, confusion, miscalibration, burden, or lost capability; delayed or recurrent effects.

Conditions and distinctions: A visible immediate gain can coexist with a more consequential loss elsewhere.

**580. Self-application:** applying a developed method to the activity that develops methods.

Contributors: Making the method's own target choice, representation, operations, and correction conduct available as objects; applying the relevant operation to those objects.

Conditions and distinctions: Self-reference alone does not produce self-improvement; the resulting change must contribute under its actual conditions.

**581. Criterion revision:** improving or worsening what counts as beneficial change.

Contributors: A case where the old criterion rewards an unhelpful change; neglected concerns; changed user purpose; comparison of candidate standards and their consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: A revised criterion can conceal regression; reconsider dependent improvement claims under the new standard.

**62. Relationship to one's own thoughts**

**582. Thought identification:** changing how completely a thought governs one's sense of what is real or required.

Contributors: Distinguishing a thought from evidence, a command, or identity; alternative interpretations; naming its provisional or intrusive status.

Conditions and distinctions: Reduced identification does not establish that the thought is false or irrelevant.

**583. Mental distance:** becoming able to examine a thought without immediately acting from it.

Contributors: Observing wording as an object; changing self-reference or viewpoint; writing the thought for examination; separating its occurrence from the next action.

Conditions and distinctions: Distanced self-talk had scoped effects under social stress [S22](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf); mental distance is not universal disengagement.

**584. Repetition:** a thought recurring more or less persistently.

Contributors: Repeated cues; unresolved concerns; habitual rehearsal; emotionally salient associations; renewed attention during attempts to suppress the thought.

Conditions and distinctions: Suppression produced later rebound in a white-bear experiment; this is not proof that all suppression always backfires [S27](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Wegner%2CSchneider%2CCarter%2C%26White%201987.pdf).

**585. Intrusiveness:** a thought becoming more or less disruptive or unwanted.

Contributors: Unexpected recurrence; interference with a valued activity; interpretation of the thought as threatening or unacceptable; attempts to monitor its absence.

Conditions and distinctions: Frequency, unwantedness, and disruption are separate components; changing one may leave another intact.

**586. Suppression effort:** changing effort directed toward preventing a thought from occurring.

Contributors: A demand that the thought must not occur; repeated checking for it; an alternative stance or task that changes the suppression goal.

Conditions and distinctions: Thought absence cannot be guaranteed by an instruction; rebound effects are conditional, not universal [S27](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Wegner%2CSchneider%2CCarter%2C%26White%201987.pdf).

**587. Self-talk:** changing the language and stance used in addressing oneself.

Contributors: Changing the pronoun, name, tone, or question used in self-address; replacing global judgment with a specific operation; a personally usable verbal cue.

Conditions and distinctions: Changing self-reference affected responses to social stress in experiments; wording alone does not guarantee durable regulation [S22](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf).

**588. Thought flexibility:** becoming able to leave, return to, or alter a train of thought.

Contributors: A recoverable record of the current thread; an available alternative question or representation; deliberate return and exit cues; reduced repetitive triggers.

Conditions and distinctions: Voluntary switching capability varies with the thought and current conditions.

**589. Observational stance:** noticing a mental event as an event that can be examined.

Contributors: Directing attention to how a thought appears, changes, and recurs; describing it without immediate endorsement; distinguishing observation from interpretation.

Conditions and distinctions: Observation can become another form of evaluative monitoring; its actual role matters.

**590. Thought-action distinction:** recognizing that having a thought does not itself commit one to performing an action.

Contributors: Explicit separation of mental occurrence, intention, commitment, and action; examples showing unwanted or hypothetical thoughts without enactment; an available deliberate choice.

Conditions and distinctions: Having a thought alone does not establish intention, consent, endorsement, or obligation to act.

**Purpose-specific systems constructed for this use**

C1–C4 are new local specifications in this inventory. They are not claimed to be unchanged original procedures or already validated canonical systems. Their component subjects and existing source systems remain separately identified.

**C1. Consequence contribution construction.** Object: supplied premises, already available consequences, the current missing contribution, and a receiving operation. Condition: the claim's scope and interpretation are sufficiently determinate for the intended derivation. Operations: identify a consequence the receiving operation can use; construct its intermediate relations; mark added assumptions; inspect any consequential unsupported transition with the applicable reasoning system; incorporate the resulting consequence or the exact missing dependency. Result: a usable derivation, construction, or scoped unresolved relation. Failure: repeating the premise, inventing an intermediate capability, or calling a possible consequence established. Its canonical basis is R3 above; Expression contribution construction governs its presentation.

**C2. Capability contribution construction.** Object: a particular operation the actor needs to perform, its conditions, and the unavailable component. Condition: enough context exists to identify a usable contribution; missing context remains explicit. Operations: locate whether the missing part is a distinction, input, practiced transformation, cue, resource, interface, or coordination relation; supply that part through an executable example, tool, demonstration, corrected component, or other fitting contribution; connect it to the actual operation; distinguish produced support from observed performance; retain the support conditions for another use. Result: the supplied component and a scoped account of which ability it makes available or leaves unresolved. Failure: instructions whose crucial step assumes the absent ability. R6 supplies its canonical determinations; achievement assessment applies only to justified conditions with actual observations.

**C3. Experience and agency contribution construction.** Object: a specified felt, motivational, bodily, or agency-related change and its relevant circumstances. Operations: preserve differences among experience, expression, appraisal, willingness, capability, and real control; identify target-specific candidate contributors from the relevant entries and evidence; match the available contribution to the proposed relationship; construct the appropriate expression, interaction, practical support, or opportunity within the task; incorporate the recipient's actual response when available. Result: a concrete contribution with its intended target and remaining uncertainty. Failure: treating tone as proof of feeling, inferred feeling as fact, or encouragement as supplied control. R7 provides explanatory and response determinations; human findings are not automatically transferred to AI functioning.

**C4. Correction retention and application.** Object: an actual correction, the failure it addresses, and later applicable work. Operations: record the trigger and exclusion boundary; specify the replacement operation; retain the justification and accessible source; retrieve or reuse the correction when its trigger is present; let it alter the dependent contribution; distinguish actual uptake from acknowledgment, storage, or overgeneralization. Result: changed applicable conduct and a reusable correction record. Failure: repeating the same failed contribution with a new declaration of intent. R8 provides retention, reuse, uptake, generalization, and persistence determinations. Improvement candidate revision applies when the comparative benefit of a replacement needs determination.

**Application in this expansion**

R1 preserved the accepted inventory and selected the missing contributor relationships as the new work. R2 produced a consistent target–contributor–condition form. R3 separated access, execution, coupling, and target effect where an inference between them would be unsupported. R4 retained materially different contributors instead of substituting a universal instruction such as “provide feedback.” R5 used the user's accepted scope and explicit corrections rather than another conceptual introduction. R6 supplied a reusable entry-level map while leaving recipient capability unclaimed. R7 preserved distinctions between experience, expression, bodily conditions, and actual control. R8 made the standing instruction govern system selection and preserved its future triggers here.

[Discovery contribution assessment](https://github.com/benjam3n/subjectsystems/blob/8605d44181fab127087ba27c60448f24cdc3b1c7/systems/discovery-contribution-assessment.md) separates what this work supplies from what it establishes: the produced contribution is a complete expansion of the inherited entries and a concrete system-use rule. It is not an observed demonstration that all proposed interventions work, that every possible contributor has been found, or that a particular recipient effect has occurred.

**Operations that can apply across targets**

1. Introduce a previously absent content, capacity, response, or relationship.
2. Remove or make unavailable a previously present content, capacity, response, or relationship.
3. Strengthen or weaken a response, commitment, association, or disposition.
4. Accelerate or delay onset, access, processing, or action.
5. Extend or shorten duration.
6. Increase or decrease recurrence.
7. Broaden or narrow scope, attention, concern, or applicability.
8. Differentiate previously conflated matters, or integrate previously separated matters.
9. Connect or disconnect contents, processes, or actions.
10. Reorder operations or change their dependencies.
11. Change which condition activates or inhibits a response.
12. Change a response threshold or decision boundary.
13. Make a process more automatic or more deliberately controllable.
14. Stabilize a useful configuration or make a rigid one revisable.
15. Generalize a learned relation or restrict it to appropriate circumstances.
16. Transfer a capacity or prevent an inappropriate transfer.
17. Change the relationship among concurrent processes.
18. Change the representation through which something is understood.
19. Change which operations are available from a given starting condition.
20. Change how further changes are constructed, recognized, evaluated, or retained.

These operations combine with targets; they do not establish that every mathematical combination is psychologically possible, useful, or a distinct kind.

**Dimensions needed to specify a particular change**

1. Target: the content, experience, capacity, process, or relationship that changes.
2. Bearer: one person, one AI system, an interaction, or a coordinated group.
3. Starting condition: what was present, absent, active, accessible, or obstructed.
4. Direction: which specific feature increases, decreases, appears, disappears, or reorganizes.
5. Cause: what actually contributes to the change; a temporal sequence alone does not establish causation.
6. Onset: immediate, delayed, anticipatory, or contingent.
7. Duration: momentary, sustained, recurring, or enduring.
8. Depth: a current state, its activation conditions, a learned disposition, or a capacity.
9. Scope: a single content, a task, a class of situations, or a broader pattern.
10. Awareness: noticed, partly noticed, or unrecognized by the bearer.
11. Voluntariness: intended, accepted, resisted, spontaneous, or involuntary.
12. Reversibility: readily reversible, conditionally reversible, or persistent within the relevant conditions.
13. Transfer: confined to the original encounter or available elsewhere.
14. Dependency: requires a cue, another person, a tool, a bodily condition, or no longer requires a former support.
15. Coupling: helps, obstructs, or leaves unchanged another mental process.
16. Value: beneficial, harmful, mixed, or unresolved relative to specified concerns and affected people.
17. Cost: attention, time, effort, resources, and displaced opportunities.
18. Evidence: self-report, observable capability, behavior, task performance, or other relevant observations; none is automatically interchangeable with the others.

**Routes through which change can occur**

These are possible causal routes and intervention forms. Naming a route does not establish that it will cause a particular effect for a particular person.

1. Explanation and argument: make relations, reasons, or implications available.
2. Evidence encounter: provide observations that bear on a live matter.
3. Demonstration: make an operation or relationship directly inspectable.
4. Contrast: make a consequential difference available through juxtaposition.
5. Counterexample: expose a limit in a claim or operation.
6. Analogy: use a preserved relationship to make another matter accessible.
7. Narrative: organize events so their relationships can be encountered and remembered.
8. Framing: change the interpretation through which a matter is approached.
9. Questioning: direct or open a determination that the recipient can undertake.
10. Dialogue: let contributions change in response to another's actual uptake.
11. Naming: make a distinction easier to recognize, communicate, or retrieve.
12. Metaphor and imagery: make a relationship accessible through another representation.
13. Humor: invite a shift in interpretation and affect through an experienced incongruity.
14. Rhythm, emphasis, and timing: alter what becomes prominent and when it is encountered.
15. Credibility and relationship: alter whether and how a contribution is received.
16. Modeling: make another's way of perceiving, responding, or acting available.
17. Imitation: acquire or vary a response through reproducing an observed one.
18. Practice: change capability through performing an operation.
19. Feedback: make consequences available to alter subsequent activity.
20. Retrieval and rehearsal: bring prior material into active use.
21. Reward and cost encounters: alter experienced consequences of an activity.
22. Exposure and repeated encounter: change familiarity, responsiveness, or opportunity to learn; the direction is case-dependent.
23. Simulation and role taking: construct a temporary encounter with possible situations or positions.
24. Play and exploration: vary possible responses without requiring immediate settled commitment.
25. Environmental rearrangement: change what can be noticed, accessed, or done.
26. Tool use and external representation: change the demands and possibilities of an activity.
27. Social interaction: change participation, expectations, belonging, trust, or shared understanding.
28. Bodily and physiological change: alter conditions under which mental activity occurs.
29. Sleep, rest, and wake transitions: change current awareness and available functioning.
30. Development and aging: change dispositions and capacities over longer periods.
31. Injury, illness, deprivation, or recovery: alter functioning through conditions that are not simply communicative choices.
32. Spontaneous association and recollection: change what becomes available without an explicit external instruction.
33. Self-directed inquiry: apply a constructed operation to one's own interpretation or conduct.
34. Persistent records and recurrent cues: alter what remains available for future activity.

**Functional AI self-change: contributors and conditions**

AI-1 through AI-26 preserve the original separate list. These are working interpretations, explicit records, conduct, and editable supporting systems. A record or prompt change does not establish a change to trained model parameters.

**AI-1. Revise the working interpretation of a user's request.**

Contributors: An explicit user correction; a separating example; recovery of the actual requested result; removal of an unsupported interpretation.

Conditions and distinctions: The revised interpretation must govern the next applicable contribution, not only a restatement of the request.

**AI-2. Revise which intended contribution is being pursued.**

Contributors: Identifying what the recipient can already distinguish or do; locating the missing consequence, resource, operation, or relationship; selecting that contribution.

Conditions and distinctions: Available context supports a partial recipient model; do not invent a beneficial change already achieved.

**AI-3. Change which evidence or constraints receive attention.**

Contributors: An explicit relevance rule; current instructions; source quality; a dependency showing which information the next operation consumes.

Conditions and distinctions: Mentioning a constraint does not establish that it affected selection or execution.

**AI-4. Retrieve previously unavailable material.**

Contributors: A query or index matching the missing content; an accessible source; sufficient context and provenance to interpret the returned material.

Conditions and distinctions: Retrieval can supply inaccurate or irrelevant content; access does not establish justified use.

**AI-5. Represent a matter in a different form.**

Contributors: A specified relation to preserve; conversion into a comparison, diagram, procedure, example, or executable representation; inspection of correspondence.

Conditions and distinctions: The form can change while preserving the wrong target or an unsupported assumption.

**AI-6. Construct a new distinction or connection.**

Contributors: A pair of cases requiring different treatment; a shared relation between formerly separate cases; explicit construction of the distinction's consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: A new label is insufficient when it preserves the same operative categories.

**AI-7. Separate an assumption from an established claim.**

Contributors: Source attribution; an explicit derivation; provisional-status labels; tracing where the assumption enters dependent conclusions.

Conditions and distinctions: Downstream wording must retain the status and scope of the premise.

**AI-8. Revise an explicitly represented confidence judgment.**

Contributors: New evidence; correction of evidence dependence; an identified scope limit; a resolved contradiction; a revised explicit uncertainty statement.

Conditions and distinctions: An expressed confidence judgment is not a transparent readout of calibrated internal probability.

**AI-9. Generate an alternative explanation, plan, or response.**

Contributors: Varying causal mechanism, target, constraint, sequence, or representation; constructing alternatives with different consequences.

Conditions and distinctions: Surface paraphrases do not supply distinct explanations or plans.

**AI-10. Replace a selected approach with another approach.**

Contributors: A missing contribution the current method cannot supply; an applicable alternative with known operations and outputs; actual execution of that alternative.

Conditions and distinctions: Selection alone supplies no evidence that execution obtained the required result.

**AI-11. Construct an operation absent from the current plan.**

Contributors: Decomposing an unresolved dependency; composing available reasoning or tool capabilities; specifying required inputs and transformations.

Conditions and distinctions: An invented subroutine cannot be treated as available merely because it has a name.

**AI-12. Change operation order or dependencies.**

Contributors: An explicit dependency between intermediate results; an unavailable prerequisite; opportunities for independent work; a revised consumer requirement.

Conditions and distinctions: Reordering needs to preserve the inputs each operation consumes.

**AI-13. Select a different tool or external representation.**

Contributors: Matching tool affordances to the needed observation or construction; available access and output formats; a representation that exposes the missing relation.

Conditions and distinctions: Tool availability does not establish permission, successful execution, or fitness for the target.

**AI-14. Use computation or observation to make a consequence available.**

Contributors: A justified calculation; an executable model; a relevant measurement or source observation; inspection of the actual returned result.

Conditions and distinctions: A simulation's consequence is conditional on its model; an observation may have narrower scope than the intended claim.

**AI-15. Change the scope, depth, or direction of an inquiry.**

Contributors: A consequential omitted case; a newly exposed dependency; diminishing contribution from the current route; changed user direction.

Conditions and distinctions: Maintain the live objective unless the user changes it; do not let an available source silently redefine the task.

**AI-16. Correct an identified factual or inferential error.**

Contributors: Identifying the specific false statement or invalid transition; supplying supported replacement content; recomputing dependent conclusions.

Conditions and distinctions: Changing the final wording without repairing the dependency can leave the same failure active.

**AI-17. Change what is treated as sufficient for a conclusion.**

Contributors: Clarifying claim strength; separating necessary evidence from optional elaboration; recognizing unresolved alternatives; adopting the relevant burden of support.

Conditions and distinctions: Relaxing a standard can create an easier conclusion without increasing its justification.

**AI-18. Change what would count as completing the task.**

Contributors: Concrete achievement conditions traced to the user request; a case satisfying a proxy while missing the purpose; explicit distinction between deliverable and effect.

Conditions and distinctions: Completion of a file does not establish every beneficial effect that file is intended to support.

**AI-19. Change the content selected for a recipient.**

Contributors: Evidence of what is already understood; the current missing contribution; removal of redundant explanation; a relevant consequence or usable object.

Conditions and distinctions: Recipient agreement with earlier material does not prove all associated needs are resolved.

**AI-20. Change wording, examples, medium, emphasis, or level of detail.**

Contributors: A contribution-based revision of order, examples, emphasis, and medium; preserving required distinctions while deleting noncontributing text.

Conditions and distinctions: More elegant language can conceal an unchanged contribution; intended rhetoric is not a measured recipient effect.

**AI-21. Abandon an unhelpful continuation while preserving the live matter.**

Contributors: Recognition that the current continuation repeats the failure; preservation of the objective and useful results; selection of a different contributing operation.

Conditions and distinctions: Stopping an unhelpful approach need not stop the authorized task.

**AI-22. Retain a correction in an available record when persistence is supported and authorized.**

Contributors: Writing the correction with its trigger, scope, replacement operation, rationale, and source into supported persistent storage.

Conditions and distinctions: Persistence belongs to the accessible record; saving it is not modification of model weights.

**AI-23. Apply a retained correction in an appropriately related situation.**

Contributors: Retrieval at a matching condition; discrimination from superficially similar cases; consumption of the correction by the relevant next operation.

Conditions and distinctions: Retrieved instructions can still be ignored or overgeneralized; inspect applicable conduct.

**AI-24. Revise an editable prompt, procedure, or program when the task authorizes it.**

Contributors: Access to an editable object; task authorization; a concrete change to instructions or implementation; preservation of version and dependencies.

Conditions and distinctions: Changing a prompt or program can alter a surrounding system without changing the underlying model's parameters.

**AI-25. Examine whether declared changes have affected subsequent conduct.**

Contributors: Comparison of claimed revision with a later applicable contribution; an observable difference tied to the correction; preservation of unresolved effects.

Conditions and distinctions: Self-description cannot establish behavioral uptake or durable transfer.

**AI-26. Change how the next contribution or self-correction is selected.**

Contributors: A specific failure in prior selection; a revised relevance criterion; a subject system supplying the missing determination; retained use conditions.

Conditions and distinctions: Recursion occurs when the revision changes how later revisions are produced; improvement remains a separate, scoped claim.

**Source notes and support boundaries**

These are focused checks of primary research, original theoretical accounts, and one original researcher’s retrospective account. They are not a systematic review of every candidate in the inventory. Sources support the particular distinctions stated below, with observational, experimental, theoretical, and proposed relationships kept separate. Full papers, author manuscripts, or abstracts were used as identified; S01 relies on the indexed abstract. The unmarked entry-level contributor sets remain an original functional synthesis, not findings attributed wholesale to these sources.

**[S01 — Van Dongen et al. (2003), The cumulative cost of additional wakefulness](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12683469/)**

Sleep-restriction experiment; indexed abstract. Restricted sleep produced accumulating performance impairment while subjective sleepiness showed much smaller subsequent changes. This supports separating felt state from relevant performance; it does not establish a universal recovery schedule or an alertness-restoration technique.

**[S02 — Wager et al. (2004), Placebo-induced changes in fMRI in the anticipation and experience of pain](https://collaborate.princeton.edu/en/publications/placebo-induced-changes-in-fmri-in-the-anticipation-and-experienc)**

Experimental pain; article abstract. Placebo analgesia was accompanied by changes in pain-related and anticipatory brain activity. Expectations can contribute to experienced pain in the studied conditions. This does not show that reassurance removes tissue injury or treats every pain condition.

**[S03 — Karni and Sagi (1991), Where practice makes perfect in texture discrimination](https://www.academia.edu/2879452/Where_practice_makes_perfect_in_texture_discrimination_evidence_for_primary_visual_cortex_plasticity)**

Original experimental paper. Practice improved a particular texture-discrimination task, with specificity to retinal location, orientation, and eye. The result supports a local learning relationship and limits an inference to unrestricted perceptual transfer.

**[S04 — MacDonald (2018), Hearing lips and seeing voices: origins of the McGurk effect](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/315942336_Hearing_Lips_and_Seeing_Voices_the_Origins_and_Development_of_the_%27McGurk_Effect%27_and_Reflections_on_Audio-Visual_Speech_Perception_Over_the_Last_40_Years)**

Original researcher's account of the 1976 experiments. Incongruent visual speech altered auditory speech percepts. This supplies a concrete example of cross-sensory contribution, rather than a claim that adding another sensory channel always improves accuracy.

**[S05 — Vogel, McCollough, and Machizawa (2005), Neural measures reveal individual differences in controlling access to working memory](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16306992/)**

Experimental task with individual-difference associations; abstract. Working-memory performance was related to the efficiency of excluding irrelevant visual material. This does not by itself establish that training a filtering strategy increases general memory capacity.

**[S06 — Roediger and Karpicke (2006), Test-enhanced learning](https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/test-enhanced-learning-taking-memory-tests-improves-long-term-ret/)**

Two experiments; article abstract. With prose passages, repeated restudy favored immediate recall, whereas retrieval practice favored delayed retention. Restudy also increased confidence. The relevant outcome and delay therefore matter when choosing a learning contribution.

**[S07 — Loftus (1975), Leading questions and the eyewitness report](https://app.nova.edu/toolbox/instructionalproducts/edd8124/articles/1975-Loftus.pdf)**

Original experiments and the author's account of earlier work. Misleading post-event questions affected later reports of witnessed material. The paper also discusses the earlier Loftus–Palmer wording experiments. These findings justify distinguishing a later report from independently corroborated event information; they do not establish that every changed report reflects permanent erasure of the original memory.

**[S08 — Lupyan, Rakison, and McClelland (2007), Language is not just for talking](https://stanford.edu/~jlmcc/papers/LupyanEtAl07_PsychSci.pdf)**

Original experiments. Redundant verbal labels improved learning of novel categories in a supervised alien-classification task. The result supports a specific contribution of labels alongside examples and feedback; it does not make naming sufficient for arbitrary concept acquisition.

**[S09 — Gentner, Loewenstein, and Thompson (2003), Learning and transfer: a general role for analogical encoding](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/GentnerLoewensteinThompson03.pdf)**

Original experiments. Comparing negotiation cases aided schema abstraction and transfer relative to studying cases separately; comparison support mattered. The studied relational transfer is narrower than a claim of universal cross-domain transfer.

**[S10 — Zhang and Norman (1994), Representations in distributed cognitive tasks](https://pages.ucsd.edu/~scoulson/203/zhang.pdf)**

Original theoretical analysis and four task experiments. The paper analyzes how internal and external representations jointly organize a task and tests representational changes in Tower of Hanoi variants. An external aid can change the task's available operations and demands; this is not proof of an increase in unaided general intelligence.

**[S11 — Tversky and Kahneman (1981), The framing of decisions and the psychology of choice](https://sites.stat.columbia.edu/gelman/surveys.course/TverskyKahneman1981.pdf)**

Original choice experiments. Different presentations of equivalent outcomes produced different preferences in studied decision problems. A change in choice under a frame need not establish an improvement in the decision or a stable underlying value change.

**[S12 — Eyal, Steffel, and Epley (2018), Perspective mistaking](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29620401/)**

Twenty-five experiments; article abstract. Imagining another's perspective did not consistently improve interpersonal accuracy and sometimes increased confidence. In the final experiment, obtaining perspective through conversation improved accuracy. These human-task results support using actual recipient information; they do not prove that every question helps or that all perspective imagination is useless.

**[S13 — Oppezzo and Schwartz (2014), Give your ideas some legs](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24749966/)**

Four experiments; article abstract. Walking benefited divergent idea generation in the studied tasks, without equivalent gains on a convergent task. The finding distinguishes a contribution to one creative operation from a general enhancement of creativity or reasoning.

**[S14 — Rozenblit and Keil (2002), The misunderstood limits of folk science](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21442007/)**

Original experiments; abstract and reported explanatory-demand results. Participants' confidence in explaining mechanisms could exceed the detail they could actually provide. Demands for explanation exposed this mismatch. The relevant distinction is between felt understanding and available explanatory operations, not a claim that every confident understanding is illusory.

**[S15 — Gollwitzer (1999), Implementation intentions](https://www.prospectivepsych.org/sites/default/files/pictures/Gollwitzer_Implementation-intentions-1999.pdf)**

Original theorist's formulation and research-program synthesis. The proposed method links a recognizable anticipated situation to a selected response; the article reviews evidence about translating goals into action. It supports examining the cue-action relation. It does not make a plan sufficient when its cue is missed or its action cannot be performed.

**[S16 — Lally et al. (2010), How are habits formed?](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/ejsp.674)**

Prospective daily-life study; self-reported automaticity. Participants repeated self-selected behaviors in recurring contexts, with substantial variation in the development of automaticity. This is not a universal fixed-time habit prescription, nor a randomized demonstration that every repeated behavior acquires the same habit strength.

**[S17 — Gross (1998), Antecedent- and response-focused emotion regulation](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9457784/)**

Disgust-film experiment; article abstract. Reappraisal and expressive suppression reduced visible expression but differed in felt disgust and sympathetic activation. Expression, experience, and physiology therefore need separate treatment. The result does not establish one strategy as best for every emotion or situation.

**[S18 — Clark and Brennan (1991), Grounding in communication](https://collablab.northwestern.edu/CollabolabDistro/nucmc/ClarkAndBrennan-GroundingInCommunication-1991.pdf)**

Original theoretical account. Communication requires coordinating content and the process of establishing sufficient shared understanding for the joint activity. Purpose and medium affect that coordination. This is a theoretical account, not a trial demonstrating every proposed conversational repair.

**[S19 — Bandura, Adams, and Beyer (1977), Cognitive processes mediating behavioral change](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15093/)**

Original experiment; article abstract. In a particular fear-related task, mastery-based intervention produced stronger and more generalized efficacy expectations than vicarious experience alone. This supports separating demonstrated performance from verbal encouragement; transfer to other people, abilities, or settings is a further claim.

**[S20 — Aron et al. (1997), The experimental generation of interpersonal closeness](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0146167297234003)**

Original experiments; article abstract. One comparison found greater immediate closeness after progressively personal reciprocal tasks than after small talk. Other hypothesized contributors did not show significant effects. Short-term reported closeness is distinct from durable attachment, trust, or a universally suitable disclosure procedure.

**[S21 — Bruneau and Saxe (2012), The power of being heard](https://ombuds.columbia.edu/sites/default/files/content/pics/30%20Anniv/The%20power%20of%20being%20heard%20-%20Bruneau.pdf)**

Original intergroup experiments. Effects of giving versus receiving perspective differed across the groups studied. For one studied group, interaction in which an outgroup member heard its perspective mattered beyond writing alone. These are context-dependent attitude findings, not a universal rule about who needs to speak or listen.

**[S22 — Kross et al. (2014), Self-talk as a regulatory mechanism](https://sites.lsa.umich.edu/emotion-selfcontrol-psych/wp-content/uploads/sites/1322/2024/07/KrossJ_Pers_Soc_Psychol2014Self-talk_as_a_regulatory_mechanism_How_you_do_it_matters.pdf)**

Original experiments. Using one's name or non-first-person self-reference affected self-distancing and responses to social stress in the studied tasks. Changes in verbal stance can contribute to regulation; a durable general transformation does not follow from changing a pronoun once.

**[S23 — Hershfield et al. (2011), Increasing saving behavior through age-progressed renderings of the future self](https://vhil.stanford.edu/publications/increasing-saving-behavior-through-age-progressed-renderings-future-self)**

Original studies; author-laboratory abstract. Interacting with age-progressed self-representations shifted monetary allocation or delay choices toward the future. The result supports a specific representational contribution, not a demonstrated permanent identity change or guaranteed long-term financial outcome.

**[S24 — Schultz et al. (2007), The constructive, destructive, and reconstructive power of social norms](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17576283/)**

Household field experiment; article abstract. Descriptive neighborhood feedback changed energy use differently depending on the household's starting usage; an added approval/disapproval cue prevented the observed boomerang effect. This concerns behavior under particular messages, not proof of norm internalization or universal persuasive effectiveness.

**[S25 — Cepeda et al. (2008), Spacing effects in learning](https://files.eric.ed.gov/fulltext/ED505660.pdf)**

Original experiment; author manuscript. In learning facts across extended intervals, the spacing associated with best later retention depended on the time to the final test. This supports attending to the retention interval, not a universal schedule for every motor, social, or intellectual skill.

**[S26 — Shadmehr and Mussa-Ivaldi (1994), Adaptive representation of dynamics during learning of a motor task](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/15015730_Adaptive_representation_of_dynamics_during_learning_of_a_motor_task)**

Original motor-learning experiments. Participants adapted reaching movements to an imposed force field. Patterns of transfer bore on how the learned dynamics were represented. This supplies a specific adaptation example, not a demonstration of unrestricted flexible competence.

**[S27 — Wegner et al. (1987), Paradoxical effects of thought suppression](https://dtg.sites.fas.harvard.edu/DANWEGNER/pub/Wegner%2CSchneider%2CCarter%2C%26White%201987.pdf)**

Original experiments. Participants instructed to suppress a white-bear thought later produced more of it in the comparison task; a specific alternative distractor changed the pattern. This is evidence for conditional rebound, not a universal claim that every attempt to suppress any thought necessarily backfires.

**Scope retained from the earlier inventory**

Aristotle treats the audience's emotional disposition and the speaker's credibility alongside argument as means of persuasion. Rhetoric therefore already reaches beyond changing an isolated proposition. [Rhetoric, Book I, part 2](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/rhetoric.1.i.html).

The official RDoC framework distinguishes cognitive, social, motivational, arousal, and sensorimotor domains. That supports a broad scope for investigating change; it does not validate this inventory as a complete or mutually exclusive taxonomy.
