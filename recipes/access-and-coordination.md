# Change what can be accessed or enacted

Use these routes when existing knowledge is inaccessible at the point of use, or accepted knowledge does not enable the action. The routes can separate memory, interpretation, ability and authority without assuming they are unrelated in every case.

## Route A: make the consequential exception retrievable

1. Preserve each source case as a canonical record with its trigger, action, exception, actor, result and provenance. Give it a stable ID.
2. From the actual retrieval question, choose a secondary index. If the question names a trigger, map that trigger to canonical IDs. Store references rather than independently editable copies.
3. Return the action together with the exception and its applicability conditions. A shorter response that loses the exception can be worse than a slower full record.
4. Check a known case, a case with a changed actor, and a case in which the exception reverses the action. Confirm every source field remains recoverable.
5. Measure the access property actually at issue: record inspections, elapsed time, successful use, or later retention. Do not substitute one for another.

In the [MEMK capture case](../post-allocation/cycle-01/memory-capability/01-memk-capture.md), eight records have six content fields each. The normalized records and index preserve 48 fields. Trigger lookup avoids scanning up to eight records. This supports an access improvement under the declared operation count. It does not establish that a person learned the content, that every query became faster, or that an external index is identical to internal memory.

The narrative alternative remains useful when sequence and context matter. Add a view for that use rather than deleting its information to win a trigger query. Setup and maintenance costs can defeat the index for sufficiently rare retrieval.

## Route B: distinguish accepted evidence from ability to act

1. Ask or inspect the intermediate conditions: what is understood, what is accepted, what the person intends, what interface is available, and who has actual authority. Do not infer these solely from the final action.
2. Locate a condition supported by the available statements or observations. If several explanations remain, request or perform an authorized discriminating check rather than choose a psychological diagnosis.
3. If the condition is access, provide the exact artifact and requested action to a person or interface authorized to act. Specify any relevant deadline and fallback. Obtain separate authorization before sending a person-directed message where required.
4. Distinguish a prepared request, a sent request, an access grant and a completed downstream action. Use the relevant receipt for each claim.
5. Reassess after the condition changes. New disagreement may require evidence; a new workflow failure may require another operation.

In the [Alex case](../post-allocation/cycle-03/recipient/01-persua-locus-before-message.md), Alex explicitly accepts the corrected calculation and says publication awaits permission. Another arithmetic explanation does not grant permission. An authorized grant or an authorized publisher is a candidate route; whether either happens remains an empirical question. If Alex subsequently disputes evidence independence, the [lineage route](evidence-lineage.md) becomes pertinent.

These cases show why failure to act need not be failure to understand. They do not establish that unwillingness, bodily inability or perceptual exclusion can always be repaired by a tool or permission change. Where the relevant capability is unavailable, specify what access would enable an attempt and continue the design without claiming its execution.
