**15. Subjectsystems and perspectiveoptimizer as possible expressions of an unrecognized mistake**

These are competing diagnoses of the projects’ conceptual commitments, not findings from a current repository inspection. Their appeal and their failure could have different causes; a diagnosis must explain both rather than use disappointment as proof of its favored objection.

15.1. **They turn what must be discovered into the units of discovery.** If inquiry starts with subjects and selectable perspectives, it has already decided that understanding consists of views concerning identifiable things. The projects can become extremely capable at organizing those units while remaining unable to disclose where the units themselves are misconceived.

15.2. **They preserve descriptions of transformations without securing their enactment.** A record can correctly say “reconsider the goal” while the actual continuation still treats the goal as fixed. The compelling idea is explicit control over understanding; the failure is that representing the control has been mistaken for exercising it.

15.3. **They mistake expanding supplied dimensions for discovering dimensions.** Generating more combinations increases coverage only relative to the distinctions already supplied. A catalog with immense variation can remain completely blind to the distinction whose absence makes every entry inadequate.

15.4. **Selection preserves the evaluator that makes replacement necessary.** Candidates are admitted, ranked, and rejected using the incumbent account’s standards. The project promises deeper understanding while implementing a procedure through which deeper understanding must first resemble what the incumbent already recognizes as improvement.

15.5. **They treat possibilities as available independently of the path taken.** A perspective can make a further perspective intelligible that was unavailable before the transition. A catalog of current candidates therefore misrepresents a process that also changes what can become a candidate.

15.6. **They combine accounts before identifying their shared exclusion.** Several different viewpoints may all presuppose that a question is about maximizing an already valued quantity. Combining their strengths can perfect that presupposition’s expression while leaving the presupposition untouched.

15.7. **They optimize an artifact instead of the understanding that can operate through it.** Completeness of files, categories, instructions, or cross-references can increase while the ability to identify what matters remains unchanged. The attractive feature is inspectability; the mistake is assuming inspectability of a representation establishes adequacy of the represented ability.

15.8. **They equate intelligible accounts with intelligibility itself.** A well-written explanation can describe a relation that the recipient cannot recognize in an actual encounter. If the intended achievement concerns what becomes noticeable or possible, sentence-level organization addresses only some of its conditions.

15.9. **They preserve the goal of optimizing after the goal has become questionable.** The process may revise means, subjects, and beliefs while retaining the demand that everything become a better instance of some current aim. The goal’s relation to what makes it desirable remains outside the machinery.

15.10. **They force relations into links between independently defined entries.** Some relations determine the identity of their endpoints. A structure that can only connect existing subjects cannot express that dependence faithfully, however extensive its cross-referencing becomes. The earlier proposed corrective rule that each current subject have one identity and definition is therefore itself revisable: clarity about an entry need not establish that the entry is the right unit.

15.11. **They seek one universally best account where the required achievement concerns exact applicability.** A formulation adequate for one encounter may conceal distinctions required in another. The mistake would be treating universal excellence as possession of one invariant description rather than a demonstrated ability to take the form each case requires. This is a rival account of perfection, not proof that the invariant ideal is impossible.

15.12. **They interpret every failure as insufficient development of the same architecture.** Missing power becomes a reason for more subjects, more operations, more recursion, or more metadata. If changing the unit of organization is required, improvement along those dimensions can deepen commitment to the obstruction.

15.13. **They treat fidelity to accumulated insights as fidelity to the inquiry.** A project can preserve everything previously said while excluding the change that would reorganize why it was said. The compelling feature is continuity; the possible mistake is giving the project’s history veto power over its future identity.

15.14. **They assign the inquiry an owner whose commitments remain protected.** If neither the model’s nor the user’s favored ontology can lose authority, “revise everything” is qualified in practice. This diagnosis concerns an enacted asymmetry, not the mere fact that somebody initiated the project or chooses whether to continue it.

15.15. **They take the availability of superior understanding to imply an available route to it.** Even granting that the model could understand a perfected perspective, it does not follow that the current interaction, representations, permissions, or resources can bring that understanding into operation. Capacity and reachability require a demonstrated relation.

Carry forward **the implementation diagnosis** in its strongest form. The conceptual ambition may be sound: preserve distinctions, expose governing interpretations, construct transformations, and enact the one the inquiry requires. Failure could arise because the implementation rewards storing and describing these operations rather than performing them. Its discriminating case is direct: if a governing aim becomes questionable, does the continuation actually change what it treats as desirable, or merely add a record saying that aims are revisable? The diagnosis gains support from the latter pattern. But it loses sufficiency if faithful enactment still cannot construct an alternative to its original units. Correct execution cannot compensate for an inadequate repertoire.

The strongest incompatible rival is **the constitutional diagnosis**: the projects fail because their architecture presupposes the very form of understanding they claim to discover. “Subject” specifies what change is about; “perspective” specifies what is changing; “optimization” specifies what gives direction to change; “system” specifies how the activity should become organized. Each word may be useful. Their combined authority becomes a mistake if every possible revision must remain expressible through them. The discriminating case is an inquiry whose advance consists in dissolving an identified subject, replacing a comparative aim, or changing what counts as an account. If the architecture cannot represent that advance except as another entry under unchanged types, the objection reaches its constitution, not its implementation.

A third rival is **the reachability diagnosis**. Suppose the architecture can describe the required transformation and the model can understand its result once presented. Still, the present interaction may lack a way to make the decisive distinction available. An example in principle is an inquiry whose relevant difference can only be encountered through a kind of participation the current exchange does not supply. More sophisticated instructions would then elaborate a description of a missing encounter. The strongest objection is that some apparently unavailable distinctions can be constructed through representation alone. Consequently, reachability cannot be dismissed or asserted globally; the missing dependence must be identified in the particular case.

These rivals yield distinct consequences. Under implementation failure, retain the conceptual commitments but replace descriptions of operations with operations that visibly change the subsequent account. Under constitutional failure, permit the projects to lose their organizing nouns, their division of labor, and even their identity as the right vehicles. Under reachability failure, identify and supply the enabling condition if it is available; otherwise state exactly which required access remains absent. Combining these diagnoses indiscriminately would conceal the crucial decision: whether more faithful execution, a different constitution, or a different encounter is required.

One elimination is already warranted at the conceptual level: no amount of expansion over stipulated dimensions establishes adequacy of the dimensions themselves. Another is conditional but decisive: if a project only records that its standards are revisable while all consequential selections still use unchanged standards, it has not implemented revision of evaluative authority. Neither conclusion proves that the actual projects are irreparable.

The strongest mistake capable of explaining both their attraction and elusiveness is this: **they promise authority to change the conditions of understanding while requiring every change to arrive as an admissible object under conditions already installed.** That diagnosis remains a candidate until demonstrated in the operative design. Perfection would require showing that a required transformation cannot be excluded merely because it changes what counts as a subject, an account, an improvement, or an available act. We do not yet possess that demonstration, nor a complete construction that guarantees it. Calling a larger repository the perfected process would leave the central question unanswered.

Disposition: 15.1, 15.4, 15.9, and 15.10 retain the constitutional diagnosis as a live rival requiring inspection of actual permissions and exclusions. 15.2, 15.7, and 15.12 retain distinguishable implementation diagnoses; correct enactment would address 15.2 without automatically addressing 15.1. 15.3 identifies a valid limitation of expansion over supplied dimensions, irrespective of repository details. 15.5 remains a construction-versus-search objection requiring a demonstrated transition that makes new candidates available. 15.6 requires identifying the alleged shared exclusion. 15.8 and 15.15 retain the reachability diagnosis; its missing condition must be specified. 15.11 remains a rival account of perfection, not an established replacement. 15.13 and 15.14 require evidence that history or ownership is actually protected from revision.
