Verbatim inquiry extract. Its numbered entries are explored alternatives, not an exhaustive possibility space or new demonstrated findings.

**6. Begin from “everything is resource allocation,” then let perspective transform that starting point.**

The identity claim is distinct from saying that everything consumes something or that everything deliberately maximizes a quantity. No implication ordering among these claims has been established, and neither is a licensed substitute for the identity. Taken literally, the identity requires an account of allocation broad enough to include how resources, recipients, uses, and alternatives become distinguishable at all. Otherwise it already exempts the activity that supplies its own terms.

Fourteen materially different possibilities follow. They are rival proposals about what the identity would mean, not an exhaustive division of reality.

1. **Distribution among established uses.** Resources and possible uses already exist; perspective determines which distribution appears appropriate. This preserves ordinary allocation, but cannot yet explain the creation of its categories.
2. **Production of capacities.** Allocation changes what can later be allocated and done. Learning a language changes the available activities, not just the quantities devoted to existing activities. Perspective becomes part of the production of future powers.
3. **Constitution through use.** Something becomes a resource because a relation makes it usable. A locked room, a key, and knowledge of the lock are not independent additions to a common stock; their conjunction changes what each can do.
4. **Differentiation before selection.** What appears to be allocation begins when differences become actionable. Two sounds receive different treatment only after something distinguishes them. Perspective partly establishes the recipients between which allocation can occur.
5. **Arrangement without transfer.** Identical components and quantities can support different activities when their relations change. Allocation must therefore include placement, coupling, and sequence, rather than only amounts assigned.
6. **Exclusion through commitment.** An allocation is characterized by what it makes unavailable elsewhere. This requires specifying which counterfactual alternatives genuinely coexist; imagined alternatives cannot automatically establish an opportunity cost.
7. **Formation of the feasible.** Some allocations create new alternatives or destroy the conditions for previous ones. The set of possible uses is an effect within the account, not a fixed menu outside it.
8. **Reproduction through time.** What exists now depends on earlier allocations, while present activity preserves or changes those dependencies. A perspective is partly an inherited pattern of what continues to receive support without renewed choice.
9. **Maintenance of an organized living activity.** Distinctions matter through their consequences for continued life and action. Perspective includes bodily readiness and environmental access. This explains a class of allocations; it does not by itself establish the universal identity.
10. **Jurisdiction over use.** Ownership, permission, and obligation determine what counts as available to whom. A room can be physically accessible and unavailable under an accepted commitment. Perspective therefore includes practical authority, not just representation.
11. **Formation of participants.** People and groups are not always fixed recipients. An allocation can create an institution, dependency, or collective agent. The identities of allocator and recipient can be consequences of the relations being investigated.
12. **Assignment of significance.** What receives urgency, credibility, or sacrifice changes subsequent activity. “Resource” must then distinguish physical quantities from practical standing; calling both allocations does not make them interchangeable.
13. **Unchosen differentiation.** If everything is allocation, unintended events must qualify too. Allocation cannot intrinsically require a decision maker. An account must explain what distinguishes allocation from mere occurrence without borrowing intention when convenient.
14. **A discipline of description.** “Everything is resource allocation” may require every account to expose dependencies, exclusions, and enabling conditions. That can be useful without proving that allocation is the fundamental substance or structure of everything.

The first possibility gives a familiar relationship: there is an independently specified world of resources and feasible uses; a perspective represents it; changing perspective helps select a better distribution. Its strongest attraction is that error has an external object. Miscounting the available hours remains an error even if a different description feels liberating. Its limitation appears when the question concerns the formation of “an hour available for work.” An uninterrupted hour and sixty scattered minutes can have equal duration without supporting the same activity. Once this matters, allocation over a quantity of time is underdescribed. The inquiry must include interruption, dependency, and readiness in what it takes itself to be allocating.

That implication does not establish that the world is created by a description. The stronger alternative, combining possibilities three through seven, is that description, arrangement, and action can change the practical identities of what exists. The same room becomes shelter, a workplace, a meeting place, or unusable space through different relations. Some transitions require construction; others require recognition or permission. A representational account of perspective could explain how acquiring a distinction causally completes a usable relation. If it claims to explain these transformations, however, it owes that account rather than assuming that representing an already divided world explains how usable relations form. Likewise, an allocation account that treats resources as independently given atoms owes an explanation of how usability comes into existence if it claims to include such formation. Neither account is refuted merely by pointing to a changed relation.

The strongest incompatible rival says that every such change is simply a movement within one enormous, permanently fixed space of physical possibilities. On that account, new possibilities are merely possibilities that were previously unknown. This could be true at one level and insufficient at another. A complete inventory of physically possible events would not automatically identify which events constitute a promise, a usable method, or a resource for a particular undertaking. The reduction requires a derivation of those relations, not the assertion that physics must contain them. Conversely, their practical emergence does not prove that physical possibilities literally expanded. These claims must remain separate.

Possibilities ten through twelve introduce another transformation. If allocation determines who has standing to decide, the procedure evaluating an allocation is partly one of its consequences. Suppose a group allocates every speaking opportunity to members already regarded as competent. Their later apparent competence can reflect the opportunities that the rule distributed. A perspective formed within that arrangement can mistake an effect of allocation for independent justification of it. Resource allocation must then include the production of its own evidence and judges. Perspective must include the social conditions under which an account can become credible or even audible.

This does not justify erasing differences in competence. It defeats the inference from “this arrangement produced the currently recognized competence” to “the arrangement independently deserves its authority.” Establishing the latter requires additional grounds.

There are precise combinations available. Distribution among fixed uses is retained as a special case when identities and feasibility can reasonably be held constant. Capacity production and relational constitution combine when a changed relation creates a later ability. Jurisdiction and physical feasibility combine only if they remain distinguishable: a prohibition and a material impossibility can constrain the same act without being the same constraint. Calling all three “allocation” fails to explain their differences if it erases the distinctions needed for that explanation. This establishes a limit on explanatory adequacy; the truth of the universal identity remains unsettled.

What is established conditionally is that taking the identity seriously removes the exemption of resource formation, alternative formation, participant formation, and evaluative authority from allocation. The unresolved obstacle is an account precise enough to include these formations while excluding something a rival account would mistakenly include. If “allocation” imposes no discriminating commitments, the identity explains nothing; if it begins with fixed resources and uses, it omits the formation it was supposed to explain. A perfected account has not yet been obtained between those two failures.

Disposition: 1 retained for fixed conditions; its unrestricted adequacy eliminated. 2–5 and 7–8 are compatible contributions. 6 requires genuinely feasible alternatives. 9 remains restricted to living activity. 10–12 are compatible if their differences survive. 13 is required by literal universality but remains underdefined. 14 is a live rival to the metaphysical reading. Exhaustion is unproved.
