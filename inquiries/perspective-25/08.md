**8. Let relationships change what the subjects are.**

The mistaken division need not be an inaccurate map of otherwise settled territories. It may be the presumption that there must first be independently identifiable territories for relationships to connect. That presumption requires examination without the opposite presumption that everything must dissolve into undifferentiated connection.

Fifteen accounts compete over how units of inquiry acquire their identities.

1. **Intrinsic kinds.** A subject concerns things sharing properties independently of our interests. Relationships supply information about those things but do not constitute their identities.
2. **Common causal origin.** Things belong together because a shared process produces them. Superficially similar effects can belong to different inquiries when their origins differ.
3. **Intervention dependence.** A subject is what changes together under an explicitly specified intervention. The relevant boundary can differ for changing a cause and predicting an observation.
4. **Explanatory sufficiency.** A unit is the smallest inclusion of relations sufficient for a particular explanation. Changing what needs explaining can change the unit without anyone making an error.
5. **Persistence through transformation.** A subject is identified by what remains trackable through specified changes. This makes its identity depend on which transformations matter, rather than only its present properties.
6. **Joint constitution.** Creditor and debtor, teacher and pupil, or sender and recipient acquire those identities through relations. One cannot define all such participants completely before describing the relation.
7. **A history with consequences.** The present properties of two things may match while their histories support different expectations or obligations. Inquiry follows a lineage of dependence rather than a static class.
8. **Coordination for action.** A subject bundles what must be considered together to accomplish something. A discipline organized for teaching can therefore differ from one organized for building or repairing.
9. **Institutional inheritance.** Departmental boundaries preserve training, funding, and professional jurisdiction. Their durability does not by itself show that they reflect the deepest divisions in what is studied.
10. **Shared means of access.** Things are grouped because the same instrument or method reveals them. A new means of access can expose dependencies the earlier grouping systematically missed.
11. **Question-dependent individuation.** Asking what a thing is for, what produced it, or who can change it can pick out different boundaries. “The same subject” may conceal several incompatible individuations.
12. **Emergence across scale.** A pattern can have stable properties that its isolated participants lack. The relevant subject becomes an organized activity, while the participants remain relevant to other questions.
13. **Relations without privileged endpoints.** Begin with transformations, dependencies, or exchanges; identify stable participants through repeated relations. Objects are recovered from how they enter the activity rather than supplied beforehand.
14. **Standing and exclusion.** A boundary determines whose experience and consequences count. A subject described as “workplace productivity” can omit unpaid recovery or domestic labor by how it is initially delimited.
15. **Several legitimate partitions.** There may be no uniquely correct division independent of purpose. The ambition becomes exact knowledge of when each division is warranted and what translation between divisions preserves or loses.

Consider an inquiry beginning with “attention.” One branch treats attention as a property of an individual. Another examines tasks, interruptions, rewards, sleep, language, and social permission. Simply placing links from an attention node to six other nodes preserves the original decision about what the central object is. A stronger revision asks whether the activity to be explained is an achievement of person, task, environment, and timing together. If so, the unit changes. Some failures previously located inside the person now belong to a relation that no isolated person possesses.

This is not a license to deny individual differences. The intrinsic account has a strong alternative: environmental relations are inputs to a genuine individual capacity, and causal analysis can separate them. To choose between these accounts, specify the claim whose unit is disputed. If “attention” means a person's ability across a stated range of environments, the individual capacity may remain an appropriate object. If it means the sustained coordination actually occurring in one activity, the environment cannot be dismissed as an external input merely by definition. These accounts may concern different objects under one inherited word. Combining them requires distinguishing those objects, not averaging their descriptions.

The joint-constitution account goes further. “Teacher” and “pupil” are intelligible through activities of teaching and learning, even though the people exist independently. Thus the relationship can constitute a subject's relevant identity without creating its entire material existence. This defeats the false choice between self-contained objects and a world with no objects. It also shows why a catalog organized around already named participants can repeatedly miss the transformation it seeks: the next discovery may change what a participant is counted as doing.

The account beginning with relations has its own concealed commitment. A relation must be differentiated from other relations. If every relation is first defined by endpoints drawn from the inherited catalog, nothing has changed. But if relations are primitive without criteria of identity, the vocabulary has merely moved the unexplained boundary. A usable departure therefore needs cases of sameness across changing endpoints: the same dependency can persist after a person is replaced; the same person can enter a materially different dependency. This gives relational individuation actual work without declaring it universally prior.

The plural-partition account offers another route. A subject division is warranted relative to a question and an operation, while cross-subject relations disclose when that warrant ends. Its strongest limitation is dependence on the supplied question. If the initial question contains the central mistake, purpose-relative organization can preserve it perfectly. Consequently, question formation must be among the activities capable of altering subject boundaries. The inquiry cannot put its organizing aim outside the relations it is studying.

There are warranted local eliminations. A fixed taxonomy is inadequate as the sole organizer wherever the relevant identities change through relations it cannot represent. An unqualified “everything is connected” is inadequate wherever it cannot distinguish dependencies that change the answer from connections that do not. A single imposed relational ontology is also unearned if several different individuations explain different aspects without contradiction.

For subjectsystems, the concrete implication is that a discovery cannot always arrive as a new entry or link. It may invalidate the identity conditions of existing entries. To represent such a revision accurately, the record must preserve the original claim with its original meanings, represent the revised successor separately, and specify which commitments, if any, carry across the change. It should also be able to record that two subjects were one activity divided for convenience and distinguish disappearance of a boundary from loss of the distinctions it previously protected. Changing what a noun denotes does not by itself preserve the claim expressed with it. Without these distinctions, accumulated organization can become a veto on the discoveries it was designed to support.

What is established is that subjects can be products of inquiry and relations, not merely its inputs. What remains unresolved is a general basis for choosing or generating the right unit when neither the aim nor the admissible relations are already secure. Replacing the old catalog with a universal catalog of relations does not settle that question.

Disposition: 1–2 remain live for some subjects. 3–5, 7–8, and 10–12 are compatible means of individuation under specified conditions. 6 defeats mandatory independence of participants. 9 and 14 expose possible distortions. 13 remains a live foundational rival, not a universal conclusion. 15 is warranted locally; its sufficiency when aims change remains unresolved. Exhaustion is unproved.
