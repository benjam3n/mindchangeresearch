**7. Reconsider the matter without the five governing nouns.**

I will start with events and distinctions, rather than replace the forbidden terms with five new labels. Consider a meeting in which everyone receives five minutes. Someone concludes that everyone had an equal chance to contribute. Another participant cannot understand the technical language; another cannot speak without risking retaliation; another has evidence that becomes intelligible only after the meeting ends. Equal duration is true. The conclusion about equal participation does not follow. What must be examined is how duration acquired the authority to stand for participation.

Fourteen different accounts can begin here.

1. **Something happened, but nobody registered it.** A sound occurred; no change in subsequent conduct distinguished it from silence. The relevant difference is between occurrence and registration.
2. **Someone registered it without being able to identify it.** A listener noticed an unfamiliar expression but could not distinguish a warning from a joke. Registration does not supply intelligibility.
3. **Someone identified it without recognizing its bearing.** A participant understood every sentence but missed that a proposed rule would exclude them. Comprehension of sentences does not settle what follows for a life.
4. **Someone recognized its bearing without feeling its urgency.** A distant consequence can be understood and remain practically inert. Being able to state its importance and being moved by it are different achievements.
5. **Someone felt its urgency without being able to act.** Fear, exhaustion, lack of permission, or material dependence can prevent a response. Neither persuasion nor further explanation necessarily changes the relevant condition.
6. **Someone could act, but the act would mean something else to others.** Silence intended as refusal may be recorded as agreement. An act and its public uptake can diverge.
7. **The invitation changed the invitee.** Asking someone to speak as a representative can create responsibilities and loyalties that were absent before the invitation. A participant need not remain identical through participation.
8. **The first commitment altered what later commitments could mean.** A promise, followed by a refusal, differs from a refusal followed by a promise. The order does more than rearrange independently meaningful pieces.
9. **A comparison became possible only after a new distinction appeared.** “Everyone had five minutes” ceased to settle fairness when exposure to retaliation became expressible. A newly available distinction can change what earlier evidence supports.
10. **The available names suppressed a difference.** Calling every interruption “discussion” erased the difference between requesting clarification and preventing someone from finishing. A shared name can hide unequal acts.
11. **An absent participant changed what those present owed.** A decision concerning a child, an absent colleague, or a future inhabitant can carry consequences for someone who cannot enter the exchange. Presence and standing are different.
12. **A description made its own evidence.** Calling one participant unreliable caused others to discount their contributions, producing a record in which they apparently contributed little. The record cannot independently settle the description that helped create it.
13. **Two descriptions remained true while their connection failed.** Everyone spoke; some people were not heard. The error can lie in treating the first truth as sufficient for denying the second.
14. **No repair of the description could perform the missing act.** Correctly explaining that a door is locked does not open it. Understanding and changing what happens must remain distinguishable even where each enables the other.

The first strong continuation treats the meeting as a matter of transmission. A speaker has something to convey; the listeners need to receive and understand it. Its strongest form includes language differences, prior knowledge, interruption, and feedback. It is not defeated merely by showing that communication is complicated. It becomes inadequate when the intended content itself depends on what the exchange makes available. A participant may discover what they have to say only when another person names a distinction. There was no fully formed message waiting unchanged at the entrance.

The second continuation therefore treats the exchange as the formation of meanings and commitments. The important unit is an interaction through which something becomes sayable, binding, or contestable. On an account that treats a valid promise as undertaking an obligation, making it changes what the speaker owes; conveying an intention alone does not establish that undertaking. A rival can recognize changed expectations while disputing whether any independent obligation was created. The example therefore exposes competing accounts of what making a promise does; it does not settle their dispute. This continuation also has a limit. A commitment may be clearly formed while the person making it lacks food, access, or the ability to perform it. The enacted meaning does not create every enabling condition.

The third continuation begins with those enabling conditions. Who can remain awake, enter the room, tolerate the noise, understand the language, and speak without retaliation? Here changing a meeting can require changing bodies, schedules, ownership, or dependence. The claim is stronger than “context matters.” The described activity cannot occur without conditions that earlier descriptions treated as peripheral. Nevertheless, reducing the entire exchange to enabling conditions would omit differences between a request, a threat, and a promise made under identical lighting and nutrition. Material support is necessary for these acts without being a complete account of their identities.

These continuations can be combined where the dependency is specified. A newly expressible distinction changes a commitment; the commitment changes permission; permission makes an act possible; the act changes what others can observe. They cannot be combined by assuming that every difference is one more example of the same unnamed thing. A difference between understanding a warning and possessing an exit remains essential.

The strongest rival to this reconstruction is that all these distinctions were available in the original vocabulary and nothing substantial was gained by withholding it. That objection can be right. The value of withholding a word is not magical access to what language concealed. It is the removal of a shortcut that previously allowed an inference to pass without specifying its terms. If equal time never counted as equal participation, the example reveals no such gain. If it did, the exact improvement is the defeat of that substitution.

The withheld nouns may return only after their work can be stated without them. Does a word distinguish registration from understanding, join them under an explicit relation, or silently collapse them? Does it identify what must change, or merely announce that change is desirable? This requirement also remains revisable; vocabulary sometimes permits discoveries that cannot initially be paraphrased without loss. Provisional use can therefore precede complete clarification, while the word receives no exemption from subsequent examination.

What has become available is a set of noninterchangeable transitions: occurrence, registration, intelligibility, bearing, urgency, ability, act, uptake, and obligation. They need not form one fixed sequence; an obligation can precede understanding, and an act can reveal its own significance afterward. The remaining obstacle is to explain which transitions are required in a particular case and why, without assuming that this list already contains every way something can begin to matter.

Disposition: 1–6 survive as distinguishable occurrences. 7–12 survive as possible changes to participants, meanings, and available evidence. 13 defeats the inference from separate truths to adequate connection. 14 defeats the sufficiency of description for action. These accounts combine only where their relations are specified. Their collective completeness remains unproved.
