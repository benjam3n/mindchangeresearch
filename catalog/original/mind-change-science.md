# Mind-change science: a conditional recipe library

**72 change targets · 100 attempt types · 300 recipe variants · 16 ways gains can compound**

**A recipe needs two specifications: what it must produce, and why that result is worth producing.** Perfectly producing the wrong result is an objective failure. Producing nothing useful after correctly following the instructions is a recipe failure. Neither can be repaired by praising the cook's execution.

The practical unit in this catalog is a **conditional procedure**: given an identifiable starting condition, perform specified operations to produce a defined change; if a necessary condition is absent, take a specified repair, alternative, or stop branch. Adaptation belongs inside the recipe wherever it can be specified. The cook should not have to invent the central causal steps.

The previous 72 targets and 100 adverse routes are retained. Every target now has a protocol, alternative recipe links, and an outcome check. Every attempt has three distinct variants with use conditions, ordered operations, and failure handling. These are a substantial design library, **not 300 experimentally validated interventions**. The cited research supports particular ingredients and boundaries; newly composed procedures remain proposals until evaluated in their intended settings.

<a id="execution-standard"></a>
## What makes a recipe executable and worth executing

| Requirement | What must be specified before relying on the recipe | What failure means |
|---|---|---|
| Worthwhile destination | The capability, experience, judgment, or action that should improve; whose values determine improvement; material costs. | An impeccably produced result may still be useless. Change or retire the target. |
| Applicable starting conditions | Necessary knowledge, resources, opportunity, motivation, context, and support level. Use visible context before asking questions. | Route to the missing prerequisite or another procedure. Inapplicability is not successful mind change. |
| Sufficient operations | Actual transformations, their inputs, and why their outputs enable the next step. | Replace a missing or invalid operation. More conscientious execution cannot supply an absent causal link. |
| Observable effect | An observation that would indicate the intended change independently of following instructions or agreeing with the communicator. | Record ineffective or unverified delivery, even when every step was completed. |
| Appropriate horizon | Immediate usefulness, later retention, transfer, independence, or repeated action—whichever the actual goal requires. | Report the narrower gain. Do not infer durability or transfer from immediate performance. |

**Instantiate before delivery.** These catalog recipes are reusable procedures, not finished messages for every possible person. Fill their references with the actual claim, goal, contrast, cue, constraint, operation, and evidence. For example, “identify the missing prerequisite” must become “the person cannot compare these rates because the denominators differ; convert both to outcomes per 1,000 comparable cases.” If the executor cannot name the missing operation, the recipe is not ready to be executed.

**Select a route by the observed obstruction.** Someone who already knows and values an action may need access or a cue. Someone who has access but rejects the goal needs a goal discussion. Someone who can repeat a rule but cannot recognize its use conditions needs discrimination or transfer practice. The selected intervention must address the actual missing transition; do not run every potentially relevant intervention in sequence.

**Use the least burdensome sufficient evidence.** Existing context, an artifact, a naturally occurring action, or a resolved outcome may establish what is needed. Questions and performance checks are operations with costs, not obligatory rituals. Ask only when the answer changes the route. Do the substantive work yourself when user participation adds no necessary learning or information.

**Separate fidelity from efficacy.** Fidelity asks whether the specified operations were performed correctly. Efficacy asks whether those operations produced the intended effect. “The person engaged with the exercise” answers the first question. It cannot answer the second. In a randomized gym study, participants engaged with a planning exercise but it did not increase attendance. A different field experiment found a benefit from a specific date-and-time planning prompt for a one-time action. The implication is conditional design, not a universal verdict for or against planning. [Carrera et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30336306/), [Milkman et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21670283/)

**Preserve the outcome vector.** An attempt can improve factual accuracy while decreasing trust, increase effort while worsening the chosen goal, or improve assisted output while reducing independent learning. Choose the primary target and any material secondary effects beforehand. Do not replace the whole evaluation with agreement, satisfaction, surprise, or one convenient score. These can be legitimate outcomes when they are what the person actually wants; they do not certify other changes.

**Use an explicit stop branch.** Stop when the requested result is complete, when the target has no defensible value, when necessary conditions cannot be supplied, or when further effort is not justified. Successful routing away from a bad attempt is useful decision making; it is still not evidence that the original intended mind change occurred.

## What can actually be made nearly certain

Some operations permit a logical guarantee under explicit premises: a valid calculation returns the stated quantity; a genuine counterexample disproves a universal claim; a completed dependency check can show that a necessary input is absent. The premises, data, and implementation still require scrutiny. The recipient's attention, understanding, acceptance, motivation, and later use are additional transitions.

For a psychological effect, “near guaranteed” is a claim about measured reliability in a defined domain. It requires a stated outcome, eligible population and context, time horizon, uncertainty around the success rate, and evidence beyond the cases used to design the recipe. Merely adding branches does not establish that reliability.

A numerical illustration explains why this matters: if five required transitions each succeed with probability 95% **conditional on all preceding transitions succeeding**, the probability of all five succeeding is 0.95⁵, about 77.4%. This is an illustrative calculation, not a psychological estimate; no independence assumption is needed because the rates are conditional. Reliability improves by repairing weak transitions, reducing unnecessary transitions, or choosing a result achievable through fewer uncertain responses.

That last option can change the design substantially. If the valued result is a correct comparison, an external representation can sometimes make the comparison easy enough that the person no longer needs a major increase in motivation, memory, or calculating skill. If independent skill is the goal, that substitution is insufficient and the recipe must develop the skill. **The same intervention can be excellent or inadequate depending on the destination.**

## Four instantiated examples

### 1. Correct a probability judgment by changing its representation

**Situation.** In an illustrative batch of 10,000 messages, 100 are spam. A filter flags 90% of spam and 5% of nonspam. Someone treats the 90% detection rate as the probability that a flagged message is spam. The desired change is correct interpretation of a positive signal.

**Recipe, using A85a and A11a.** First, preserve the same underlying population. Second, compute flagged spam: 90 of 100. Third, compute flagged nonspam: 495 of 9,900. Fourth, compare the relevant joint counts: 90 spam messages among 585 flagged messages, or approximately 15.4%.

| Among 10,000 messages | Flagged | Not flagged |
|---|---:|---:|
| Spam: 100 | 90 | 10 |
| Nonspam: 9,900 | 495 | 9,405 |
| Total | 585 | 9,415 |

**Why this route fits.** It supplies the missing denominator and removes much of the mental transformation. Natural-frequency representations have improved probabilistic reasoning in studied tasks; this exact illustration and four-step procedure are our construction. [Hoffrage et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11188724/)

**Branch.** If the rates refer to different populations, resolve comparability before calculating. If the person already understands the denominator, omit the lesson and supply the relevant implication. If independent transferable understanding is required, use a fresh case with a different prevalence; correct reading of this table alone does not demonstrate it.

**What is assured.** The arithmetic consequence follows from the stated numbers. The person's comprehension and later transfer remain separate outcomes.

### 2. Improve initiation only after locating its cause

**Situation.** A person has already said that producing a one-page project brief is worthwhile, knows the required content, and has the information. They repeatedly postpone starting because the task has no defined first operation. Desired result: a usable brief begun, with a clear continuation path.

**Recipe, using A55a, A74a, and A54a.** First, create the brief's three required fields: decision, supporting reason, and unresolved dependency. Second, put the already available information beside those fields. Third, specify the first operation as filling the decision field in one sentence. Fourth, attach that operation to an actual available work period, followed by the supporting reason and unresolved dependency.

**Branch.** If the decision cannot yet be stated, the obstacle is missing analysis; resolve its controlling uncertainty instead. If no work period or access exists, change resources or scope. If the person no longer values the brief, reconsider the target. If a cue is noticed but action still does not start, do not merely make the cue louder.

**What counts.** A useful first field exists and supports continuing the brief. A detailed schedule or expressed enthusiasm is not that result. Planning evidence is mixed across contexts; these branches are proposed diagnoses and repairs, not a validated universal initiation treatment. [Carrera et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30336306/), [Milkman et al.](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21670283/)

### 3. Develop discrimination instead of repeating a definition

**Situation.** A learner can define “necessary” and “sufficient” but confuses them in new arguments. Desired result: correctly distinguish the two in unfamiliar cases.

**Recipe, using A65a, A65c, and A64c.** First, present the relation “being a square implies being a rectangle.” Second, supply a rectangle with unequal adjacent sides, establishing that being a rectangle does not imply being a square. Third, map the arrows to the distinction: rectangle is necessary for square; square is sufficient for rectangle. Fourth, apply the relation to a different domain with an explicit rule, such as access requiring both a valid credential and an active account: either alone is necessary; their conjunction is sufficient under that rule.

**Branch.** If the learner cannot interpret implication, teach that prerequisite. If the error concerns a hidden extra condition, add it to the rule. If only the geometric example works, vary context while preserving the logical relation. Comparing cases has supported transfer in studied domains; this particular instructional sequence remains a designed example. [Gentner et al.](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/GentnerLoewensteinThompson03.pdf)

**What counts.** The learner correctly identifies the relationship in an unseen case and handles a counterexample. Repeating the two definitions is insufficient.

### 4. Reject an excellent route to a useless conversational result

**Situation.** A response accurately paraphrases a request the user already knows was understood. Desired result: improve what the user can infer, decide, want, trust, or do.

**Recipe, using A21c, A82c, and A92a.** First, assume the paraphrase is perfectly accurate. Second, identify what new capability or justified update it would create. Third, if none exists, remove it as the main deliverable. Fourth, use the accepted premise to derive an unused consequence: an adequate recipe library must evaluate the worth of its destination independently from the reliability of reaching it.

**Branch.** If ambiguity actually prevents useful work, a narrow paraphrase has instrumental value; resolve it and immediately continue. If the consequence is already known, supply the next unresolved operation—in this case, the conditional recipe library itself.

**What counts.** A previously unavailable distinction, usable procedure, warranted update, or completed result is delivered. Accurate acknowledgment alone cannot meet that target. This is a design argument applied to the conversation, not a claim that paraphrase never helps.

### Knowledge and uncertainty

<a id="t1"></a>
#### T1. Factual beliefs

**Improvement sought:** Greater factual accuracy at the relevant scope, including abandoning unsupported certainty.

**Protocol:** Isolate the exact proposition; identify the best available direct evidence; replace or narrow only the unsupported part; carry the correction into its consequential inference.

**Choose by condition:** [A11a: Correct one factual proposition](#a11a); [A11c: Narrow a partly true claim](#a11c); [A15a: Lead with a discriminating observation](#a15a).

**Outcome check and redirect:** A fresh use of the fact is correct and preserves its qualification. If assent changes but use does not, address retrieval or the dependent inference.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S4](#s4).

<a id="t2"></a>
#### T2. Causal explanations

**Improvement sought:** An explanation accounts for observations and makes discriminating predictions without invented causal links.

**Protocol:** Write the proposed chain of causes; mark observed and inferred links; derive a prediction separating a plausible rival; revise the chain using available discriminating evidence.

**Choose by condition:** [A18a: Trace the proposed cause to the outcome](#a18a); [A18b: Compare rival mechanisms](#a18b); [A23b: Test a common cause](#a23b).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The explanation survives a relevant new observation or is explicitly bounded where no discrimination is available. Fluent storytelling alone is insufficient.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S7](#s7).

<a id="t3"></a>
#### T3. Predictions

**Improvement sought:** Forecasts become better calibrated and more discriminating across comparable resolved events.

**Protocol:** Define the event and resolution date; start from a relevant reference class; adjust for independent case evidence; record the forecast before resolution and compare it with outcomes.

**Choose by condition:** [A28a: Forecast a defined event](#a28a); [A28b: Predict from competing mechanisms](#a28b); [A46b: Calibrate confidence](#a46b).

**Outcome check and redirect:** Evaluate a series of comparable forecasts with an appropriate score and calibration check. One correct prediction does not establish calibration.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S11](#s11).

<a id="t4"></a>
#### T4. Uncertainty ranges

**Improvement sought:** The uncertainty range represents defensible possibilities at a stated coverage level or clearly stated qualitative scope.

**Protocol:** Define the quantity; distinguish variation from missing knowledge; construct the range from relevant data or explicit assumptions; show whether choices differ across that range.

**Choose by condition:** [A14a: Partition the account](#a14a); [A14b: Perform sensitivity analysis](#a14b); [A28c: Find the decision boundary](#a28c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** Check observed coverage over repeated comparable cases when possible. Otherwise label the range's basis; widen or change the model if it excludes credible possibilities.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S11](#s11), [S14](#s14).

<a id="t5"></a>
#### T5. Confidence in a particular claim

**Improvement sought:** Confidence moves toward evidential support, upward or downward as warranted.

**Protocol:** Separate the claim from how certain it feels; trace the independent evidence; identify its strongest limitation; state the supported confidence and what would change it.

**Choose by condition:** [A13b: Separate a strong finding from a tentative explanation](#a13b); [A15b: Combine independent contributions](#a15b); [A77c: Calibrate uncertainty](#a77c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** Confidence responds to new evidence rather than repetition or presentation strength. Without repeated resolved cases, personal calibration remains only partly known.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S10](#s10), [S11](#s11), [S14](#s14).

<a id="t6"></a>
#### T6. Recognition of what is unknown

**Improvement sought:** Unknowns are localized so they neither contaminate established knowledge nor disappear behind certainty.

**Protocol:** List the conclusion's necessary inputs; mark each established, inferred, or missing; distinguish obtainable missing inputs from presently unresolved ones; pursue only unknowns consequential to the task.

**Choose by condition:** [A14c: Separate obtainable and unresolved uncertainty](#a14c); [A2b: Resolve decision-sensitive uncertainty](#a2b); [A59b: Stop when information cannot change the choice](#a59b).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The person can state what remains unknown and what can still be concluded or done. If everything becomes doubtful, restore the established premises.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

### Evidence and trust

<a id="t7"></a>
#### T7. Reliability assigned to a source

**Improvement sought:** Reliance on a source tracks relevant accuracy and independence rather than prestige or familiarity.

**Protocol:** Identify the exact claim being entrusted; inspect relevant expertise and original evidence; check independent corroboration where consequential; assign reliance at that claim's scope.

**Choose by condition:** [A31a: Scope expertise](#a31a); [A31b: Corroborate independently](#a31b); [A16c: Trace a citation chain to origin](#a16c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** Trust distinguishes supported from unsupported claims by the same source. If overall liking changes alone, source calibration has not been demonstrated.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S10](#s10).

<a id="t8"></a>
#### T8. Scope of a source's expertise

**Improvement sought:** Expertise is neither generalized beyond its domain nor dismissed within it without reason.

**Protocol:** Specify the task requiring expertise; compare it with the source's actual demonstrated work; identify transferable and missing competencies; seek another source for consequential gaps.

**Choose by condition:** [A31a: Scope expertise](#a31a); [A16b: Audit the source's applicability](#a16b); [A31c: Use a relevant track record](#a31c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** A new out-of-domain claim receives different scrutiny from an in-domain claim. If no relevant record exists, preserve uncertainty rather than manufacture a rating.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t9"></a>
#### T9. Trust in a method

**Improvement sought:** Trust in a method depends on its operating conditions, comparative results, and failure modes.

**Protocol:** Define the result the method promises; inspect evidence in comparable settings; distinguish completion from effect; specify when to use, adapt, or reject the method.

**Choose by condition:** [A95a: Match the causal conditions](#a95a); [A95b: Detect changed circumstances](#a95b); [A83c: Stress the execution path](#a83c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** A method is retained after relevant success and revised when its premises fail. One satisfying outcome does not establish general reliability.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S15](#s15), [S20](#s20), [S23](#s23).

<a id="t10"></a>
#### T10. Trust in one's own judgment

**Improvement sought:** Self-trust becomes domain-specific and responsive to actual performance.

**Protocol:** Identify the class of judgments; compare past confidence with independently resolved outcomes; separate skill from favorable circumstances; adjust reliance and verification for this task class.

**Choose by condition:** [A46b: Calibrate confidence](#a46b); [A46a: Use representative mastery evidence](#a46a); [A94a: Observe transfer](#a94a).

**Outcome check and redirect:** Appropriate independent action and selective checking improve together. If confidence rises without relevant evidence, use representative performance before reassurance.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S11](#s11), [S2](#s2).

<a id="t11"></a>
#### T11. Threshold for updating

**Improvement sought:** The threshold for revising a claim reflects evidence strength; the threshold for acting also reflects consequences.

**Protocol:** Separate belief revision from action choice; specify what evidence would materially change support; specify which support level would change action under the person's priorities; update each threshold for its own reason.

**Choose by condition:** [A28c: Find the decision boundary](#a28c); [A77a: Reopen a controlling premise](#a77a); [A53c: Locate sensitive judgments](#a53c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** New evidence can change confidence without forcing action, and changed stakes can change action without altering factual belief. If these are conflated, separate them explicitly.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t12"></a>
#### T12. Willingness to verify

**Improvement sought:** Verification is used where it can prevent a meaningful error at reasonable cost.

**Protocol:** Identify the decision-sensitive claim; estimate the consequence of error; locate the cheapest sufficiently independent check; verify and use the result before the costly commitment.

**Choose by condition:** [A56b: Place a consequential checkpoint](#a56b); [A93c: Choose the cheapest decisive operation](#a93c); [A16a: Bind each consequential claim to support](#a16a).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The check changes or supports the decision on new evidence. If it cannot affect the result, remove it unless a required gate independently justifies it.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

### Attention

<a id="t13"></a>
#### T13. What gets noticed

**Improvement sought:** Attention detects previously missed information that matters to the task.

**Protocol:** Identify a consequential missed signal; contrast a case containing it with one without it; place a cue at the natural encounter point; connect noticing the signal to the relevant response.

**Choose by condition:** [A65a: Construct a minimal pair](#a65a); [A72a: Introduce a meaningful contrast](#a72a); [A70a: Bind recall to an event](#a70a).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The signal is noticed in a fresh relevant case without swamping attention with false alarms. If detection works but response fails, change the action route.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t14"></a>
#### T14. What gets ignored

**Improvement sought:** Irrelevant input receives less attention while consequential exceptions remain visible.

**Protocol:** Name the distracting stream; separate information that can change the task from routine noise; suppress or defer the latter; preserve a route for the former.

**Choose by condition:** [A73a: Protect a coherent work unit](#a73a); [A73c: Batch routine checks](#a73c); [A72b: Reduce excess stimulation](#a72b).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The actual task gains continuity without material missed events. If important information is lost, narrow the filter.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t15"></a>
#### T15. Relative salience

**Improvement sought:** Salience reflects relevance and consequence rather than vividness alone.

**Protocol:** List the factors controlling the outcome; compare their evidential and practical importance; put the decisive factor in a prominent comparable representation; attach vivid examples only within their representative scope.

**Choose by condition:** [A24b: Restore an omitted cost](#a24b); [A25b: Restore the distribution](#a25b); [A85a: Choose a table or spatial mapping](#a85a).

**Outcome check and redirect:** A choice or explanation gives appropriate weight to the controlling factor. If vivid exceptions dominate, restore base rates and the distribution.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S19](#s19).

<a id="t16"></a>
#### T16. Duration of attention

**Improvement sought:** Attention lasts long enough to complete a useful unit and stops when continuation loses value.

**Protocol:** Define one coherent work unit; match it to an available interval; remove demonstrated interruptions; finish or checkpoint before reassessing whether to continue.

**Choose by condition:** [A73a: Protect a coherent work unit](#a73a); [A71c: Allocate reflection by decision value](#a71c); [A80c: Fit challenge to available capacity](#a80c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** Useful work is completed with tolerable cost. Longer sitting alone is not improvement; if progress stalls, inspect prerequisites and task size.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t17"></a>
#### T17. Conditions for switching attention

**Improvement sought:** Switching responds to meaningful completion, invalidation, or urgency rather than every incoming stimulus.

**Protocol:** Define the current work boundary; name exceptions that warrant immediate switching; capture nonurgent arrivals; switch at the boundary or when a real exception occurs.

**Choose by condition:** [A73b: Capture without switching](#a73b); [A73c: Batch routine checks](#a73c); [A76c: Review strategy at a boundary](#a76c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** Necessary switches occur while avoidable fragmentation declines. If the rule delays consequential information, refine the exception.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t18"></a>
#### T18. What stays available in the background

**Improvement sought:** Important background information remains recoverable without continuously occupying attention.

**Protocol:** Identify information needed intermittently; encode it in a short accurate external reference; attach a recognizable use cue; retrieve it only when the task reaches that condition.

**Choose by condition:** [A70a: Bind recall to an event](#a70a); [A97c: Separate core from recoverable detail](#a97c); [A60a: Externalize a difficult representation](#a60a).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The information is available at use and does not require constant rehearsal. If cues fail, change their placement; if content is wrong, repair the reference.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

### Representation

<a id="t19"></a>
#### T19. Distinctions

**Improvement sought:** A distinction separates cases that require different inferences or actions.

**Protocol:** Choose a currently confused pair; isolate the consequential differing feature; derive why it changes the result; apply the distinction to a new case.

**Choose by condition:** [A65a: Construct a minimal pair](#a65a); [A8b: Split an overloaded term](#a8b); [A6a: Construct a minimal pair](#a6a).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The person handles a near-match correctly and can state the governing difference. If only the label is learned, return to the contrast.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S12](#s12), [S13](#s13).

<a id="t20"></a>
#### T20. Categories

**Improvement sought:** Categories support reliable recognition without erasing material exceptions.

**Protocol:** Collect representative members and neighboring nonmembers; identify the governing features or relations; compare mixed cases; retain exceptions affecting use.

**Choose by condition:** [A63b: Interleave confusable cases](#a63b); [A23c: Unify with exceptions](#a23c); [A65c: Defeat a misleading resemblance](#a65c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** Unseen cases are classified accurately at the required scope. If surface resemblance dominates, compare structurally matching and misleading examples.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S13](#s13).

<a id="t21"></a>
#### T21. Boundaries

**Improvement sought:** Boundaries specify where a claim, category, or procedure changes validity.

**Protocol:** Write the current rule; locate cases on either side of its proposed limit; identify the condition responsible; express the boundary and unresolved borderline region.

**Choose by condition:** [A65b: Locate a boundary](#a65b); [A17b: Repair the boundary](#a17b); [A5b: Expose a boundary](#a5b).

**Outcome check and redirect:** Near-boundary cases receive the appropriate rule or uncertainty. If the boundary is conventional, success means using the convention accurately, not discovering a natural law.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t22"></a>
#### T22. Level of detail

**Improvement sought:** Detail matches the operation the person needs to perform.

**Protocol:** Specify that operation; identify information required to perform it correctly; remove redundant detail; restore any missing premise or exception that changes the result.

**Choose by condition:** [A4c: Separate the usable core from detail](#a4c); [A5a: Repair a missing inference](#a5a); [A97c: Separate core from recoverable detail](#a97c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The representation supports the intended inference or action with lower effort. If compression changes the result, restore the lost distinction.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t23"></a>
#### T23. Connections among ideas

**Improvement sought:** Connections among ideas support justified inference and transfer.

**Protocol:** Align the functional roles across ideas; identify a shared relation or dependency; distinguish analogy from established causation; apply the connection to a new relevant problem.

**Choose by condition:** [A7a: Map relationships explicitly](#a7a); [A23a: Extract a relation](#a23a); [A64b: Expose an invariant](#a64b).

#### T67. Interpretation of difficulty

**Improvement sought:** Difficulty is interpreted according to its cause and actual effect on learning or work.

**Protocol:** Locate the difficult operation; distinguish missing knowledge, useful practice demand, excessive load, and an unsuitable objective; inspect actual performance; select instruction, practice, simplification, or goal revision accordingly.

**Choose by condition:** [A80a: Repair prerequisite difficulty](#a80a); [A80c: Fit challenge to available capacity](#a80c); [A100a: Separate experience from performance](#a100a).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The interpretation predicts which repair helps. Neither ease nor struggle by itself establishes learning or incapacity.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S1](#s1), [S2](#s2), [S8](#s8).

<a id="t68"></a>
#### T68. Recognition of an error

**Improvement sought:** Consequential errors are detected and distinguished from uncertainty or disagreement about standards.

**Protocol:** Specify the external standard or intended result; compare the actual output; locate the first material mismatch; determine whether the cause is an error, missing evidence, or a disputed criterion.

**Choose by condition:** [A66a: Repair the controlling error](#a66a); [A35b: Repair the generating rule](#a35b); [A99a: Anchor revisions externally](#a99a).

**Outcome check and redirect:** A real error is identified accurately and repaired. If the benchmark is wrong or unclear, fix it rather than rewarding agreement with it.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t69"></a>
#### T69. Choice of reasoning strategy

**Improvement sought:** The reasoning strategy fits the operation needed to resolve the present problem.

**Protocol:** Classify the missing operation as calculation, causal inference, comparison, retrieval, or generation; check the strategy's prerequisites; select a matching procedure; change strategy when a specific validity condition fails.

**Choose by condition:** [A30a: Distinguish knowing from being able](#a30a); [A95c: Recombine components](#a95c); [A85a: Choose a table or spatial mapping](#a85a).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The strategy supplies the required output at justified cost. If effort repeats without new information or inference, change the operation rather than its wording.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t70"></a>
#### T70. What gets monitored

**Improvement sought:** Monitoring focuses on signals that can trigger a useful correction.

**Protocol:** Identify a consequential failure mode; select its most informative observable signal; place the check at a natural boundary; attach a specific repair to the signal.

**Choose by condition:** [A76a: Monitor a specific warning sign](#a76a); [A76b: Keep a minimal error record](#a76b); [A76c: Review strategy at a boundary](#a76c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The signal detects actionable problems with tolerable false alarms and burden. If nothing changes when it is measured, retire or redesign the measure.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="t71"></a>
#### T71. Standard used to judge improvement

**Improvement sought:** The improvement standard measures the valued result separately from appealing proxies.

**Protocol:** Specify what should become possible or better; distinguish execution, subjective experience, immediate effect, and durable capability; choose the observation matching that target; preserve important costs in the judgment.

**Choose by condition:** [A100a: Separate experience from performance](#a100a); [A94b: Observe a decision consequence](#a94b); [A21c: Reject a perfectly executed useless aim](#a21c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** A method can be judged ineffective despite praise or perfect execution, and useful despite little excitement. If the measure rewards a useless outcome, change the target and record why.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S1](#s1), [S2](#s2), [S15](#s15), [S21](#s21).

<a id="t72"></a>
#### T72. Procedure for revising that standard

**Improvement sought:** Standards are revised for a defensible change in goals, evidence, or measurement validity, without erasing prior failure.

**Protocol:** Record the existing standard and its purpose; identify the new evidence or goal change; test whether the revision still tracks endorsed value; apply the revised standard prospectively while preserving prior results under the old one.

**Choose by condition:** [A35b: Repair the generating rule](#a35b); [A97a: Preserve decision structure](#a97a); [A99c: Preserve a recoverable revision history](#a99c).

**Outcome check and redirect:** The revision improves relevance or validity and cannot simply convert a failed outcome into a claimed success. If only convenience motivates the change, retain the original judgment.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

### Attention and presentation · A1–A10

<a id="a1"></a>
#### A1. Repeat something familiar

**Adverse route:** Boredom or irritation makes the person disengage from relevant later material.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The familiar material produces a new usable inference, warranted confidence revision, or successful use.

<a id="a1a"></a>
**A1a. Derive an unused consequence**

**Use when:** A proposition is already accepted but a relevant implication is unused.

1. Write the accepted proposition
2. Attach one presently relevant condition
3. Derive the consequence without restating background
4. Apply that consequence to the live question.

**Redirect:** If the consequence is already available, select a different unresolved dependency rather than rewording it.

<a id="a1b"></a>
**A1b. Recalibrate justified confidence**

**Use when:** The claim is known but its evidential strength is misunderstood.

1. Identify the claimed confidence and reason
2. Add independent confirming or limiting evidence
3. Revise the supported range
4. State which choice the revision affects.

**Redirect:** If no new evidential distinction exists, omit the repetition.

<a id="a1c"></a>
**A1c. Recover knowledge at the moment of use**

**Use when:** The person knows the rule but fails to retrieve it in a relevant situation.

1. Identify the recognizable use condition
2. Pair it with a short accurate cue
3. Apply the rule to the current case
4. Preserve the exception with the cue.

**Redirect:** If the rule is retrieved but still unused, diagnose relevance or action constraints rather than memory.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a2"></a>
#### A2. Choose a topic

**Adverse route:** A relevant-sounding tangent displaces the issue that actually matters.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The selected issue has a defensible route to a valuable change in the present situation.

<a id="a2a"></a>
**A2a. Find the blocking dependency**

**Use when:** Progress is blocked despite substantial available knowledge.

1. Name the desired next transition
2. List its necessary prerequisites
3. Identify the first missing prerequisite from available evidence
4. Address that prerequisite before neighboring topics.

**Redirect:** If no prerequisite is missing, examine the value of the selected transition.

<a id="a2b"></a>
**A2b. Resolve decision-sensitive uncertainty**

**Use when:** A real choice depends on something unsettled.

1. List feasible choices
2. Name the facts that could reverse their ordering
3. Separate obtainable from unavailable facts
4. Address the cheapest consequential uncertainty.

**Redirect:** If no uncertainty changes the choice, help implement or revisit the objective.

<a id="a2c"></a>
**A2c. Correct a mistaken question**

**Use when:** The literal question presupposes a disputed condition.

1. Write the presupposition and its consequence
2. Check whether it holds
3. Reformulate the question with the corrected condition
4. Carry through the implication for the user's original concern.

**Redirect:** If the presupposition is sound, retain the original question.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a3"></a>
#### A3. Open with background

**Adverse route:** The person leaves before encountering the decisive material.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The recipient reaches the decisive point with the prerequisites needed to interpret it.

<a id="a3a"></a>
**A3a. Insert one prerequisite**

**Use when:** One missing concept prevents the main inference.

1. Write the main conclusion
2. Identify the one term or relation it depends on
3. Supply a minimal example of that prerequisite
4. Immediately use it in the main inference.

**Redirect:** If several prerequisites are missing, order them by dependency and provide a short route through them.

<a id="a3b"></a>
**A3b. Start at the expert's unresolved edge**

**Use when:** Existing context shows that basic background is already understood.

1. List the established starting facts privately
2. Delete explanations of them
3. Introduce the first unresolved relation
4. Expand only the part that cannot be inferred from the established facts.

**Redirect:** If prior knowledge is uncertain and changes the route, resolve that specific uncertainty.

<a id="a3c"></a>
**A3c. State the disputed premise first**

**Use when:** Background itself contains the contested assumption.

1. Name the disputed premise
2. Distinguish conditional reasoning from endorsement
3. Show what follows if it holds and if it fails
4. Pursue the branch supported by available evidence.

**Redirect:** If neither branch can be selected, preserve the conditional result.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a4"></a>
#### A4. Compress an explanation

**Adverse route:** A missing qualification produces a confident misunderstanding.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The shorter representation preserves the inferences, qualifications, and actions needed for the task.

<a id="a4a"></a>
**A4a. Remove inferential duplicates**

**Use when:** Several passages do the same work.

1. Assign each passage its distinct contribution
2. Merge passages with the same contribution
3. Retain one concrete demonstration
4. Check that the conclusion still follows.

**Redirect:** If deleting a passage breaks an inference, restore the missing relationship in fewer words.

<a id="a4b"></a>
**A4b. Replace prose with a relation**

**Use when:** A verbal sequence hides a simple comparison or dependency.

1. Extract the entities and relationship
2. Choose a table, equation, or short ordered sequence
3. Encode every consequential condition
4. Apply the representation to one relevant case.

**Redirect:** If symbols require more explanation than the original, keep the clearer prose.

<a id="a4c"></a>
**A4c. Separate the usable core from detail**

**Use when:** Different readers need different levels of support.

1. Write the conclusion with its scope
2. Place the minimum supporting reason beside it
3. Move optional evidence beneath it
4. Label what extra question each optional detail resolves.

**Redirect:** If the core invites a foreseeable misreading, move its missing qualification into the core.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a5"></a>
#### A5. Add detail

**Adverse route:** Processing demands exceed what the person can use.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The added detail repairs a named gap without introducing avoidable cognitive work.

<a id="a5a"></a>
**A5a. Repair a missing inference**

**Use when:** The recipient can follow everything except one transition.

1. Locate the last understood step and first unsupported step
2. Expose the operation connecting them
3. Work it on the actual case
4. Return to the main argument.

**Redirect:** If the operation requires another prerequisite, teach that prerequisite before repeating the step.

<a id="a5b"></a>
**A5b. Expose a boundary**

**Use when:** A statement is being applied too broadly.

1. State the rule's conditions
2. Show a matching and a near-matching nonmatching case
3. Identify the condition that changes the result
4. Restate the bounded rule.

**Redirect:** If the cases differ in several consequential ways, construct a cleaner pair.

<a id="a5c"></a>
**A5c. Add decision-relevant precision**

**Use when:** A vague quantity or condition changes what should be done.

1. Identify the threshold separating actions
2. Refine only the quantities that could cross it
3. Show the remaining uncertainty
4. Select the action robust to that uncertainty.

**Redirect:** If precision cannot affect the action, omit it.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a6"></a>
#### A6. Use a vivid example

**Adverse route:** An exceptional case distorts judgments of what is typical.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The example changes the intended distinction without supporting a false generalization.

<a id="a6a"></a>
**A6a. Construct a minimal pair**

**Use when:** Two conditions are repeatedly confused.

1. Choose two cases identical on irrelevant features
2. Vary the decisive feature
3. Derive the different outcomes
4. State exactly which distinction the pair establishes.

**Redirect:** If the causal difference is ambiguous, simplify the pair further.

<a id="a6b"></a>
**A6b. Use a representative case with context**

**Use when:** The recipient needs a concrete sense of an ordinary process.

1. Choose a case justified as relevant to the target population
2. Show the process step by step
3. Provide the frequency or range needed to interpret it
4. Separate illustration from prevalence evidence.

**Redirect:** If representativeness is unknown, label the case illustrative and avoid frequency conclusions.

<a id="a6c"></a>
**A6c. Use an existence counterexample**

**Use when:** An absolute impossibility or universal claim blocks thought.

1. Quote the exact universal claim
2. Verify a case satisfying its premises
3. Show the conflicting outcome
4. Revise only the defeated quantifier or condition.

**Redirect:** If the case violates a premise, it is not a valid counterexample; replace it.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a7"></a>
#### A7. Use an analogy

**Adverse route:** The person transfers a feature that the two situations do not share.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The recipient can transfer the intended relationship and reject a tempting invalid transfer.

<a id="a7a"></a>
**A7a. Map relationships explicitly**

**Use when:** A familiar domain has the relevant structure.

1. Write the target relationship
2. Choose a familiar case with the same relationship
3. Map roles and causal links
4. Identify one salient feature that does not transfer.

**Redirect:** If only superficial resemblance survives, discard the analogy.

<a id="a7b"></a>
**A7b. Compare two source cases**

**Use when:** A general structure must transfer beyond one memorable example.

1. Present two cases sharing the target relationship
2. Align corresponding roles
3. Extract the common relation
4. Apply it to a third case with different surface features.

**Redirect:** If transfer follows surface words, use a third case where those words mislead.

<a id="a7c"></a>
**A7c. Use an analogy to reveal its limit**

**Use when:** An existing analogy is distorting interpretation.

1. Write the inference imported from the analogy
2. Identify the structural requirement for that inference
3. Show that requirement is absent in the target
4. Replace the imported conclusion with a target-specific account.

**Redirect:** If the requirement actually holds, retain the valid portion of the analogy.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S12](#s12).

<a id="a8"></a>
#### A8. Introduce a name

**Adverse route:** Recognizing the label is mistaken for understanding the phenomenon.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The label enables accurate recognition or action that was previously difficult.

<a id="a8a"></a>
**A8a. Name a recurring distinction**

**Use when:** A useful difference is repeatedly rediscovered.

1. Show a positive case and a near-miss
2. State the difference in ordinary words
3. Assign the shortest unambiguous name
4. Use it in a fresh instance.

**Redirect:** If the difference cannot be stated, postpone naming.

<a id="a8b"></a>
**A8b. Split an overloaded term**

**Use when:** One word refers to several consequentially different things.

1. List its meanings in the live conversation
2. Assign separate literal phrases
3. Substitute them into the disputed claims
4. Identify which disagreement survives.

**Redirect:** If no consequence changes, keep the ordinary term.

<a id="a8c"></a>
**A8c. Replace an abstract label with an operation**

**Use when:** A noun conceals what is supposed to happen.

1. Identify the actor, input, operation, and changed state
2. Write the literal action
3. Demonstrate it once
4. Retain a short label only as an index to that action.

**Redirect:** If the operation is unspecified, mark the entry incomplete rather than inventing apparent precision.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a9"></a>
#### A9. Offer a large catalog

**Adverse route:** The volume obscures which entry would help now.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The catalog leads to a suitable entry while preserving access to relevant alternatives.

<a id="a9a"></a>
**A9a. Route by the present obstruction**

**Use when:** The user needs help now from a large collection.

1. Describe the current obstruction in observable terms
2. Choose the first discriminating routing question from existing context
3. Show the few matching entries
4. Expose why each is applicable.

**Redirect:** If routing depends on an unknown fact, ask only that fact or preserve both branches.

<a id="a9b"></a>
**A9b. Order by prerequisites**

**Use when:** Entries depend on one another.

1. Record each entry's required starting knowledge or capability
2. Construct the dependency order
3. Place the first missing prerequisite at the entry point
4. Make later entries accessible when it is acquired.

**Redirect:** If the dependency is merely customary, remove it.

<a id="a9c"></a>
**A9c. Index by the change enabled**

**Use when:** Subject labels do not reveal usefulness.

1. For each entry name its before-and-after change
2. Group entries that enable comparable transitions
3. Distinguish variants by starting conditions
4. Provide a direct path from the user's situation to those conditions.

**Redirect:** If an entry has no identifiable change it enables, move it to background reference.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a10"></a>
#### A10. Draw a diagram

**Adverse route:** A tidy picture makes an unsupported relationship appear established.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The visual representation supports the intended inference without invented relationships.

<a id="a10a"></a>
**A10a. Draw a dependency structure**

**Use when:** The obstacle is understanding what depends on what.

1. List entities and verified relations
2. Label each link with its literal meaning
3. Mark unknown links explicitly
4. Trace the path relevant to the current question.

**Redirect:** If the diagram adds unsupported causal arrows, replace them with the actual relation.

<a id="a10b"></a>
**A10b. Draw a decision fork**

**Use when:** The obstacle is selecting among conditional actions.

1. List available actions
2. Identify the facts that distinguish their applicability
3. Order those conditions into branches
4. Walk one ordinary and one boundary case through the diagram.

**Redirect:** If branches omit a feasible case, add an explicit unresolved branch.

<a id="a10c"></a>
**A10c. Draw change over time**

**Use when:** Event order or delay controls interpretation.

1. List dated or ordered events
2. Separate observation from hypothesized causation
3. Represent delays and feedback explicitly
4. Use the picture to answer the temporal question.

**Redirect:** If time is unknown, show partial order rather than fabricated spacing.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

### Evidence, confidence, and interpretation · A11–A20

<a id="a11"></a>
#### A11. Correct a false claim

**Adverse route:** A bare denial leaves the old explanation available for later inference.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** A supported correction changes the exact erroneous inference while preserving valid knowledge.

<a id="a11a"></a>
**A11a. Correct one factual proposition**

**Use when:** The claim, scope, and decisive evidence are identifiable.

1. State the correct proposition with its scope
2. Identify the conflicting part of the old claim
3. Show the decisive evidence and why it bears on that part
4. Apply the corrected proposition to the live inference.

**Redirect:** If evidence is contested, inspect the source or measurement issue before repeating the conclusion.

<a id="a11b"></a>
**A11b. Replace a causal explanation**

**Use when:** The false claim fills an explanatory role.

1. Map what the old explanation accounts for
2. Preserve observations that remain true
3. Supply a supported alternative that accounts for them
4. Trace how the practical conclusion changes.

**Redirect:** If no alternative is known, retain the unexplained observation without restoring the false cause.

<a id="a11c"></a>
**A11c. Narrow a partly true claim**

**Use when:** A valid claim has been generalized beyond its support.

1. Separate supported and unsupported cases
2. Identify the boundary from evidence
3. Rewrite the claim with that condition
4. Apply it to the disputed case.

**Redirect:** If the boundary is unknown, state the known domain and leave the extension unresolved.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S4](#s4).

<a id="a12"></a>
#### A12. Repeat a claim

**Adverse route:** Familiarity is mistaken for additional evidence.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Repetition increases useful access or warranted support without being miscounted as new evidence.

<a id="a12a"></a>
**A12a. Repeat through retrieval**

**Use when:** The issue is later access to verified information.

1. Present the accurate relation and qualification once
2. At a relevant later moment request its use or recall
3. Supply only the missing cue
4. Correct errors before another use.

**Redirect:** If recall is impossible, restore the prerequisite rather than increasing pressure.

<a id="a12b"></a>
**A12b. Repeat the qualification with the claim**

**Use when:** The memorable version tends to lose its boundary.

1. Identify the exception whose loss changes meaning
2. Attach it directly to the compact claim
3. Use a boundary case during repetition
4. Preserve the pair in later summaries.

**Redirect:** If the qualification is too long, change the wording of the claim itself.

<a id="a12c"></a>
**A12c. Return with genuinely new support**

**Use when:** Confidence should change because new evidence exists.

1. Identify the earlier evidential basis
2. Add the new independent observation
3. Distinguish its contribution from repeated sources
4. Revise confidence only by the additional support.

**Redirect:** If independence cannot be established, treat the repetition as a reminder.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S1](#s1), [S10](#s10).

<a id="a13"></a>
#### A13. Speak confidently

**Adverse route:** Certainty in delivery inflates certainty beyond the evidence.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Communicated certainty matches the argument or evidence while enabling appropriate action.

<a id="a13a"></a>
**A13a. State a proved consequence firmly**

**Use when:** The relevant premises and inference are established.

1. List the necessary premises
2. Verify the inferential step
3. State the conclusion without unnecessary hedging
4. Place uncertainty on any premise that actually remains unsettled.

**Redirect:** If a premise is uncertain, state the result conditionally.

<a id="a13b"></a>
**A13b. Separate a strong finding from a tentative explanation**

**Use when:** The result is better established than its proposed cause.

1. State the observation
2. State the proposed explanation separately
3. Name a competing explanation
4. Identify the observation that would distinguish them.

**Redirect:** If no distinction is currently available, preserve multiple causal accounts.

<a id="a13c"></a>
**A13c. Give a robust action under uncertainty**

**Use when:** A choice is stable across the plausible range.

1. Identify the plausible range and available choices
2. Compare choices across that range
3. State the action that remains appropriate
4. Name the condition that would reverse it.

**Redirect:** If no choice is robust, expose the unresolved trade-off instead of projecting confidence.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a14"></a>
#### A14. Express uncertainty

**Adverse route:** Local uncertainty becomes indiscriminate distrust of everything nearby.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The recipient can locate uncertainty and use what remains known.

<a id="a14a"></a>
**A14a. Partition the account**

**Use when:** Uncertainty in one component contaminates the whole.

1. Separate observations, interpretations, and unknowns
2. Attach each uncertainty to its component
3. Show what follows under every live interpretation
4. Preserve the conclusions common to them.

**Redirect:** If no common conclusion matters, identify the deciding unknown.

<a id="a14b"></a>
**A14b. Perform sensitivity analysis**

**Use when:** A decision may depend on an uncertain value.

1. Specify the plausible range
2. Locate where the preferred choice changes
3. Check whether current uncertainty crosses that boundary
4. Seek precision only if it can change the choice.

**Redirect:** If the boundary cannot be located, expose the missing decision model.

<a id="a14c"></a>
**A14c. Separate obtainable and unresolved uncertainty**

**Use when:** More inquiry may or may not be useful.

1. Name the unknown
2. List observations that could reduce it
3. Identify their availability and cost
4. Choose a feasible observation or a decision rule that tolerates the remaining uncertainty.

**Redirect:** If no observation distinguishes alternatives, do not promise resolution through more explanation.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S14](#s14).

<a id="a15"></a>
#### A15. Present much evidence

**Adverse route:** Correlated or weak examples create a false impression of independent support.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Evidence changes the relevant comparison without double-counting or obscuring the decisive issue.

<a id="a15a"></a>
**A15a. Lead with a discriminating observation**

**Use when:** One finding separates the main accounts.

1. Write the competing predictions
2. Select an observation where they differ
3. Inspect its quality
4. Show exactly which account it favors and by how much the evidence permits.

**Redirect:** If both predict it, do not present it as decisive.

<a id="a15b"></a>
**A15b. Combine independent contributions**

**Use when:** Several observations each supply new information.

1. Trace each item to its originating observation
2. Group dependent reports
3. Compare independent contributions
4. Summarize the combined direction with unresolved limitations.

**Redirect:** If sources share an origin, count the underlying observation once.

<a id="a15c"></a>
**A15c. Reconcile conflicting evidence**

**Use when:** Credible studies or observations disagree.

1. Align their populations, interventions, measures, and timing
2. Locate the differences that could explain the conflict
3. Separate genuine disagreement from different questions
4. Retain conditional conclusions where needed.

**Redirect:** If the difference remains unexplained, report the conflict rather than selecting the preferred result.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a16"></a>
#### A16. Cite sources

**Adverse route:** Prestige substitutes for checking whether a source supports the claim.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The recipient can inspect the evidence supporting the specific claim.

<a id="a16a"></a>
**A16a. Bind each consequential claim to support**

**Use when:** The answer depends on externally verifiable facts.

1. Identify the claims whose failure changes the conclusion
2. Open their primary sources
3. Locate the relevant evidence
4. Attach a direct citation and preserve the source's actual scope.

**Redirect:** If no source supports a claim, remove it or mark it as an inference.

<a id="a16b"></a>
**A16b. Audit the source's applicability**

**Use when:** A respected source may concern the wrong case.

1. Match the source's population, date, method, and outcome to the current question
2. Identify mismatches
3. Retain supported components
4. Limit the resulting conclusion.

**Redirect:** If the mismatch controls the answer, find more applicable evidence.

<a id="a16c"></a>
**A16c. Trace a citation chain to origin**

**Use when:** Many references may repeat one unsupported assertion.

1. Follow the pivotal assertion to its earliest accessible evidence
2. Distinguish reporting from new observation
3. Inspect that evidence
4. Update the claim using its actual support.

**Redirect:** If the origin cannot be inspected, mark the support unverified rather than laundering it through citations.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a17"></a>
#### A17. Give a counterexample

**Adverse route:** One exception is used to reject more than it logically refutes.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The counterexample defeats or bounds only the inference it logically addresses.

<a id="a17a"></a>
**A17a. Refute a universal claim**

**Use when:** The proposition asserts all, none, always, or impossible.

1. Write the proposition with explicit conditions
2. Construct or verify a case meeting those conditions
3. Show the contrary result
4. Withdraw the universal assertion.

**Redirect:** If a condition is not met, the case does not refute the assertion.

<a id="a17b"></a>
**A17b. Repair the boundary**

**Use when:** A useful generalization has a meaningful exception.

1. Compare the exception with valid cases
2. Identify a condition explaining the difference
3. Formulate the narrower rule
4. Examine another case near the new boundary.

**Redirect:** If no stable boundary is supported, retain a probabilistic or unresolved account.

<a id="a17c"></a>
**A17c. Correct an overextended intuition**

**Use when:** An intuitive rule is useful in some settings and misleading in others.

1. Present a familiar case where the intuition works
2. Change one consequential condition
3. Calculate or trace the changed outcome
4. State which feature controls transfer.

**Redirect:** If the revised case is unrealistic for the task, choose a relevant boundary case.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a18"></a>
#### A18. Ask for a mechanism

**Adverse route:** Fluent improvisation creates a false sense of explanation.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** A mechanism account supports a discriminating prediction and exposes unresolved links.

<a id="a18a"></a>
**A18a. Trace the proposed cause to the outcome**

**Use when:** An explanation contains unnamed intermediate steps.

1. State the cause and outcome
2. Fill each necessary intermediate transition with a literal operation
3. Mark unsupported transitions
4. Derive an observable consequence of the weakest disputed link.

**Redirect:** If a transition cannot be specified, label the mechanism incomplete.

<a id="a18b"></a>
**A18b. Compare rival mechanisms**

**Use when:** Several stories explain the same observation.

1. Write each mechanism as a sequence
2. Locate where their predicted consequences diverge
3. Check the available evidence at that point
4. Preserve the surviving accounts.

**Redirect:** If the mechanisms make identical available predictions, do not choose by narrative fluency.

<a id="a18c"></a>
**A18c. Build a bounded model**

**Use when:** The mechanism can be represented with explicit inputs and rules.

1. Choose a small case preserving the causal relationship
2. State the rules
3. Calculate or simulate the outcome
4. Compare with the target behavior and identify omitted factors.

**Redirect:** If the model matches only after ad hoc changes, expose those changes as new assumptions.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S7](#s7).

<a id="a19"></a>
#### A19. Ask for reasons

**Adverse route:** The person rehearses defenses without examining what would decide the issue.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Reasons reveal what actually governs the judgment and what could change it.

<a id="a19a"></a>
**A19a. Find the deciding reason**

**Use when:** A list of reasons obscures which one matters.

1. List the stated reasons
2. Consider the choice with each removed
3. Identify which removals alter the judgment
4. Examine those reasons first.

**Redirect:** If no removal matters, look for an unstated governing concern.

<a id="a19b"></a>
**A19b. Separate factual and evaluative reasons**

**Use when:** Evidence disputes conceal disagreements about priorities.

1. Classify each reason as factual, causal, or evaluative
2. Resolve factual claims with evidence
3. Express evaluative trade-offs explicitly
4. Show which disagreement remains.

**Redirect:** If priorities differ, do not pretend another fact alone resolves them.

<a id="a19c"></a>
**A19c. Use the person's own accepted premises**

**Use when:** The conclusion conflicts with another accepted commitment.

1. State both commitments accurately
2. Derive their joint consequence
3. Identify the conflict in a concrete case
4. Make the available revisions explicit.

**Redirect:** If the commitments are conditional rather than conflicting, clarify their different conditions.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S7](#s7).

<a id="a20"></a>
#### A20. Present several perspectives

**Adverse route:** Unequal accounts are treated as equally supported.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Alternative frames reveal consequential differences while preserving evidential discrimination.

<a id="a20a"></a>
**A20a. Expose an omission**

**Use when:** The current frame hides a relevant part of the situation.

1. List what the current frame represents
2. Identify a consequential omitted factor
3. Choose a frame that makes that factor explicit
4. Compare how the available actions change.

**Redirect:** If the alternative adds no consequential difference, omit it.

<a id="a20b"></a>
**A20b. Extract what survives multiple representations**

**Use when:** A conclusion may depend on a particular description.

1. Express the same situation through two materially different frames
2. Derive the conclusion within each
3. Identify common results and frame-dependent results
4. Investigate the latter's assumptions.

**Redirect:** If translations change the underlying facts, repair the translation before comparing.

<a id="a20c"></a>
**A20c. Choose a frame conditionally**

**Use when:** Different representations serve different operations.

1. Specify the operation needed now
2. List what each frame makes easy or obscures
3. Select the frame fitting that operation
4. Preserve access to another frame for its relevant condition.

**Redirect:** If the operation itself is mistaken, revise the target before selecting a frame.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

### Problem representation and possibility · A21–A30

<a id="a21"></a>
#### A21. Reframe a problem

**Adverse route:** A pleasing reformulation hides the original constraint or cost.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The reformulation preserves the worthwhile objective and reveals a feasible route or a reason to abandon a mistaken objective.

<a id="a21a"></a>
**A21a. Preserve the result, replace the formulation**

**Use when:** The current wording assumes a particular means.

1. State the valued outcome without its proposed means
2. List the constraints that actually bind
3. Construct one alternative means
4. Compare its outcome and costs with the original.

**Redirect:** If no meaningful alternative appears, retain the original formulation.

<a id="a21b"></a>
**A21b. Change the unit of intervention**

**Use when:** Repeated local fixes leave the same difficulty.

1. Locate the recurring interaction
2. Describe it at the level where it is produced
3. Identify a change to that interaction
4. Trace effects back to each affected local case.

**Redirect:** If the larger frame hides local losses, restore them before choosing.

<a id="a21c"></a>
**A21c. Reject a perfectly executed useless aim**

**Use when:** Success at the stated target would not resolve the underlying concern.

1. Imagine the target fully achieved
2. Specify what would improve for the person
3. Identify any missing connection
4. Replace or retire the target if that connection cannot be justified.

**Redirect:** If the target has independent endorsed value, preserve that value explicitly.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a22"></a>
#### A22. Decompose a problem

**Adverse route:** Interactions disappear when parts are examined separately.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** A component or interaction is identified whose repair changes the overall result.

<a id="a22a"></a>
**A22a. Find a prerequisite**

**Use when:** The task contains dependent operations.

1. Write the required output
2. Work backward through necessary inputs
3. Locate the earliest unavailable input
4. Supply or construct it before executing downstream steps.

**Redirect:** If inputs exist, inspect the transformation rather than collecting more.

<a id="a22b"></a>
**A22b. Locate the first divergence**

**Use when:** There is a correct example or specification.

1. Align actual and required intermediate results
2. Find their first consequential difference
3. Isolate the operation producing it
4. Repair that operation and propagate the correction.

**Redirect:** If several paths interact, inspect their join rather than blaming one part.

<a id="a22c"></a>
**A22c. Preserve interactions**

**Use when:** The parts look adequate but the whole fails.

1. List exchanges between parts
2. Specify timing and compatibility requirements
3. Locate a violated interface
4. Repair the interface and recombine the parts.

**Redirect:** If no interface explains the failure, revisit the decomposition.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a23"></a>
#### A23. Unify several ideas

**Adverse route:** Useful differences are erased by an excessively broad category.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** A shared rule supports useful reuse while exceptions remain distinguishable.

<a id="a23a"></a>
**A23a. Extract a relation**

**Use when:** Several examples look different but may share structure.

1. Align corresponding roles
2. Identify the relationship preserved across cases
3. Express that relationship independently of surface details
4. Apply it to a new case.

**Redirect:** If only vocabulary is shared, do not infer a common mechanism.

<a id="a23b"></a>
**A23b. Test a common cause**

**Use when:** Several outcomes might arise from one upstream factor.

1. Name the candidate cause
2. Trace its path to each outcome
3. Derive an observation that separates it from separate causes
4. Use available evidence to retain or reject it.

**Redirect:** If discriminating evidence is missing, keep the unification provisional.

<a id="a23c"></a>
**A23c. Unify with exceptions**

**Use when:** Multiple rules overlap but cannot safely be collapsed.

1. Write their common condition
2. Retain each outcome-changing exception
3. Construct a routing rule for those exceptions
4. Use the common rule only in its remaining domain.

**Redirect:** If exception handling exceeds the benefit, keep the rules separate.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a24"></a>
#### A24. Add a dimension

**Adverse route:** Extra variables create complexity without changing a choice.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The added variable changes a justified explanation, comparison, or action.

<a id="a24a"></a>
**A24a. Explain matched outcomes**

**Use when:** Similar visible inputs produce different results.

1. Hold the known features constant
2. Identify one differing condition with a causal path
3. Compare cases on that condition
4. Revise the explanation only as evidence permits.

**Redirect:** If the condition cannot explain the difference, discard it.

<a id="a24b"></a>
**A24b. Restore an omitted cost**

**Use when:** A favored option wins by ignoring a consequence.

1. Name the omitted consequence
2. Estimate it comparably across options
3. Add it to the existing comparison
4. Recompute which options remain acceptable.

**Redirect:** If rankings and acceptability stay unchanged, keep the added detail optional.

<a id="a24c"></a>
**A24c. Add time**

**Use when:** Immediate and later effects may conflict.

1. Specify the decision horizon
2. Separate immediate from delayed outcomes
3. Include transition and maintenance costs
4. Compare options over that horizon.

**Redirect:** If the horizon is uncertain, identify choices robust across plausible horizons.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a25"></a>
#### A25. Change the scale

**Adverse route:** Individual or local consequences vanish in an aggregate description.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The chosen scale exposes the relevant pattern without erasing material variation.

<a id="a25a"></a>
**A25a. Separate total from marginal**

**Use when:** An aggregate fact is being used to judge the next increment.

1. State the aggregate quantity
2. Identify the next unit under consideration
3. Calculate that unit's incremental costs and benefits
4. Decide using the incremental comparison.

**Redirect:** If increments change the system, include those system effects.

<a id="a25b"></a>
**A25b. Restore the distribution**

**Use when:** An average hides who gains or loses.

1. Identify affected groups or cases
2. Disaggregate the outcome
3. Locate material variation
4. Reconsider the decision using the person's stated distributional priorities.

**Redirect:** If groups are too small for reliable estimates, retain uncertainty.

<a id="a25c"></a>
**A25c. Match the time scale**

**Use when:** A snapshot is mistaken for a trend or steady state.

1. Separate short fluctuations from lasting changes
2. Inspect observations over the relevant interval
3. Identify transition effects
4. Revise the claim to the supported time scale.

**Redirect:** If the record is too short, withhold the trend claim.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a26"></a>
#### A26. Reverse an assumption

**Adverse route:** A weak opposite is adopted merely because it is different.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** An assumption is either justified, weakened, or replaced with a feasible alternative.

<a id="a26a"></a>
**A26a. Release a supposedly fixed variable**

**Use when:** A constraint has been asserted without verification.

1. Name who or what imposes it
2. Distinguish physical limits from policies or habits
3. Identify an available way to change it
4. Reassess the options under the revised constraint.

**Redirect:** If the constraint is real and immovable, design within it.

<a id="a26b"></a>
**A26b. Reverse means and ends**

**Use when:** An inherited method has become the objective.

1. Identify the outcome the method originally served
2. Compare the method against that outcome now
3. Find a direct alternative
4. Choose on current consequences rather than tradition.

**Redirect:** If the method itself is valued, include that value rather than deleting it.

<a id="a26c"></a>
**A26c. Change the quantifier**

**Use when:** An absolute assumption blocks partial progress.

1. Write the exact always, never, all, or none claim
2. Search for a valid exception
3. Replace the absolute with its supported scope
4. Derive the newly available action.

**Redirect:** If no exception is established, do not manufacture one.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a27"></a>
#### A27. Construct a counterfactual

**Adverse route:** Unattainable alternatives feed regret or distorted causal conclusions.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** An alternative world yields a justified inference about causation, choice, or robustness.

<a id="a27a"></a>
**A27a. Change one causal input**

**Use when:** A mechanism can specify what follows from an intervention.

1. Fix the background conditions
2. Change the candidate cause
3. Propagate changes through the stated mechanism
4. Compare the predicted outcome with the original.

**Redirect:** If downstream effects are unknown, report alternative branches rather than one invented outcome.

<a id="a27b"></a>
**A27b. Compare feasible alternatives**

**Use when:** Regret or planning concerns a real choice.

1. List what was knowable and available at decision time
2. Choose a feasible alternative
3. Compare expected consequences using that information
4. Extract one prospective decision rule.

**Redirect:** If the alternative required hindsight, exclude it from the judgment.

<a id="a27c"></a>
**A27c. Test necessity and robustness**

**Use when:** A conclusion rests on a potentially fragile condition.

1. Remove or vary that condition
2. Preserve other relevant assumptions
3. Identify where the conclusion changes
4. State the resulting boundary of the recommendation.

**Redirect:** If the boundary is outside plausible conditions, retain the recommendation with that scope.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a28"></a>
#### A28. Elicit a prediction

**Adverse route:** Public commitment makes the person defend the forecast after contrary evidence.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** A prior expectation becomes explicit enough to compare with a later observation.

<a id="a28a"></a>
**A28a. Forecast a defined event**

**Use when:** A future outcome can be resolved unambiguously.

1. Define the event and deadline
2. Start from a relevant reference class
3. Adjust for case-specific evidence
4. Record the probability and resolution rule before the outcome.

**Redirect:** If no defensible number exists, use an ordered range without invented precision.

<a id="a28b"></a>
**A28b. Predict from competing mechanisms**

**Use when:** Two explanations fit existing observations.

1. Write each explanation's next observable consequence
2. Choose a situation where they differ
3. Record both predictions
4. Compare with independently obtained observations.

**Redirect:** If they predict the same thing, choose another observation.

<a id="a28c"></a>
**A28c. Find the decision boundary**

**Use when:** A forecast matters only if it changes an action.

1. List available actions
2. Locate the forecast level where their ordering changes
3. Compare present uncertainty with that boundary
4. Seek more information only if it could alter the choice.

**Redirect:** If values cannot be quantified, use explicit conditional comparisons.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S11](#s11).

<a id="a29"></a>
#### A29. Show a new possibility

**Adverse route:** An attractive but infeasible option displaces workable action.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** A previously excluded option becomes demonstrably feasible or appropriately bounded.

<a id="a29a"></a>
**A29a. Use a verified existence example**

**Use when:** The obstacle is a claim of impossibility.

1. Find a genuine successful instance
2. Verify the claimed result
3. Compare its enabling conditions with the present case
4. Identify which conditions can be reproduced.

**Redirect:** If conditions cannot transfer, establish possibility only, not local feasibility.

<a id="a29b"></a>
**A29b. Construct the missing path**

**Use when:** The destination is imaginable but no route is visible.

1. Define the destination concretely
2. Work backward through prerequisites
3. Connect the current state to the first attainable prerequisite
4. Show the complete conditional path.

**Redirect:** If a link is missing, identify it rather than promising arrival.

<a id="a29c"></a>
**A29c. Change available resources**

**Use when:** A limitation depends on the current tool or environment.

1. Identify the operation that cannot currently be performed
2. Find an accessible resource that performs it
3. Check compatibility and cost
4. Integrate it into a workable sequence.

**Redirect:** If access or compatibility fails, the option remains unavailable.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a30"></a>
#### A30. Reclassify an obstacle

**Adverse route:** External barriers are mislabeled as personal attitude defects.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The obstacle is assigned to a cause that implies a useful intervention.

<a id="a30a"></a>
**A30a. Distinguish knowing from being able**

**Use when:** Advice is repeated without action.

1. Check whether the person can state the action
2. Inspect whether they can perform it under favorable conditions
3. Identify missing access, time, or incentives
4. Address the demonstrated gap.

**Redirect:** If the goal is not endorsed, revisit the goal instead of increasing pressure.

<a id="a30b"></a>
**A30b. Repair a prerequisite**

**Use when:** A difficult step depends on an absent component.

1. Specify the failed operation
2. List its necessary inputs
3. Identify the absent input
4. Supply it and resume the original operation.

**Redirect:** If supplying it does not resolve the failure, inspect the transformation.

<a id="a30c"></a>
**A30c. Find interacting blockers**

**Use when:** Removing one obstacle has not helped.

1. List conditions jointly necessary for action
2. Mark which currently hold
3. Identify the smallest feasible set of repairs
4. Implement that set in dependency order.

**Redirect:** If the full set is infeasible, revise the action or objective.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

### Trust, identity, and social interpretation · A31–A40

<a id="a31a"></a>
**A31a. Scope expertise**

**Use when:** A source's reputation is carrying a specific claim.

1. Identify the exact claim
2. Verify relevant domain experience

<a id="a31b"></a>

**Use when:** A consequential claim relies on one authority.

1. Trace the original evidence
2. Find another qualified assessment
3. Check whether it has independent information

**Redirect:** If both repeat one origin, count one evidential stream.

<a id="a31c"></a>

2. Inspect resolved judgments including failures

<a id="a32"></a>

<a id="a32a"></a>
**A32a. Expose the exact contradiction**

**Use when:** The conclusion conflicts with an accepted premise.

4. Identify which premise or inference must change.

**Redirect:** If the contradiction depends on disputed wording, resolve that wording first.

<a id="a32b"></a>

**Use when:** A weak caricature would be easy to dismiss.

1. Reconstruct the strongest relevant argument
2. Preserve its valid parts
3. Identify its decisive unsupported step
4. Show how repairing that step changes the conclusion.

**Redirect:** If the strongest case survives, revise your challenge.

<a id="a32c"></a>
**A32c. Make consequences explicit**

**Use when:** An error has immediate practical implications.

1. Name the decision affected
2. Trace the error to its consequence
3. State the warranted correction directly
4. Provide the feasible action that follows.

**Redirect:** If the stakes are speculative, reduce the force of the warning.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a33"></a>
#### A33. Use warmth or praise

**Adverse route:** Flattery is interpreted as a substitute for substance.

<a id="a33a"></a>

2. Name what it accomplished

<a id="a33b"></a>

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a34"></a>
#### A34. Personalize

**Adverse route:** A mistaken assumption about the person misdirects the entire attempt.

<a id="a35"></a>

<a id="a37"></a>

<a id="a39"></a>

### Choices and action · A51–A60

<a id="a51"></a>
#### A51. Expand the option set

**Adverse route:** More possibilities make a previously manageable decision stall.

**Redirect:** If needs do not distinguish options, avoid pretending one is universally best.

<a id="a52b"></a>

#### A53. Rank decision criteria

**Adverse route:** A convenient metric displaces the actual objective.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Criteria distinguish acceptable options and support a choice consistent with endorsed priorities.

<a id="a53a"></a>
**A53a. Separate constraints from preferences**

**Use when:** A weighted score could hide an unacceptable failure.

2. Eliminate violating options
3. Identify preferences among remaining options
4. Compare only feasible candidates.

<a id="a53b"></a>

#### A54. Create an if-then plan

**Adverse route:** An inappropriate cue triggers action after circumstances change.

<a id="a54a"></a>

2. Show each consequential transformation and its reason

4. Give a nearby problem with less support.

**Redirect:** If a prerequisite is missing, explain that prerequisite before continuing.

<a id="a61b"></a>
**A61b. Contrast a correct and failed solution**

**Use when:** A recurring mistake is hard to distinguish from correct work.

1. Hold the problem constant
2. Place correct and flawed solutions side by side
3. Identify their first consequential divergence
4. Derive the different outcomes from that divergence.

**Redirect:** If several features differ, simplify the pair before drawing a lesson.

<a id="a61c"></a>
**A61c. Make the next operation predictable**

**Use when:** A learner can follow a demonstration but remains passive.

1. Show the state before a meaningful step
2. Invite a prediction when useful
3. Reveal the correct operation and reason
4. Transfer the next comparable step to the learner.

**Redirect:** If prediction becomes blind guessing, restore the missing explanation.

<a id="a62"></a>
#### A62. Ask for retrieval

**Adverse route:** An impossible recall demand teaches discouragement or rehearses an error.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Relevant information or a procedure can be recovered without the original answer in view.

<a id="a62a"></a>
**A62a. Retrieve without the answer**

**Use when:** The material has been understood but may not be accessible later.

1. Hide the original answer
2. Use a cue matching the intended use
3. Retrieve the answer
4. Compare with the source and correct omissions.

**Redirect:** If retrieval repeatedly fails, restore instruction before another attempt.

<a id="a62b"></a>
**A62b. Use decreasing cues**

**Use when:** Unaided recall is currently too difficult.

1. Start with the smallest useful cue
2. Recover the missing content
3. Remove part of the cue on the next suitable occasion
4. Continue toward the intended support level.

**Redirect:** If cues simply reveal the answer, redesign them to require the target operation.

<a id="a62c"></a>
**A62c. Retrieve through application**

**Use when:** The goal is usable knowledge rather than verbal repetition.

1. Present a fresh problem requiring the rule
2. Withhold the rule's name
3. Apply the recalled rule
4. Compare both rule selection and execution with the specification.

**Redirect:** If the rule is known but not selected, practice recognition of its use conditions.

<a id="a63"></a>
#### A63. Space revisits

**Adverse route:** The interval exceeds what can be recovered with the available support.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Knowledge remains accessible across the delay relevant to its intended use.

<a id="a63a"></a>
**A63a. Space according to recoverability**

**Use when:** Repeated use over time is required.

1. Establish an initially correct representation
2. Revisit after a delay
3. Attempt retrieval before reopening the answer
4. Lengthen or shorten the next interval according to recovery.

**Redirect:** If the interval exceeds current recoverability, add support rather than repeating failure.

<a id="a63b"></a>
**A63b. Interleave confusable cases**

**Use when:** The task requires choosing among similar categories or methods.

1. Select contrasting categories
2. Alternate representative examples
3. Require choosing the category or method
4. Explain the feature that distinguishes each case.

**Redirect:** If the learner lacks any category model, provide initial examples first.

<a id="a63c"></a>
**A63c. Revisit at actual use points**

**Use when:** A rule is repeatedly needed in a recurring workflow.

1. Identify the recurring use occasion
2. Place a retrieval cue there
3. Recover and apply the rule
4. Correct it from a reliable reference when needed.

**Redirect:** If use is too rare for retention, preserve a dependable external reference.

<a id="a64"></a>

<a id="a64a"></a>

**Use when:** A rule is tied too narrowly to one example.

1. Keep the governing relation fixed
2. Change one surface or contextual feature
3. Apply the rule
4. State why the changed feature does not alter its validity.

<a id="a64b"></a>
**A64b. Expose an invariant**

**Use when:** Several diverse cases share a useful structure.

1. Align the cases by functional roles
2. Identify the preserved relation
3. Express it without case-specific vocabulary
4. Solve another structurally matching case.

**Redirect:** If no invariant is supported, retain separate rules.

<a id="a64c"></a>
**A64c. Practice adaptation**

**Use when:** A new case preserves some structure but changes a consequential condition.

1. Identify the shared part
2. Locate the changed condition
3. Modify the affected operation
4. Verify the adapted result against the new requirements.

<a id="a65a"></a>

<a id="a69"></a>

#### A70. Create a memory cue

**Adverse route:** The slogan is remembered while its qualifications disappear.

<a id="a70a"></a>
**A70a. Bind recall to an event**

**Use when:** The rule is forgotten at a recurring use point.

1. Identify the event that precedes use

<a id="a70b"></a>

<a id="a70c"></a>

**Use when:** A memorable claim could outlive its limiting condition.

1. Name the material qualification
2. Incorporate it into the same retrievable phrase or representation

### Readiness and self-regulation · A71–A80

<a id="a71"></a>
#### A71. Add time to reflect

**Adverse route:** Reflection becomes a way to postpone a necessary decision.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Additional time produces a consequential inference or decision rather than repeated unproductive thought.

<a id="a71a"></a>
**A71a. Reflect on a named gap**

**Use when:** One inference remains unresolved after the initial answer.

1. State the precise gap
2. Identify the premises that bear on it
3. Work through their relationship
4. Stop when the inference is resolved or its missing input is named.

**Redirect:** If nothing new enters the reasoning, change the input or stop.

<a id="a71b"></a>
**A71b. Return after a bounded break**

**Use when:** Continued effort is repeating the same unproductive route.

1. Preserve the current state and unresolved question
2. Take a bounded break from that route
3. Return to a specified alternative representation
4. Retain any improvement that survives inspection.

**Redirect:** If the break yields no improvement, do not treat more waiting as a method.

<a id="a71c"></a>
**A71c. Allocate reflection by decision value**

**Use when:** A choice deserves thought but not unlimited delay.

1. Identify what reflection could change
2. Estimate the cost of delay
3. Allocate time to the consequential comparisons
4. Act when the remaining uncertainty cannot justify more delay.

**Redirect:** If missing external evidence is decisive, seek it rather than introspecting longer.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a72"></a>
#### A72. Increase stimulation

**Adverse route:** Excitement increases distractibility or obscures costs.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The stimulation level supports the required discrimination and sustained performance.

<a id="a72a"></a>
**A72a. Introduce a meaningful contrast**

**Use when:** Monotony hides an important difference.

1. Identify the distinction the person misses
2. Present two sharply contrasting relevant cases
3. Direct attention to the governing difference
4. Return to the original task using that distinction.

**Redirect:** If surprise distracts from the distinction, remove the embellishment.

<a id="a72b"></a>
**A72b. Reduce excess stimulation**

**Use when:** Additional novelty or sensory input is degrading work.

1. Identify the interfering input
2. Remove or reduce it
3. Preserve information required for the task
4. Resume from the last coherent state.

**Redirect:** If difficulty persists, inspect task complexity or missing knowledge.

<a id="a72c"></a>
**A72c. Change timing or setting**

**Use when:** A recurring context predictably supports poor attention.

1. Identify a more workable available period or place
2. Move a bounded task there
3. Keep the task and success criterion comparable
4. Retain the change if actual performance improves.

**Redirect:** If moving is infeasible, reduce the task's demands within the current setting.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a73"></a>
#### A73. Reduce interruptions

**Adverse route:** Important signals are blocked along with distractions.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Necessary work receives sufficient continuity while important interruptions remain reachable.

<a id="a73a"></a>
**A73a. Protect a coherent work unit**

**Use when:** Interruptions break a task's dependent reasoning.

1. Identify a unit that can be completed in one available interval
2. Defer nonurgent notifications for that interval
3. Keep the required inputs at hand
4. Finish or checkpoint the unit before switching.

**Redirect:** If urgent contact is necessary, preserve a specific channel for it.

<a id="a73b"></a>
**A73b. Capture without switching**

**Use when:** Incoming thoughts or requests repeatedly redirect work.

1. Provide a simple capture location
2. Record only enough to resume later
3. Return to the current operation
4. Review captured items at a natural boundary.

**Redirect:** If an item changes the current task's validity, address it immediately.

<a id="a73c"></a>
**A73c. Batch routine checks**

**Use when:** Frequent checking adds little immediate value.

1. Identify checks with tolerable delay
2. Select intervals matching actual urgency
3. Batch those checks
4. Revise the interval if material delays occur.

**Redirect:** If a stream requires immediate response, separate it from routine traffic.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a74"></a>
#### A74. Change the environment

**Adverse route:** A narrow cue-dependent success fails whenever the setting changes.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The environment supplies a missing condition or removes a demonstrated obstruction.

<a id="a74a"></a>
**A74a. Prepare the necessary resources**

**Use when:** Starting fails because inputs are unavailable at the right moment.

1. List resources needed for the first action
2. Place them in the actual use setting
3. Remove a known access obstacle
4. Connect the prepared setting to the action cue.

**Redirect:** If readiness is already sufficient, diagnose another cause.

<a id="a74b"></a>
**A74b. Change the social conditions**

**Use when:** The setting lacks real support for the intended behavior.

1. Identify the needed interaction or opportunity
2. Locate a setting where it is actually available
3. Arrange access where feasible
4. Connect the person to the relevant activity.

**Redirect:** If exclusion or constraints persist, do not rely on a change in interpretation alone.

<a id="a74c"></a>
**A74c. Redesign the physical sequence**

**Use when:** The current arrangement repeatedly cues the wrong action.

1. Trace the usual action path
2. Identify the cue or friction that controls it
3. Reposition resources to support the endorsed action
4. Inspect behavior in the changed setting.

**Redirect:** If behavior does not change, reassess the assumed controlling feature.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a75"></a>
#### A75. Reinterpret an emotion

**Adverse route:** A valid warning is dismissed simply because it is emotional.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The meaning assigned to an emotion becomes more accurate or useful without denying the feeling.

<a id="a75a"></a>
**A75a. Separate event from appraisal**

**Use when:** An interpretation is being treated as an observed fact.

1. State what happened in observable terms
2. Identify the added interpretation
3. Compare alternative interpretations against the facts
4. Act on the best supported account.

**Redirect:** If the original appraisal is well supported, address the situation itself.

<a id="a75b"></a>

**Use when:** The person interprets ordinary task arousal as incapacity.

1. Distinguish the sensation from the capability claim
2. Identify the actual task demand

**Redirect:** If impairment is material, reduce demands rather than insisting on a positive label.

<a id="a75c"></a>
**A75c. Keep feelings from becoming evidence about unrelated claims**

**Use when:** An emotional reaction is carrying an inference it cannot establish.

1. Identify the feeling and its likely trigger
2. State the separate factual question
3. Inspect evidence relevant to that question
4. Preserve the feeling as information about experience, not proof.

**Redirect:** If the feeling points to a real unexamined consequence, investigate that consequence.

### Conversation and joint work · A81–A90

<a id="a81"></a>
#### A81. Ask a question

**Adverse route:** Unnecessary questioning transfers avoidable work to the recipient.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The question supplies information or performs a cognitive operation that changes the next useful response.

<a id="a81a"></a>
**A81a. Separate consequential branches**

**Use when:** A missing fact would lead to different actions.

1. State the competing routes
2. Identify the fact distinguishing them
3. Ask the shortest question that obtains it
4. Execute the corresponding route using the answer.

**Redirect:** If both answers lead to the same response, omit the question.

<a id="a81b"></a>
**A81b. Expose a missing premise**

**Use when:** A conclusion cannot be assessed without one assumption.

1. Identify the unsupported inference
2. Isolate its required premise
3. Ask for that premise or its evidence
4. Revise the conclusion according to what is supplied.

**Redirect:** If the premise is already visible in context, use it directly.

<a id="a81c"></a>
**A81c. Elicit an operation**

**Use when:** Performing a small operation is itself useful for learning or diagnosis.

1. Choose the specific inference, prediction, or comparison needed
2. Provide its prerequisites
3. Request that operation
4. Use the result to supply the next missing piece.

**Redirect:** If participation only shifts work to the user, perform the operation yourself.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a82"></a>
#### A82. Paraphrase

**Adverse route:** Repeated recognition replaces progress and drains attention.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Paraphrase resolves a consequential ambiguity or produces an unused implication.

<a id="a82a"></a>
**A82a. Disambiguate a referent**

**Use when:** A word could refer to materially different things.

1. Locate the ambiguous term
2. Offer an operational interpretation grounded in context
3. Identify the consequence of that interpretation
4. Proceed conditionally or ask only if the difference blocks work.

**Redirect:** If the referent is already clear, skip the paraphrase.

<a id="a82b"></a>
**A82b. Translate into an action specification**

**Use when:** The request describes an experience but leaves execution implicit.

1. Identify the desired result
2. Translate it into observable requirements
3. Derive the required work
4. Produce that work rather than stopping at the translation.

**Redirect:** If the translation loses a valued feature, restore it.

<a id="a82c"></a>
**A82c. Isolate local disagreement**

**Use when:** Repeated broad restatements have not advanced understanding.

1. Mark what is already agreed
2. Extract the exact unresolved clause
3. Reformulate only that clause in discriminable terms
4. Resolve its factual or practical consequence.

**Redirect:** If no ambiguity remains, add evidence or an inference instead.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a83"></a>
#### A83. Invite objections

**Adverse route:** Objections become a performance with no consequential revision.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** An objection exposes a material defect or increases warranted confidence within a stated scope.

<a id="a83a"></a>
**A83a. Find the controlling objection**

**Use when:** A proposal has many possible criticisms of unequal importance.

1. Identify the assumption whose failure would invalidate the proposal
2. Construct its strongest relevant objection
3. Inspect available evidence
4. Repair or reject the proposal if necessary.

**Redirect:** If the objection is already resolved, move to another consequential assumption.

<a id="a83b"></a>
**A83b. Construct a countermodel**

**Use when:** A claimed inference may fail despite its premises holding.

1. Write the premises precisely
2. Construct a case satisfying them
3. Test whether the conclusion can still fail
4. Narrow or repair the inference when it does.

**Redirect:** If no countermodel is found, do not mistake unsuccessful search for proof.

<a id="a83c"></a>
**A83c. Stress the execution path**

**Use when:** The idea may be sound but its implementation fragile.

1. Trace the required sequence
2. Insert a plausible missing input or disturbance
3. Identify where execution fails
4. Add a feasible branch for that condition.

**Redirect:** If recovery is infeasible, state the operating limit rather than hiding it.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a84"></a>
#### A84. State common ground

**Adverse route:** An appearance of consensus hides the actual disagreement.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Shared premises enable a new inference, coordinated action, or precise disagreement.

<a id="a84a"></a>
**A84a. Derive from accepted facts**

**Use when:** Relevant facts are shared but their consequence is unused.

1. Select the agreed facts
2. Connect them through an explicit valid inference
3. State the new consequence
4. Apply it to the current issue.

**Redirect:** If the consequence is already accepted, address the remaining disagreement.

<a id="a84b"></a>
**A84b. Coordinate compatible values**

**Use when:** Different priorities can support one action for different reasons.

1. State each endorsed priority
2. Find an action advancing both under actual constraints
3. Inspect remaining trade-offs
4. Propose the action without claiming identical values.

**Redirect:** If compatibility fails, present the real conflict.

<a id="a84c"></a>
**A84c. Align definitions**

**Use when:** Apparent disagreement may arise from different uses of a term.

1. Specify each usage
2. Translate the disputed claim into observable statements
3. Compare those statements
4. Retain any substantive disagreement after translation.

**Redirect:** If definitions were already shared, do not prolong semantic work.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a85"></a>
#### A85. Offer format choices

**Adverse route:** Choosing how to receive help becomes another burdensome task.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The representation reduces the operation needed to understand or use the information.

<a id="a85a"></a>
**A85a. Choose a table or spatial mapping**

**Use when:** The task requires comparing exact relationships.

1. Identify the comparison operation
2. Assign consistent rows, columns, or positions
3. Place comparable quantities on the same basis
4. Expose the difference that controls the conclusion.

**Redirect:** If spatial form adds decoding effort, use direct prose.

<a id="a85b"></a>
**A85b. Choose interaction for changing conditions**

**Use when:** Understanding depends on how an outcome changes with inputs.

1. Identify the consequential inputs
2. Bind them to an explicit model
3. Let changes reveal the resulting differences
4. State assumptions and unsupported regions.

**Redirect:** If a single static case answers the question, avoid building unnecessary interaction.

<a id="a85c"></a>
**A85c. Choose a direct sequence**

**Use when:** The user needs to execute dependent actions.

1. Identify the required output
2. List actions in dependency order
3. Attach conditions at the relevant steps
4. End at the completed output.

**Redirect:** If a format preference is already known, honor it unless it prevents the required operation.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S19](#s19).

<a id="a86"></a>
#### A86. Sequence several messages

**Adverse route:** The crucial point arrives only after attention or patience is exhausted.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The message sequence supplies prerequisites and advances toward a result without redundant turns.

<a id="a86a"></a>
**A86a. Order by understanding dependencies**

**Use when:** A later inference requires an earlier concept.

1. Identify the desired inference
2. Trace its prerequisites
3. Supply only missing prerequisites first
4. Derive and apply the inference as soon as they are available.

**Redirect:** If prerequisites are already known, begin with the inference.

<a id="a86b"></a>
**A86b. Lead with the consequential contrast**

**Use when:** A familiar explanation would be ignored before its novelty appears.

1. Identify the difference that changes the decision
2. Present it first
3. Provide the evidence needed to establish it
4. Add context only where it changes interpretation.

**Redirect:** If the contrast overstates the evidence, narrow it before using it.

<a id="a86c"></a>
**A86c. Make every turn advance**

**Use when:** A continuing conversation risks cycling through acknowledgments.

1. Track the last established result
2. Identify the next unresolved dependency
3. Resolve it in the next message
4. Update the shared result rather than repeating the trajectory.

**Redirect:** If no dependency remains, complete the task and stop.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a87"></a>
#### A87. Supply an immediate answer

**Adverse route:** A premature answer anchors interpretation before the question is understood.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The immediate answer resolves the actual question at the warranted scope.

<a id="a87a"></a>
**A87a. Supply a settled fact**

**Use when:** The question has a verifiable bounded answer.

1. Identify the exact fact requested
2. Verify it when needed
3. State it directly
4. Include only qualifications that change its use.

**Redirect:** If the fact is unavailable, state the gap and obtain it where feasible.

<a id="a87b"></a>
**A87b. Give the supported verdict**

**Use when:** Existing information is enough to choose or assess.

1. Apply the stated criterion
2. Compare the live alternatives
3. Give the conclusion first
4. Explain the decisive reason and material limitation.

**Redirect:** If the criterion is missing but consequential, resolve it before claiming a best choice.

<a id="a87c"></a>
**A87c. Answer conditionally**

**Use when:** Several unresolved conditions imply different answers.

1. Identify the smallest consequential branching condition
2. Give the answer under each plausible branch
3. State which branch current evidence supports
4. Obtain missing information only if needed to act.

**Redirect:** If one action works across branches, recommend that action directly.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a88"></a>
#### A88. Use humor

**Adverse route:** The attempt trivializes a consequential concern.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Humor supports a correct distinction, easier engagement, or accurate recall.

<a id="a88a"></a>
**A88a. Expose an incongruity**

**Use when:** A harmless contradiction can make a logical error visible.

1. Identify the exact inconsistency
2. Construct a brief analogous absurdity
3. Connect the humor back to the actual inference
4. Supply the corrected reasoning.

**Redirect:** If the joke changes the issue or humiliates the recipient, omit it.

<a id="a88b"></a>
**A88b. Release conversational tension**

**Use when:** Tension interferes with a task and the context permits humor.

1. Choose a low-stakes feature of the situation
2. Use brief nonpersonal humor
3. Return immediately to the substantive issue
4. Judge usefulness by continued engagement, not laughter alone.

**Redirect:** If the topic or response indicates distress, use direct considerate language.

<a id="a88c"></a>
**A88c. Attach an accurate mnemonic**

**Use when:** A rule needs a memorable retrieval cue.

1. Identify the rule's essential relation
2. Build a short humorous image around it
3. Keep the exception visible
4. Use the cue to retrieve and apply the rule.

**Redirect:** If the joke outlives the accurate rule, replace it.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a89"></a>
#### A89. Tell a story

**Adverse route:** Narrative coherence substitutes for causal or evidential support.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The story conveys a warranted causal or decision structure that can be used beyond the story.

<a id="a89a"></a>
**A89a. Trace a mechanism through events**

**Use when:** An abstract process is hard to follow.

1. Choose a representative documented or clearly fictional case
2. Show each consequential event
3. Connect actions to outcomes without unsupported causation
4. Extract the governing relation.

**Redirect:** If the case is exceptional, label what it can and cannot establish.

<a id="a89b"></a>
**A89b. Make a decision visible**

**Use when:** The audience needs to understand a choice under constraints.

1. Show what the actor knew at the time
2. Present the feasible alternatives
3. Explain the trade-off that governed the choice
4. Compare the result with the quality of the decision process.

**Redirect:** If the story relies on hindsight, revise it to preserve the actual uncertainty.

<a id="a89c"></a>
**A89c. Transfer the story's structure**

**Use when:** A memorable case risks remaining an isolated anecdote.

1. Identify its functional roles
2. Compare them with a second case
3. Name the shared relation and differing conditions
4. Apply only the transferable relation to the present problem.

**Redirect:** If the mapping fails, keep the story as illustration rather than evidence.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a90"></a>
#### A90. End with a next step

**Adverse route:** Generic invitations keep the interaction going without improving it.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The ending completes the work or specifies an action that advances an unresolved dependency.

<a id="a90a"></a>
**A90a. Implement the supported decision**

**Use when:** The choice is settled and execution is authorized.

1. Identify the first remaining operation
2. Supply its inputs
3. Carry out the authorized work
4. Report the concrete result and any material limit.

**Redirect:** If execution is blocked, identify the actual blocker rather than offering generic encouragement.

<a id="a90b"></a>
**A90b. Resolve the missing input**

**Use when:** One unavailable fact or resource blocks completion.

1. Name that input precisely
2. Identify who or what can supply it
3. Obtain it within authorization where possible
4. State the conditional action that follows.

**Redirect:** If the user alone can supply it, ask one concise question.

<a id="a90c"></a>
**A90c. Stop at completion**

**Use when:** The requested result is already delivered.

1. Compare the result with the request
2. Confirm material requirements are met
3. Present the result accessibly
4. End without manufacturing another task.

**Redirect:** If a real requirement remains, complete it or state the precise limitation.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

### Optimization and evaluation · A91–A100

<a id="a91"></a>
#### A91. Optimize approval

**Adverse route:** Agreement and flattery are rewarded despite worse understanding.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Approval informs delivery preferences without replacing accuracy or the person's endorsed aims.

<a id="a91a"></a>
**A91a. Separate liking from correctness**

**Use when:** Positive reaction may conceal factual or practical defects.

1. Record what was liked
2. Inspect the substantive claim independently
3. Retain accurate useful elements
4. Repair errors even if they were well received.

**Redirect:** If approval is the actual legitimate outcome, still identify any material deception or cost.

<a id="a91b"></a>
**A91b. Optimize for endorsed aims**

**Use when:** Immediate gratification conflicts with a stated longer-term goal.

1. Identify the goal already endorsed
2. Compare responses by their effect on that goal
3. Choose the useful response
4. Make the immediate trade-off explicit where relevant.

**Redirect:** If the longer-term goal is assumed, do not impose it.

<a id="a91c"></a>
**A91c. Use honest disagreement**

**Use when:** The requested conclusion is not supported.

1. State the supported conclusion plainly
2. Identify the decisive evidence or inference
3. Preserve the valid part of the user's concern
4. Provide a constructive consequence.

**Redirect:** If the evidence changes, update rather than defending disagreement for its own sake.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a92"></a>
#### A92. Optimize novelty

**Adverse route:** Unfamiliarity is pursued even when the new idea is irrelevant or false.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Novelty adds a useful capability, warranted inference, or available option.

<a id="a92a"></a>
**A92a. Derive a new consequence**

**Use when:** The ingredients are familiar but their implication is unused.

1. Select accepted premises
2. Combine them in a valid relevant way
3. Derive the previously unused consequence
4. Show what the person can now decide or do differently.

**Redirect:** If the consequence is already known, move to a remaining dependency.

<a id="a92b"></a>
**A92b. Supply a new operation**

**Use when:** Existing knowledge cannot currently be put to use.

1. Identify the missing transformation
2. Teach or perform that operation
3. Connect it to the existing knowledge
4. Produce the useful output.

**Redirect:** If the operation changes no relevant capability, omit it.

<a id="a92c"></a>
**A92c. Remove an unnecessary problem**

**Use when:** Effort is aimed at a task built on a false necessity.

1. Identify the assumed requirement
2. Check whether the valued outcome depends on it
3. Show a route that avoids it
4. Retire the unnecessary work where justified.

**Redirect:** If the requirement is real, do not call dismissal an insight.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a93"></a>
#### A93. Optimize speed

**Adverse route:** Premature closure creates a fast but wrong interpretation.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Total effort to a sufficiently good result decreases without losing a material requirement.

<a id="a93a"></a>
**A93a. Prune unnecessary dependencies**

**Use when:** The workflow contains steps unrelated to the required result.

1. Define the sufficient result
2. Trace which steps actually support it
3. Remove redundant or irrelevant steps
4. Complete the remaining dependency chain.

**Redirect:** If removing a step changes validity or usability, restore its necessary function.

<a id="a93b"></a>
**A93b. Use an easier representation**

**Use when:** Much effort comes from transforming the information mentally.

1. Identify the difficult operation
2. Encode the same information in a form that performs part of it externally
3. Preserve denominators, relations, and limits
4. Complete the remaining comparison.

**Redirect:** If the representation loses information that changes the answer, revise it.

<a id="a93c"></a>
**A93c. Choose the cheapest decisive operation**

**Use when:** Several investigations could resolve the issue.

1. Identify which outcomes would change the conclusion
2. Compare available operations by cost and discrimination
3. Perform the cheapest adequate one
4. Stop once the consequential uncertainty is resolved.

**Redirect:** If no operation is decisive, report the supported partial result rather than overclaiming speed.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a94"></a>
#### A94. Measure agreement

**Adverse route:** Social compliance is recorded as genuine understanding.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Agreement is interpreted at its actual scope and supplemented only by observations needed for the goal.

<a id="a94a"></a>
**A94a. Observe transfer**

**Use when:** The claimed change concerns understanding a rule.

1. Identify the agreed rule
2. Present or observe a fresh relevant case
3. Inspect rule selection and application
4. Infer understanding only to that demonstrated scope.

**Redirect:** If observation is unavailable, record expressed agreement without claiming transfer.

<a id="a94b"></a>
**A94b. Observe a decision consequence**

**Use when:** The claim should alter a real choice.

1. Specify the choice affected
2. Identify how the accepted claim should change it
3. Observe the person's actual reasoning or action where available
4. Distinguish constraints from failure to update.

**Redirect:** If the claim changes no decision, assess the relevant nondecision target instead.

<a id="a94c"></a>
**A94c. Separate assent from confidence**

**Use when:** Politeness or ambiguity may make agreement hard to interpret.

1. Clarify the exact proposition only if needed
2. Allow disagreement without penalty
3. Obtain an unleading confidence judgment when useful
4. Preserve unresolved uncertainty in the record.

**Redirect:** If measurement would burden a simple exchange, leave the effect unverified.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a95"></a>
#### A95. Reuse a previously successful method

**Adverse route:** Success in one starting state is treated as evidence for every later state.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** A previously useful method is reused only where its causal prerequisites still apply.

<a id="a95a"></a>
**A95a. Match the causal conditions**

**Use when:** A past success suggests a reusable intervention.

1. Identify what changed previously
2. Reconstruct the likely enabling conditions
3. Compare them with the present case
4. Reuse the relevant operations where those conditions hold.

**Redirect:** If the original cause is unknown, treat reuse as provisional rather than established.

<a id="a95b"></a>
**A95b. Detect changed circumstances**

**Use when:** A once-effective routine is losing value.

1. Identify the original validity conditions
2. Inspect changes in task, resources, or recipient state
3. Locate the condition that no longer holds
4. Revise or replace the affected branch.

**Redirect:** If conditions are stable, inspect execution or measurement before redesigning.

<a id="a95c"></a>
**A95c. Recombine components**

**Use when:** Only part of a previous method fits.

1. Separate its operations by function
2. Retain the operations matching current needs
3. Replace incompatible operations
4. Inspect the combined sequence for missing dependencies.

**Redirect:** If interactions make the combination invalid, use a coherent alternative.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a96"></a>
#### A96. Personalize from feedback

**Adverse route:** A mistaken model of the person becomes more entrenched.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Feedback improves the user model without turning one response into a permanent personality claim.

<a id="a96a"></a>
**A96a. Update a demonstrated preference**

**Use when:** Feedback identifies a specific useful or harmful feature.

1. Extract the feature named
2. Connect it to the task condition
3. Change the relevant response behavior
4. Observe subsequent task results without demanding praise.

**Redirect:** If the feedback is broad, use the narrowest interpretation supported by context.

<a id="a96b"></a>
**A96b. Retain competing explanations**

**Use when:** One reaction supports several possible diagnoses.

1. List the consequential explanations
2. Compare each with visible evidence
3. Choose a response useful across the plausible explanations
4. Ask only if their difference blocks progress.

**Redirect:** If the response fails again, update the diagnosis rather than intensifying the same tactic.

<a id="a96c"></a>
**A96c. Retire a stale assumption**

**Use when:** Past personalization conflicts with current instructions or evidence.

1. Identify the conflicting assumption
2. Give current explicit instructions priority
3. Remove the obsolete constraint
4. Adapt the present work accordingly.

**Redirect:** If the conflict is unresolved, keep the adaptation conditional.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a97"></a>
#### A97. Summarize for later reuse

**Adverse route:** Compression strips out the boundary that made the claim correct.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** The summary preserves what a future user needs to infer or act correctly.

<a id="a97a"></a>
**A97a. Preserve decision structure**

**Use when:** A later reader must reuse a conclusion.

1. State the conclusion
2. Include its controlling reasons
3. Preserve conditions that would reverse it
4. Provide the action implied under those conditions.

**Redirect:** If compression removes a reversal condition, expand the summary.

<a id="a97b"></a>
**A97b. Preserve scope and uncertainty**

**Use when:** A qualified result may become an overgeneralized slogan.

1. Identify its population, task, and boundary
2. Attach the material uncertainty to the claim
3. Include a representative use condition
4. Link supporting detail where useful.

**Redirect:** If the short form misleads when quoted alone, rewrite the short form.

<a id="a97c"></a>
**A97c. Separate core from recoverable detail**

**Use when:** The original material is too large for frequent reuse.

1. Identify the recurring use task
2. Keep information needed for that task in the core
3. Place supporting detail behind explicit references
4. Verify that the core still supports the intended operation.

**Redirect:** If different tasks need different cores, create purpose-specific summaries.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a98"></a>
#### A98. Evaluate each sentence in isolation

**Adverse route:** Prerequisites are removed because their contribution occurs through later material.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Evaluation captures how statements jointly produce a useful result.

<a id="a98a"></a>
**A98a. Evaluate a dependency block**

**Use when:** Individual sentences supply prerequisites for one inference.

1. Identify the conclusion the block produces
2. Map each sentence's contribution
3. Remove only sentences whose function is redundant
4. Judge the resulting inference as a whole.

**Redirect:** If a sentence has no useful function, delete it.

<a id="a98b"></a>
**A98b. Inspect the combined effect**

**Use when:** Individually accurate statements may jointly imply something false.

1. Read the sequence as a recipient would
2. Identify its likely implication
3. Compare that implication with the evidence
4. Revise order or qualifications to correct it.

**Redirect:** If the implication is uncertain, make the intended relationship explicit.

<a id="a98c"></a>
**A98c. Evaluate the complete representation**

**Use when:** Meaning depends on tables, examples, or visual relationships.

1. Identify the operation the whole representation supports
2. Inspect labels and relations together
3. Check the conclusion a reader can derive
4. Repair any missing or misleading connection.

**Redirect:** If the representation cannot support the operation, change its form.

**Evidence status:** Design proposal.

<a id="a99"></a>
#### A99. Repeat human–AI feedback

**Adverse route:** Small errors are amplified and then reintroduced as apparently independent support.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Iteration improves externally anchored accuracy or usefulness without laundering repeated model output into evidence.

<a id="a99a"></a>
**A99a. Anchor revisions externally**

**Use when:** AI and user judgments repeatedly influence one another.

1. Identify the claim or output being revised
2. Choose an independent observation or specification
3. Compare each revision with that anchor
4. Retain changes supported by the comparison.

**Redirect:** If no independent anchor exists, mark the result as a hypothesis.

<a id="a99b"></a>
**A99b. Trace genuinely new information**

**Use when:** Repeated agreement is increasing confidence.

1. List the evidence added on each round
2. Trace its origins
3. Merge repetitions of the same source
4. Update confidence only for new relevant information or valid inference.

**Redirect:** If nothing new was added, do not count another round as corroboration.

<a id="a99c"></a>
**A99c. Preserve a recoverable revision history**

**Use when:** Iterations may drift away from the original objective.

1. Record the accepted objective and prior supported result
2. Identify each material revision and its reason
3. Inspect downstream consequences
4. Restore the prior result if the revision fails its criterion.

**Redirect:** If the objective itself changes, record that change separately from factual improvement.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S6](#s6).

<a id="a100"></a>
#### A100. Ask whether learning felt effective

**Adverse route:** Fluency and ease are mistaken for durable learning.

**Intended effect, evaluated separately:** Subjective learning experience is distinguished from the capability the activity was meant to produce.

<a id="a100a"></a>
**A100a. Separate experience from performance**

**Use when:** An activity feels easy, difficult, or satisfying.

1. Record the experience as experience
2. Specify the intended capability
3. Inspect a relevant performance when available
4. Judge usability and learning on their separate evidence.

**Redirect:** If performance is unobserved, do not infer it from the feeling.

<a id="a100b"></a>
**A100b. Compare predicted and retained knowledge**

**Use when:** Immediate fluency may mislead judgments of future recall.

1. Estimate what will remain available after a meaningful delay
2. Revisit without the answer in view
3. Compare recall with the estimate
4. Revise the preferred study method accordingly.

**Redirect:** If the task only requires immediate assisted use, evaluate that actual goal instead.

<a id="a100c"></a>
**A100c. Check unfamiliar application**

**Use when:** The intended gain is transferable understanding.

1. Choose a new case requiring the same principle
2. Remove irrelevant hints from the original example
3. Perform the relevant operation
4. Compare the result with the intended standard.

**Redirect:** If only familiar cases succeed, target the missing recognition or adaptation skill.

**Evidence status:** Proposed composition; ingredient or boundary evidence: [S1](#s1), [S2](#s2), [S13](#s13).

## Sixteen ways gains can compound

These are proposed compositions of the recipes, not established large-effect interventions. The amplification claim requires the downstream connection to exist. A demonstrated harmful feedback loop does not establish an equally strong beneficial reverse loop.

| 15. Reach a time-limited opportunity | Verify the real expiration through A57a; use A56a to locate the necessary sequence; complete the remaining feasible prerequisites in time. | A worthwhile option is actually reached before it disappears, with costs and eligibility intact. |
| 16. Combine complementary changes | Use A30c to identify all jointly necessary missing conditions; choose one fitting recipe per condition; order them by dependency and reassess after each material change. | The complete combination enables the intended result where partial repairs did not; adding more interventions is not inherently better. |

<a id="validation"></a>
## How these designs become reliable recipes

The strongest path toward the requested assurance is **narrower claims with stronger demonstrated reliability, followed by controlled expansion of scope**. A universal-sounding recipe with no defined failure boundary is less informative than a procedure whose eligible conditions and actual outcomes are known.

1. **Specify the valuable effect before selecting the operation.** State a minimum worthwhile change, its relevant time horizon, and any material costs or secondary effects. Do not define success as following the procedure, agreeing with the author, or deciding afterward that something good happened.
2. **Specify eligibility independently of the outcome.** Use conditions observable before the attempt where possible. A recipe cannot be rescued by retrospectively labeling every failure “not the right person.” Record missing prerequisites and the cost of determining eligibility.
3. **Make the recipe reproducible.** Fill the actual inputs, write the operations and branches, state what correct execution looks like, and specify which decisions the executor may adapt. A recipe still depending on unexplained expert improvisation has an unresolved instruction gap.
4. **Evaluate the intended effect against an appropriate comparison.** Random assignment is useful where feasible; a before-and-after result alone can reflect time, selection, or other changes. A single conversation can establish a local output or observation but rarely identifies a general causal success rate.
5. **Separate execution defects from recipe defects.** Record both. For an execution defect, repair clarity, tools, or prerequisites. For correct execution without the effect, revise the proposed mechanism, route, or scope. Neither kind of failure should disappear from the overall record.
6. **Evaluate the selector as well as the selected recipe.** Report outcomes across all attempted uses and among prospectively eligible, correctly executed uses. Report abstentions, route changes, and costs separately. High success in a tiny selected subset does not establish a broadly useful process.
7. **Check the horizon and support level actually promised.** Immediate assistance, retained knowledge, repeated behavior, transfer, and independent competence need their respective observations. Do not demand all of them when the goal only requires one.
8. **Validate on new cases before making a reliability claim.** Define what “near guaranteed” means numerically for that use, then estimate the success rate and its uncertainty in comparable cases not used to tune the recipe. A favorable point estimate from a small or selected sample is insufficient.
9. **Expand only across understood differences.** A result for one task, audience, or setting licenses a hypothesis elsewhere. Identify which causal conditions remain intact; evaluate changed conditions rather than copying the success label.
10. **Retain revisions and negative results.** Preserve the original target and standard alongside the new version. A changed goal can be worthwhile, but it cannot retroactively make the earlier attempt successful.

The value of comparing complete interventions is illustrated by a megastudy that tested 54 exercise programs in the same broad setting: effects varied across designs. It supports evaluating actual procedures under common conditions rather than ranking appealing psychological labels. [Milkman et al. (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8822539/)

| Field in a recipe's scientific record | Example of what belongs there |
|---|---|
| Identity | Recipe ID, version, exact operations and permitted adaptations. |
| Destination | Defined target change, worthwhile threshold, owner of the value judgment, horizon. |
| Conditions | Population or user state, prior knowledge, motivation, resources, setting, eligibility rule. |
| Mechanism | Which missing transition each operation is supposed to supply; plausible rival explanation. |
| Evidence | Source, study design, comparator, sample, effect and uncertainty where available, relevant limits. |
| Execution | Inputs supplied, steps performed, branch taken, observed deviations. |
| Outcomes | Primary effect, important secondary effects, burden, support level, retention or transfer if promised. |
| Status | Not applicable, execution incomplete, executed but unverified, ineffective, adverse, locally effective, or validated within stated scope. |
| Revision | What changed, why, and what previous successes and failures remain on record. |

These status labels prevent a circular success definition. An abstention may be the right decision; an ineffective attempt may be well executed; an adverse attempt may teach the designer something. Those are different results and must remain distinguishable.

## A reusable instruction for executing the catalog

> Identify the worthwhile change the person needs from this interaction. Use visible context to locate the missing transition between the current state and that result. Select a recipe whose prerequisites fit; fill its references with the actual claim, evidence, contrast, action, cue, and constraints. Explain or supply each necessary operation rather than substituting a desirable adjective for it. If a prerequisite is missing, follow a specific repair or alternative branch. If the destination is not worth reaching, revise or retire it. Perform the substantive authorized work. Treat correct execution, the intended effect, and later retention or transfer as separate claims. Use an existing natural observation where sufficient; ask a question or request participation only when it materially advances the operation or resolves a consequential uncertainty. Report the result at the scope supported by evidence. Stop when the useful work is complete.

This instruction can reduce omitted operations and improve route selection. It cannot make false premises true, unavailable resources available, or an unvalidated psychological effect certain. Improving the prompt and improving the recipe are complementary tasks with different failure records.

## Extension to subjectsystems and perspectiveoptimizer

Subjectsystems can store each usable distinction or operation with its prerequisites, consequences, exceptions, and evidence. Perspectiveoptimizer can select and sequence those operations against a stated valuable target, rather than maximizing agreement or novelty in isolation. Store the target separately from the intervention so that an excellent operation can be rejected when its destination is useless.

For AI-assisted work, distinguish a changed statement in the current context, an explicitly saved reusable rule, and a demonstrated capability in a new context. Human intervention studies do not establish changes to an LLM's internal learning or durable behavior. A useful artifact can persist as an artifact without implying that either the user or model has acquired the skill it contains.

<a id="evidence"></a>
## Evidence register and limits

This is a targeted research base for the catalog, not an exhaustive systematic review or a certification that the best available intervention has been found for every target. Source links on a card identify ingredients or boundary evidence; they do not validate all its branches or establish the proposed mechanism. Where no study is linked, the route is explicitly a design proposal. Logical examples and new procedure designs are distinguished from empirical findings throughout.

<a id="s1"></a>
**S1 — [Roediger & Karpicke (2006)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16507066/).** In two experiments with prose passages, repeated study outperformed prior testing at five minutes, but prior testing produced greater retention at two days or one week. Restudying also increased confidence. Supports a divergence between immediate performance, confidence, and delayed recall in these learning tasks; does not establish that difficulty always helps.

<a id="s2"></a>
**S2 — [Deslauriers et al. (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31484770/).** Randomly assigned college physics students learned more with active instruction while reporting a lower feeling of learning than students receiving passive instruction with the same content. A result about these instructional conditions; self-reported benefit remains useful evidence about experience and cannot alone establish learning.

<a id="s3"></a>
**S3 — [Schultz et al. (2007)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/6261413_The_Constructive_Destructive_and_Reconstructive_Power_of_Social_Norms).** Household energy feedback about average neighborhood consumption produced savings among higher consumers and an undesirable increase among lower consumers. Adding a message of social approval or disapproval eliminated the boomerang effect in the experiment. Direct evidence that the sign of a norm message can depend on the recipient's starting behavior; the result does not make all normative messages beneficial.

<a id="s4"></a>
**S4 — [Wood & Porter (2019)](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11109-018-9443-y).** Across five experiments involving more than 10,100 participants and 52 issues, the researchers found no factual corrections that produced the tested backfire effect. Corrective information does not routinely strengthen the corrected false belief. This result does not rule out relational, motivational, or other adverse consequences.

<a id="s5"></a>
**S5 — [Eskreis-Winkler et al. (2019)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31285348/).** In a preregistered randomized field experiment involving 1,982 high school students, giving motivational advice to younger students improved advisers' grades in math and a self-selected target class across an academic quarter. Supports an effect of the advice-giving intervention in this setting. The abstract does not establish one uniquely responsible psychological mechanism or a large universal effect.

<a id="s6"></a>
**S6 — [Glickman & Sharot (2025)](https://cris.biu.ac.il/en/publications/how-humanai-feedback-loops-alter-human-perceptual-emotional-and-s/).** Across experiments with 1,401 participants, human–AI feedback loops amplified human biases in perceptual, emotional, and social judgments. Participants often underestimated the influence. Supports harmful feedback effects in the studied tasks. It does not establish that every chatbot interaction has these effects or that beneficial amplification is symmetrical.

<a id="s7"></a>
**S7 — [Fernbach et al. (2013)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23620547/).** Generating detailed mechanistic policy explanations reduced an illusion of understanding and moderated attitudes in the experiments; enumerating reasons for preferences did not have the same effect. Supports a difference between two elicited operations in these tasks. Moderation is not itself a general measure of truth, and this is not a universal prescription to moderate beliefs.

<a id="s8"></a>
**S8 — [Yeager et al. (2019)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1466-y).** A brief growth-mindset intervention improved grades among lower-achieving students and increased overall advanced-mathematics enrollment in a nationally representative US high-school sample; effects varied with context. Supports context-sensitive motivational intervention effects, not guaranteed or uniformly large benefits from generic encouragement.

<a id="s9"></a>
**S9 — [Hulleman & Harackiewicz (2009)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19965759/).** A randomized field experiment asked students to connect science coursework with their lives. Interest and course grades increased for students with low expectations of success. Supports learner-generated relevance in the specified subgroup and context. It does not imply that imposing a supposedly relevant motive will work.

<a id="s10"></a>
**S10 — [Fazio et al. (2015)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/26301795/).** The experiments investigated repetition-based increases in perceived truth, including cases where participants had relevant knowledge. Supports concern about familiarity being used as evidence of truth. It does not establish that repeating a false claim within a clear correction routinely backfires.

<a id="s11"></a>
**S11 — [Mellers et al. (2014)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24659192/).** In a geopolitical forecasting tournament, probability training, team collaboration, and tracking top performers were associated with improved calibration and resolution. Training included reference classes and combining estimates. Tracking selected high performers, so it must not be interpreted as a purely randomized training effect. Supports concrete forecasting ingredients in this setting, not guaranteed individual calibration.

<a id="s12"></a>
**S12 — [Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson (2003)](https://groups.psych.northwestern.edu/gentner/papers/GentnerLoewensteinThompson03.pdf).** Three studies of novice negotiation learning found benefits from comparing cases for extracting and transferring common structure; support for comparison improved transfer. Supports explicit relational comparison in those tasks. It does not establish that every analogy, example pair, or newly assembled transfer sequence works.

<a id="s13"></a>
**S13 — [Kornell & Bjork (2008)](https://web.williams.edu/Psychology/Faculty/Kornell/Publications/Kornell.Bjork.2008a.pdf).** Participants learned artists' styles from paintings presented in blocks or interleaved with other artists. Interleaving improved classification of new paintings, although participants judged blocked presentation more effective. Supports discrimination and subjective-performance divergence in this category-learning task. It is not evidence that all kinds of contextual variation or difficulty improve every skill.

<a id="s14"></a>
**S14 — [van der Bles et al. (2020)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32205438/).** Experiments on communicating uncertainty about facts and numbers found increased perceived uncertainty with only small decreases in trust, mostly for verbal uncertainty communication. Supports specifying uncertainty without assuming catastrophic loss of trust. It does not promise preserved trust for every formulation, source, audience, or topic.

<a id="s15"></a>
**S15 — [Carrera et al. (2018)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30336306/).** In a randomized study with 877 gym members, selecting intended attendance days and times did not increase attendance relative to recording prior exercise. The authors reported a tightly estimated null despite participant engagement with planning. Direct evidence that performing a plausible planning procedure can fail to change repeated behavior; the catalog's proposed diagnostic branches require their own validation.

<a id="s16"></a>
**S16 — [Lally et al. (2010)](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/ejsp.674).** Volunteers chose a behavior to repeat daily in the same context over 12 weeks and reported behavior and automaticity. The study modeled habit development from these longitudinal observations. It supports investigating context, repetition, and changing automaticity, but does not supply a universal completion date or randomized proof of the catalog's habit-building recipes.

<a id="s17"></a>
**S17 — [Gross (1998)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9457784/).** In a laboratory study using a disgusting film, reappraisal and expressive suppression both reduced expressive behavior, but differed in reported disgust and physiological response. Supports distinguishing appraisal, feeling, expression, and physiology. It does not establish that reframing improves every emotion, that suppression is always harmful, or that ordinary conversational recipes treat clinical problems.

<a id="s18"></a>
**S18 — [Walton et al. (2023)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/37141344/).** A randomized study with 26,911 students at 22 institutions evaluated a brief social-belonging intervention. Benefits for first-year full-time progress depended on student group and college context; effects occurred where groups had opportunities to belong. Supports treating actual opportunity as a consequential condition, not assuming a message can compensate for every exclusionary environment.

<a id="s19"></a>
**S19 — [Hoffrage et al. (2000)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11188724/).** Natural-frequency representations improved reasoning about statistical information in the studied tasks. The relevant representation preserves joint counts and the appropriate denominator; merely replacing every percentage with an isolated count is not the same operation. Supports the representational ingredient of the probability example, not a universal advantage for every table or numerical format.

<a id="s20"></a>
**S20 — [Renkl, Atkinson & Große (2004)](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/how-fading-worked-solution-steps-works-a-cognitive-load-perspecti/).** Two experiments investigated fading worked solution steps in a computer-based learning environment. Fading supported learning, with fewer unproductive learning events; the proposed increase in productive self-explanation events was not found. Supports a specified instructional ingredient while illustrating why a plausible mediator must not be presented as established.

<a id="s21"></a>
**S21 — [Bastani et al. (2025)](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2422633122).** A high-school mathematics field experiment compared ordinary GPT assistance with a tutor design containing safeguards. Unguarded assistance improved practice performance but harmed later unassisted performance; the tutor design largely mitigated that harm. Supports separating assisted output from independent learning and attending to tool design. It does not show that all AI assistance harms learning or that this catalog's scaffolds are validated. An author-hosted version is available at [the authors’ manuscript](https://hamsabastani.github.io/education_llm.pdf).

<a id="s22"></a>
**S22 — [Milkman et al. (2021)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8822539/).** A megastudy tested 54 four-week digital programs among 61,293 fitness-chain members; effects differed across programs, with 45% significantly increasing weekly visits during the intervention. This motivates comparative evaluation of complete procedures in a common setting. Statistical significance is not a per-person success probability, and a selected top performer is not automatically best in a new setting.

<a id="s23"></a>
**S23 — [Milkman et al. (2011)](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21670283/).** A field experiment on a one-time vaccination opportunity found a 4.2 percentage-point increase from prompting people to write both a date and a time. A date-only prompt had a smaller, nonsignificant difference. Supports a specific planning intervention with an available opportunity; it neither guarantees action nor transfers automatically to recurring behavior.
